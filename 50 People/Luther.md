# Luther

Reformer; championed *Sola Scriptura*.

## Signature
"Scripture Alone" over Tradition + Magisterium; reaction against speculative theology and corruption.

## Connected
[[Scripture]] · [[Calvin]]
Fuller (criteria): Ch 5.


## Sacraments (BTh course)
Reduced the sacraments to two in The Babylonian Captivity (1520), rejecting Confirmation as lacking dominical institution (he called such rites Affenspiel, "monkey business") while approving a retained catechetical rite. His baptismal theology is arguably the richest of the sixteenth century: baptism is God's promise attached to a sign, received rather than created by faith; the Large Catechism teaches that "the old Adam in us should, by daily sorrow and repentance, be drowned." His pastoral counsel in temptation was simply "Baptizatus sum" — I am baptised.

See: [[Sacraments MOC]]


## Eucharist (BTh course)
Held the real presence firmly — Christ's body and blood "in, with, and under" bread and wine that remain bread and wine, supported by the ubiquity of Christ's glorified body — while rejecting transubstantiation as an unnecessary Aristotelian imposition. At Marburg (1529) he chalked HOC EST CORPUS MEUM on the table and refused to move. He judged the sacrifice of the Mass the graver error, calling it an abomination on the grounds of Hebrews' "once for all." Note the irony: closer to Rome than Zwingli on presence, further from Rome than Zwingli on sacrifice.

See: [[Eucharist MOC]]


## Penance (BTh course)
The Ninety-Five Theses (1517) protest the abuse of indulgences rather than rejecting the sacrament outright, but Thesis 1 — that Christ "willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance" — is the real hinge, resting on the claim that the Vulgate's poenitentiam agite narrowed the Greek metanoeite from lifelong conversion into a ritual act. He later withdrew Penance from the list of sacraments for lacking a divinely instituted visible sign, while retaining private confession and absolution enthusiastically as a consoling application of the gospel to the individual.

See: [[Penance MOC]]


## Anointing of the Sick (BTh course)
Rejected Extreme Unction in The Babylonian Captivity (1520) on three grounds: James is not of apostolic authority, there is no dominical institution, and the promise that "the Lord will raise him up" manifestly fails since the anointed die. His sharpest point was the mismatch between the promise and the practice of administering it precisely to those not expected to recover — though he added that he would not object to prayer and anointing as James describes, provided it were not called a sacrament.

See: [[Anointing MOC]]


## Holy Orders (BTh course)
To the Christian Nobility (1520) makes the foundational Reformation move: all the baptised are truly priests, and clergy differ from laity in office (Amt) rather than in status. Ordination is therefore the public calling of a man to exercise on the community's behalf an office belonging to the whole priestly people; there is no character and no ontological change, and a minister removed from office returns to being a layman. The sacrificing priesthood is rejected on Hebrews' ephapax — though Luther holds the ministry of Word and sacrament very high and insists no one may exercise it without a proper call.

See: [[Holy Orders MOC]]


## Matrimony (BTh course)
Called marriage "a worldly thing" (ein weltlich Ding) and denied its sacramentality, since it lacks a divine promise attached to a sign and existed before Christ among all peoples — while holding it in the highest esteem as a divine estate and order of creation. He assigned marriage jurisdiction to the civil authority and permitted divorce with remarriage for adultery and desertion, appealing to the Matthean exception and 1 Cor 7:15.
