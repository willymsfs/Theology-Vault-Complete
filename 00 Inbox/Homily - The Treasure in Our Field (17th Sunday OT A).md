# The Treasure in Our Field
### A Homily for the Seventeenth Sunday in Ordinary Time (Year A) — 26 July 2026
*1 Kings 3:5, 7–12 · Romans 8:28–30 · Matthew 13:44–52*

*In the method and voice of George M. Soares-Prabhu, SJ — grounded in the Collected Writings, Vols 1–4.*

---

My dear sisters and brothers,

A daily-wage labourer is digging in a field that is not his own. His back is bent — bent the way the backs of the landless poor of our country are bent, over soil they till but will never possess. And suddenly his spade strikes something hard. Treasure! Hidden, unhoped for, unearned. And "in his joy," the Gospel tells us, "he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field" (Mt 13:44).

Notice whom Jesus chooses for his story. Not a king, not a priest, not a landowner — a man digging in another man's field. If Jesus were telling this parable among us today, he would tell it, I suspect, of a migrant worker on a construction site, of a dalit farmhand in a field mortgaged to a moneylender. For the parables of Jesus are not pious decorations. They are subversive stories, told to shatter the world we have comfortably settled into, to turn our expectations upside down until our defences fall and the Kingdom can break in. Like the *dhvani* of our Indian poetics — the resonance by which a word evokes in the hearer depths of meaning it does not state — the parable does not define the Kingdom. It detonates it in the heart.

## What did Jesus mean?

Sober exegesis will not let us sentimentalize this text. The Kingdom of God that Jesus announced (Mk 1:14–15) is not heaven-after-death, and it is certainly not the Church. It is God's decisive saving action, liberating creation — a new society realized whenever human beings become free, internally and externally; whenever there is fellowship of concern among them; whenever they strive together for a just world. Freedom, fellowship, justice: these are the parameters by which the arrival — or the delay! — of God's reign is to be gauged. The twin parables of the treasure and the pearl tell us two things about this Kingdom: that it is found with joy, and that it costs everything. The merchant and the labourer do not give up their possessions grimly, as ascetics performing tapasya. They give them up laughing, because what they have found makes everything else look small.

Here is the heart of the matter. The Kingdom is not first a demand; it is first a discovery. Beneath the demand to "sell all" lies the experience Jesus himself had of God — the experience of God as *Abba*, a loving parent whose love is unconditional and therefore absolutely universal. The dharma of Jesus is not a burden added to our burdens. It proclaims freedom from the burden of the law, because it is grounded on the experience of a God who loves us, his children, before we have earned anything at all. The treasure is hidden in the field of our ordinary, sweat-soaked lives; grace comes to us where we dig.

## And Solomon?

The first reading shows us the young king, invited to ask for anything — long life, riches, the death of his enemies — and asking instead for "an understanding heart to govern your people, to distinguish right from wrong" (1 Kgs 3:9). Mark it well: the wisdom Solomon asks for is not speculative wisdom, not the wisdom of the philosophers or of the pandits. It is the wisdom to govern justly — a wisdom whose test, in the very next scene of the book, is a judgment that protects a powerless woman, a prostitute, the least of the land. Biblical wisdom is discernment ordered to justice. It is what our own tradition would call *viveka* — but a viveka that has come down from the mountain caves into the marketplace and the courtroom, where the poor wait for judgment.

This is not an elitist gift. Grasping the Kingdom, Jesus insists, does not require intellectual sophistication or spiritual status; it requires an open heart, receiving God's love with the trust and simplicity of a child. The illiterate grandmother who shares her rice with a hungrier neighbour has found the treasure. Many of us, with our degrees and our devotions, are still walking past the field.

## Reading with Indian eyes — and being read

We live in a land where the treasure-seeking of the Gospel has been replaced by another kind: the frenzied acquisitiveness of a consumer culture whose tinsel facade hides a culture of silence — crores of our people below, on, or just above the poverty line. The parable asks the rich among us a piercing question: what have you ever wanted enough to sell everything for? If the honest answer is "a flat, a car, a foreign posting," then the Kingdom has not yet been found, however regular our Mass attendance. Our tribal communities have long known the values the parable presupposes — antigreed, the freedom not to accumulate; antipride, greatness as fearless service. They are closer to the merchant of the pearl than our consumer cities are.

And the parable asks the Church a harder question still. A community that has found the treasure sells all and buys the field. Have we? A Church that clings to its institutions, its properties, its securities and its subtle castes — that still, God forgive us, seats its dalit members apart at the Eucharist — is a merchant who admired the pearl and went home to count his old stock. Where caste divides the one table of the Lord, our Eucharist is turned into a parody of itself. The dragnet parable (Mt 13:47–50) stands as a warning that the Kingdom's judgment falls first on those who presumed they were safely inside the boat.

## What then must we do?

The parable ends in joy, and so must we — but Kingdom-joy is not a feeling; it is a transaction. Something is found; everything is sold; the field is bought. This week, let each of us name one thing — one comfort, one prejudice, one hour, one rupee-amount — and sell it for the field: for the migrant family down the road, for the dalit colleague passed over again, for the neighbour of another faith whom we have politely avoided. For the liberation the Kingdom brings is total: liberation of the poor from the structures that crush them, and liberation of us, the possessing, from the greed that crushes us — the freedom our sages called *moksha*, arriving not at the end of the world but in the middle of a field, at the end of a spade.

The scribe "trained for the Kingdom," Jesus says, brings out of his storeroom "things new and old" (Mt 13:52). Out of the old storeroom of India — her thirst for God, her viveka, her tribal wisdom — and out of the newness of the Gospel, let us bring forth a community of freedom, fellowship and justice. The treasure is already in our field. Faith endures; but no complacency is perennial!

**Amen.**
