# Unit 9 — Theological Significance

**1. Christological — Christ the Physician.** The Fathers' favourite title, *Christus medicus*, is the sacrament's governing image. Christ does not explain suffering; he enters it and touches the sufferer. The anointing extends that touch.

**2. Paschal — configuration to the passion.** *LG* 11 asks the sick to "contribute to the good of the People of God by freely uniting themselves to the passion and death of Christ," and *CCC* 1521 speaks of the sick person receiving "the strength and the gift of uniting himself more closely to Christ's Passion." The theological background is **Colossians 1:24** — "in my flesh I complete what is lacking in Christ's afflictions for the sake of his body, that is, the Church." Nothing is lacking in Christ's redemptive work; what is "lacking" is the members' participation in it.

**This is the sacrament's most distinctive and most difficult claim**: that the sick person is not merely a passive object of the Church's care but an *agent* whose suffering, united to Christ's, is fruitful for the whole body. Handled badly it becomes a cruel spiritualisation of pain that tells sufferers to be grateful. Handled well it is the one thing that answers the sick person's deepest fear — that they have become useless.

**3. Pneumatological.** The form asks explicitly for "the grace of the Holy Spirit." The proper effect is a *gift*, not merely a consolation.

**4. Ecclesial.** *"The whole Church commends those who are ill"* — the sacrament is an act of the entire body, which is why communal celebration (Unit 11) is not a concession but an expression of what the sacrament is.

**5. Eschatological.** Anointing completes the anointings of Christian life. **Baptism** anoints for incorporation, **Confirmation** for mission, and the **Anointing of the Sick** fortifies for the final struggle and passage — "a solid anointing to fortify the end of our earthly life like a strong rampart for the final struggles before entering the Father's house" (*CCC* 1523).

## The Effects, per CCC 1520-1523

1. A particular **gift of the Holy Spirit** — strengthening, peace, courage against discouragement and anguish.
2. **Union with the passion of Christ**, giving the suffering a share in his saving work.
3. An **ecclesial grace** — the sick contribute to the sanctification of the Church.
4. **Forgiveness of sins**, if the person was unable to obtain it through penance.
5. **Restoration of health**, if it is conducive to the salvation of the soul.
6. **Preparation for the final journey**.

Note the ordering: healing appears fifth, not first, and is conditional — but it genuinely appears, which the medieval practice had all but forgotten.
