# Unit 10 — Contemporary Theological Debates

## 1. Transignification and Transfinalization

Dutch and Belgian theologians of the 1960s — notably **Edward Schillebeeckx** and **Piet Schoonenberg** — sought a personalist and phenomenological re-expression of the presence. Their argument: reality for persons is constituted by *meaning* and *purpose*; when Christ gives the bread a radically new meaning (*transignification*) and a radically new end (*transfinalization*), that is a real change in its deepest reality, not a merely subjective one.

**Paul VI's response** in *Mysterium Fidei* (1965) was not a flat condemnation but a demand for order: these categories may **express** something true about the sacrament, but they cannot **replace** the affirmation of an ontological conversion. The Catholic position is that the meaning changes *because* the being changes, not instead of it.

**Assessment for essays.** The debate exposes a genuine difficulty: Aristotelian "substance" is not part of the working conceptual apparatus of most modern people, and simply repeating the term does not communicate. But the personalist alternatives, taken alone, appear to make the presence depend on the community's act of signification — which is exactly what Trent excluded.

## 2. How the Sacrifice Is "the Same"

- **Odo Casel's *Mysteriengegenwart***: the saving *act* of Christ, not merely its fruits, is made present in the liturgical mystery. Enormously influential, though criticised for the obscurity of its metaphysics of "mystery presence."
- **Maurice de la Taille**: the one sacrifice comprises oblation (Supper), immolation (Cross), and acceptance (Resurrection/Ascension); the Mass is the Church's participation in the single oblation.
- **Charles Journet** and later **Joseph Ratzinger**: the Mass is our being drawn into Christ's eternally-abiding self-offering to the Father — the sacrifice is not repeated because it never ceased.
- **The ecumenical resolution** has largely come through recovering **anamnesis** in its Hebraic sense (Unit 2), which allows Protestants to affirm that the Supper makes the one sacrifice present without implying a repetition. **Max Thurian** of Taizé was the pivotal figure here.

## 3. Ecumenical Convergence

| Text | Achievement | Remaining difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| **ARCIC, Windsor Statement** (1971) + Elucidation (1979) | Claimed "substantial agreement" on Eucharistic doctrine; used *anamnesis* to resolve the sacrifice question | Whether "substantial agreement" survives without agreement on ministry and orders |
| **BEM / Lima** (1982) | Eucharist section presents five aspects: thanksgiving to the Father, anamnesis of Christ, invocation of the Spirit, communion of the faithful, meal of the Kingdom | Deliberately leaves the *mode* of presence open |
| **Lutheran-Catholic dialogues** | Broad agreement on real presence | Sacrifice, and the necessity of ordained ministry, remain |

## 4. Postmodern and Philosophical Approaches

- **Louis-Marie Chauvet**, *Symbol and Sacrament* — a substantial critique of the scholastic "productionist" and onto-theological scheme of causality, replacing it with **symbolic exchange**: the sacrament is gift, reception, and return-gift, situated in language and the body rather than in a metaphysics of production.
- **Catherine Pickstock**, *After Writing* (Radical Orthodoxy) — argues, against Derrida, that the medieval Roman Rite is the condition of meaningful language rather than its problem, and reads transubstantiation as the guarantor of signification rather than its embarrassment.
- **Jean-Luc Marion** — the Eucharist as saturated phenomenon and pure gift, resisting conceptual mastery.

**Teaching point.** All three converge on a single instinct: that the modern difficulty with transubstantiation is less about physics than about a philosophy of the sign, and that the sacrament resists being fitted into either a naive realism or a reductive symbolism.
