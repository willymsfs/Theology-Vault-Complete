# Unit 6 — The Scholastic Synthesis

## Lateran IV (1215), Canon 21 — Omnis utriusque sexus

The single most consequential piece of penitential legislation ever enacted. It requires **all the faithful who have reached the age of discretion to confess their sins to their own priest at least once a year**, and to receive the Eucharist at Easter.

It also legislates the **seal** in the strongest terms and with a striking image: the priest is to be "**discreet and cautious**, so that in the manner of a skilled physician he may pour wine and oil on the wounds of the injured"; and the priest who betrays a sin revealed in confession is to be deposed and confined to a monastery for perpetual penance. **The seal is thus as old as the universal obligation itself.**

*(Note: the same canon underlies the Easter communion obligation treated in the Eucharist course.)*

## The Anatomy of the Sacrament

The scholastics identified **four constituent elements** — three acts of the penitent and one of the priest:

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| ***Contritio*** | Sorrow for sin with the purpose of amendment |
| ***Confessio*** | Disclosure of sins to the priest |
| ***Satisfactio*** | The penance performed to repair the disorder caused by sin |
| ***Absolutio*** | The priest's sacramental absolution |

**Aquinas's structural move** (*ST* III q.84 and the Supplement): since the sacrament has no physical element like water or oil, **the three acts of the penitent function as the *quasi-materia***, and the **absolution is the form**. This is the classic solution to the awkwardness of fitting penance into the matter/form scheme.

**The threefold distinction applied:**

| Level | Content |
|---|---|
| *sacramentum tantum* | The acts of the penitent and the absolution |
| *res et sacramentum* | **Interior penance** — and, importantly, **reconciliation with the Church** |
| *res tantum* | The forgiveness of sins and the restoration of grace |

Note that penance, unlike baptism, confirmation, and orders, **imprints no character** — which is precisely why it is repeatable.

## Contrition versus Attrition

The central medieval controversy, and a standard exam question.

- ***Contritio*** — sorrow arising from the **love of God**, from having offended one who is supremely good. **Peter Abelard** and the "contritionists" held that such sorrow *of itself* obtains forgiveness, with confession serving mainly to declare a forgiveness already received. This risks making the sacrament redundant.
- ***Attritio*** — imperfect sorrow, arising from **fear of hell**, the ugliness of sin, or loss of heaven. **Duns Scotus** and the "attritionists" held that attrition, **joined to the sacrament**, suffices — the sacrament supplying what the disposition lacks.

**Aquinas's position** is subtler than either label: attrition disposes toward, and in the sacrament is transformed into, contrition by the grace conferred. Trent will settle the practical question in the attritionists' favour (Unit 9): attrition, which excludes the will to sin and hopes for pardon, is a **sufficient** disposition when joined to sacramental absolution.

**Why this matters pastorally.** The attritionist settlement is what makes the sacrament genuinely available to ordinary penitents. If only perfect contrition sufficed, no one could ever be sure of the sacrament's effect — the very anxiety the Reformers would later charge the system with producing.

## Jurisdiction and Reserved Sins

A distinctive feature of penance among the sacraments: valid absolution requires not only **orders** but also **jurisdiction** (in modern terms, the **faculty** to hear confessions). Certain grave sins were **reserved** to the bishop or the Holy See. The modern residue is in **CIC c. 1357** and the *latae sententiae* excommunications reserved to the Apostolic See — with the crucial exception that **in danger of death any priest can absolve any sin** (c. 976), a rule that expresses the Novatian settlement in canonical form.
