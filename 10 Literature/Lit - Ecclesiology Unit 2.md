# Unit 2 — Did Christ Found the Church? NT Origins

## Four NT Usages of Ekklesia
The New Testament uses ekklesia in at least four distinct senses. (1) Local assembly — a concrete congregation in a city, as in "the church in Corinth" (1 Cor 1:2). (2) The whole Church — the sum of believers across every place, as when Paul says he "persecuted the church of God" (Gal 1:13). (3) The cosmic Church — the Church as Christ's Body filling the universe (Eph 1:22-23). (4) The heavenly Church — the eschatological assembly already gathered in heaven (Heb 12:23, "the assembly of the firstborn enrolled in heaven").

## Loisy's Dictum and Its Critique
Alfred Loisy's famous, skeptical dictum (1902) runs: "Jesus proclaimed the Kingdom, and it was the Church that came." On this reading the Church is a historical accident, a substitute for the Kingdom Jesus actually expected imminently and that never arrived.

Contemporary NT scholarship, represented here by Raymond Brown, resists simple substitution. Jesus's ministry — his calling of the Twelve as a symbolic reconstitution of Israel's twelve tribes, his inclusive table-fellowship, the covenant-founding meal of the Last Supper — contains the seeds from which a post-Easter, Spirit-animated community necessarily grew. The Church is not what Jesus "meant to found" as a ready-made institution with canon law already specified; neither is it an accident. It is the historically-conditioned unfolding of what Jesus himself inaugurated.

## Blueprint Ecclesiology vs the Historico-Critical Approach
"Blueprint ecclesiology" names the older assumption that Jesus left a detailed blueprint — offices, structures, sacraments already specified — which the early Church simply executed. The historical-critical approach instead finds plurality and development: different NT communities organized themselves differently according to their own situations, only gradually converging on common patterns (bishop-presbyter-deacon) by the early second century. The Church is thus best described as founded both Christologically (rooted in Jesus's own mission and paschal mystery) and pneumatologically (constituted as a visible, missionary community at Pentecost, by the Spirit) — a post-Easter reality rooted in Jesus's kingdom preaching.

## Illustration
Like an acorn and an oak: the acorn does not contain a miniature, ready-made oak tree, yet it does determine, by its own inner life-principle, what kind of tree can grow from it. The Church's structures were not dictated by Jesus in advance and in detail, but they had to grow in real continuity with what he planted.
