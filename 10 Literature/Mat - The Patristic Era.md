# Unit 3 — The Patristic Era

## Defending Marriage Against Disparagement

The characteristic patristic task was **not** to exalt marriage over celibacy but to defend marriage as **good at all**, against Gnostic, Marcionite, Manichaean, and Encratite movements that held matter and procreation to be evil or the work of an inferior god.

- **Clement of Alexandria** (*Stromateis* III) writes the most sustained early defence, against Encratites who forbade marriage: procreation cooperates with the Creator, and the married Christian who raises children in the faith may be more perfect than the ascetic who avoids the difficulty.
- **Tertullian**, *Ad Uxorem* II.8, gives the era's most beautiful passage on Christian marriage — a marriage "which the Church arranges, the oblation confirms, the blessing seals, angels proclaim, and the Father holds as ratified... two in one flesh; where the flesh is one, one also is the spirit."
- **Ignatius** advises that those marrying "should be united with the consent of the bishop" — the earliest hint of ecclesial involvement, though not yet a required rite.

## The Jerome–Jovinian Controversy

The sharpest patristic conflict on the subject. **Jovinian** argued that the baptised who persevere are of equal merit, so **virginity is not superior to marriage**. **Jerome's** reply (*Adversus Jovinianum*, 393) was so extreme in its disparagement of marriage that Jerome's own friends were embarrassed and Augustine felt obliged to write a more balanced treatment.

Jerome's position — marriage is good chiefly because it produces virgins — represents the pole the tradition ultimately did *not* adopt while still affirming the superiority of consecrated virginity (which Trent would reaffirm, Unit 6).

## Augustine and the Three Goods

**Augustine's *De bono coniugali* (401)** is the decisive Western text, and its framework governs the next fifteen centuries. The **three goods (*tria bona*)** of marriage:

| Good | Content |
|---|---|
| ***Proles*** (offspring) | Children, to be received and raised in the faith |
| ***Fides*** (fidelity) | Mutual faithfulness, the exclusion of adultery |
| ***Sacramentum*** (the bond) | The **indissoluble bond** itself, signifying Christ's union with the Church — note that for Augustine *sacramentum* here means the permanent, unbreakable bond, not yet "sacrament" in the later technical sense |

**Augustine's ambivalence must be stated honestly.** In *De nuptiis et concupiscentia*, arguing against the Pelagians, he holds that although marriage is genuinely good, sexual intercourse is now inevitably accompanied by **concupiscence**, and it is through generation that original sin is transmitted. The result is a tradition that affirms marriage's goodness while treating its sexual expression with persistent unease — a legacy the twentieth-century personalist turn (Unit 8) set out to correct.

**John Chrysostom** offers a warmer counterweight, particularly in his homilies on Ephesians 5 and his sermon *On Marriage*, urging husbands to tell their wives, "I have taken you in my arms, and I love you, and I prefer you to my life itself" — and grounding marriage in love rather than in procreation alone.

## No Required Rite

For the first several centuries there was **no obligatory ecclesiastical ceremony**. Marriage was contracted according to local civil custom — in the Roman world by **consent** (*consensus facit nuptias*) — and Christians simply married as others did, with the Church's blessing sought but not required for validity. The nuptial blessing (*velatio*) develops gradually, and marriage does not become a required liturgical act in the West until Trent's *Tametsi* (Unit 6).
