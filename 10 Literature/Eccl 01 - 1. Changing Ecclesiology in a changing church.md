# 1. Changing Ecclesiology in a changing church.pdf

**TITLE**: A Changing Ecclesiology in a Changing Church: A Symposium on Development in the Ecclesiology of Karl Rahner. Edited by Leo J. O'Donovan, S.J. (Weston School of Theology), with contributions by J. Peter Schineller, S.J. (Jesuit School of Theology in Chicago), John P. Galvin (St. John's Seminary, Brighton, Massachusetts), and Michael A. Fahey (Concordia University, Montreal) [1-3]. Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 38, 1977, pages 736–762 [4-7].

**MAIN SUBJECT**: The historical development, core themes, and major shifts in the ecclesiology of Karl Rahner across three distinct chronological periods: his post-WWII early foundations (1946–1959), his conciliar contributions around Vatican II (1960–1965), and his post-conciliar focus on structural changes and the concrete church (1966–1976) [3].

**KEY TOPICS Covered**:
*   The relationship between individual freedom, personal responsibility, and hierarchical authority in the Church [8, 9].
*   The Church as the "protosacrament" or prime sacrament of God’s grace in the world [10, 11].
*   The theological synthesis of Church membership, baptism of desire, and salvation outside the visible Church [12, 13].
*   The holiness and inherent sinfulness of the Church ("Church of sinners") [14, 15].
*   Office in the Church, focusing on episcopal collegiality, the relationship between the Pope and the college of bishops, and the restoration of the permanent diaconate [16, 17].
*   "Anonymous Christianity" as a speculative framework for the salvation of non-Christians [18, 19].
*   The transition from a cultural "Volkskirche" to a "little flock" in a diaspora situation [20, 21].
*   Structural transformations, basic Christian communities (*Basisgemeinden*), and the task of declericalization [22-24].
*   The mission of the Church in humanizing the world versus the dangers of clericalism and "horizontalism" [25, 26].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Dual Notion of the Church**: Rahner distinguishes between the Church as the community of the redeemed (the place of inner grace) and the Church as an organized, visible, and juridical society with rules [9]. Tension arises because the organized society must safeguard the ultimately private, spiritual sphere of the unique individual before God [9].
2.  **Protosacrament (Prime Sacrament)**: The understanding that the Church, in her visible, concrete, and juridically verifiable appearance, is the real, permanent sign and physical embodiment of God's salvific will and the grace of Christ in the world [10].
3.  **Anonymous Christianity**: The theological theory rooted in Rahner's transcendental anthropology asserting that because God's salvific will is universal, those who accept the grace offered to them but have no explicit contact with the Christian message are "anonymous Christians" whose salvation is still ontologically bound to Jesus Christ [54, 175–177].
4.  **Ecclesiological Monophysitism**: The critical error where the Church conceives of itself purely in terms of its idealized, divine nature, thereby hiding and concealing its actual, historically tarnished concrete reality [27].
5.  **Incomplete Identification (Partial Identification)**: The sociological and psychological phenomenon where modern believers, experiencing various tensions and burdens toward the concrete Church, give only a "partial" or incomplete "yes" to the institutional Church rather than identifying with it unconditionally [28, 29].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **Safeguarding Individual Charism Against Collectivism**: Balancing the visible authority of the hierarchy with the freedom and initiative of the individual, ensuring the Church does not slide into an "ecclesiastical totalitarianism" or "religious dictatorship" [9, 30].
2.  **Reconciling Ecumenical Salvation with Church Necessity**: Explaining how the necessity of the Church as a means of salvation can coexist with the true possibility of salvation for non-Christians and nonbelievers [12, 13].
3.  **The Limits and Scope of Church Office**: Defining how structured church office relates to nonofficial charisms, asserting that office is deeply dependent on these charisms for its effective and fruitful exercise [31, 32].
4.  **Structural Rigidity in a Diaspora Era**: Tackling the institutional "ineptitude" and spiritual "bankruptcy" of the larger Church, and proposing that the Church must restructure itself from below through basic communities to remain viable as a minority in a highly secularized world [21, 23, 24].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "the Church is for persons, persons are not for the Church." (Page 741 / Passage 17) - Context: Setting down the basic principle to protect the rights, duties, and initiative of the individual believer against ecclesiastical collectivity.
*   "she is, in her whole concrete, visible and juridically verifiable appearance, a real sign and embodiment of the salvific will of God and of the grace of Christ." (Page 742 / Passage 22) - Context: Explaining the early foundations of Rahner's sacramental ecclesiology where the Church is viewed as the "protosacrament."
*   "The Church is the salvific society of this final, eschatological stage of salvation history," (Page 747 / Passage 37) - Context: Grounding the permanent, indefectible existence of the Church in the definitive victory of God's self-communication in Christ.
*   "the word 'Church' should be used without qualification only where Church-distinctive elements are found, not wherever Church-constitutive elements are present." (Page 751 / Passage 54) - Context: Setting terminological limits to prevent conceptual confusion when using the "anonymous Christian" theory.
*   "The Church of the future, writes Rahner, will be an open Church, ecumenical from its very roots, democratized, and especially declerical-ized." (Page 761 / Passage 86) - Context: Outlining the necessary structural modifications the Roman Catholic Church must undergo to face the modern world.

**SUMMARY**:
The symposium traces the systematic development of Karl Rahner's ecclesiology, demonstrating how his focus shifted from early postwar defenses of individual freedom, ecumenism, and the "Church of sinners" [8, 12, 14] to a conciliar defense of episcopal collegiality and "anonymous Christianity" [16, 18], and finally to a post-conciliar demand for concrete structural change [23, 24]. Facing a secularized "diaspora" world, Rahner argues that the Church must transition from a state-supported "Volkskirche" to an open, democratized, and declericalized community built from below by spontaneous "basic Christian communities" (*Basisgemeinden*) [21, 23, 24]. Ultimately, the contributors show that Rahner's speculative, transcendental theology served as an essential vehicle for moving Catholic thought from rigid Neo-Scholasticism into active, situational engagement with the modern world [33, 34].
