# Unit 13 — Contemporary Debates and Pastoral Application

## Live Debates

**1. The seal versus mandatory reporting.** The most serious contemporary conflict. Several jurisdictions — following child-protection inquiries, notably Australia's Royal Commission — have legislated or proposed to legislate that clergy must report abuse disclosed in confession. The Church's position is that the seal admits **no exception** and that a priest must accept civil penalty rather than violate it. The counter-argument is that the protection of children must override any professional privilege.

A fair treatment should note what Catholic practice actually permits, which is often missed in public debate: the confessor **may** — and pastorally should — make absolution's fruitfulness depend on the penitent's genuine purpose of amendment, which can include requiring that the penitent self-report or take protective steps; and he may urge the penitent to repeat the disclosure **outside** confession, where the seal does not bind. What he may not do is himself disclose.

**2. Fundamental option.** Developed by **Josef Fuchs** and others: mortal sin is not adequately described as a single grave act, but as a fundamental reorientation of the person away from God, which individual acts may or may not express. **Veritatis Splendor** §§65-70 accepts that there is a fundamental orientation of freedom but rejects the separation of that orientation from concrete choices, insisting that gravely disordered acts freely chosen with full knowledge *can themselves* constitute the rejection of God.

**3. Frequency and the "crisis of confession."** Whether the remedy is better catechesis on sin, more availability, better *ars celebrandi* of the sacrament, wider use of Rite II, or something more fundamental about the culture's relation to guilt.

**4. General absolution.** Whether the restrictions of *Misericordia Dei* are pastorally sustainable in mission territories and situations of severe priest shortage.

**5. Confession and psychotherapy.** They must be distinguished: the confessor is not a therapist, absolution is not catharsis, and grace is not insight. But they are not opposed, and a competent confessor recognises when a penitent's difficulty is a matter for referral rather than penance — most obviously with **scrupulosity**, which the tradition has long identified as a spiritual affliction requiring gentleness and firm direction rather than more confession.

## The Indian and Asian Context

- **Shame and honour.** In cultures organised around honour and shame rather than guilt and innocence, the disclosure of sin carries social weight that Western pastoral manuals often fail to anticipate. Anonymity may matter far more; face-to-face celebration may be experienced as exposure rather than encounter.
- **Community and reconciliation.** Indigenous and village practices of **communal reconciliation** — the resolution of disputes before elders, with restitution and restored relationship — offer a genuine point of contact with the ecclesial dimension recovered by *LG* 11, and with Rite II in particular.
- **Caste.** As with baptism and the Eucharist, the theological question is unavoidable: reconciliation with the Church that one has wounded by sin is hollow where caste division within that same Church remains unaddressed. **Social sin** (*Reconciliatio et Paenitentia* 16) supplies the vocabulary for naming this in the confessional and in preaching.
- **Inculturation.** Michael Amaladoss's methodological caution applies: adaptation must proceed from actual living symbols of reconciliation in the culture, not from a designer's scheme.

## Practical Notes for the Confessor

| Point | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **The confessor's fourfold role** | Judge, physician, teacher, and father — the tradition insists on all four, and the modern rite emphasises the last three |
| **Deferral of absolution** | Rare and requires genuine absence of contrition or purpose of amendment; Liguori's counsel against needless deferral remains normative |
| **The scrupulous penitent** | Do not permit repeated confession of the same forgiven material; give a clear, simple rule of obedience |
| **Doubtful matter** | Where the penitent's culpability is genuinely uncertain, the tradition's moral systems (Unit 10) exist precisely to protect freedom |
| **Danger of death** | **Any** priest may absolve **any** sin and lift **any** censure (c. 976) — the Novatian settlement in canonical form |
| **Anonymity** | The penitent's right; the confessional grille must remain available (*Misericordia Dei*) |
