# Ch. 42 — Theology and Film
*Chapter author: Jolyon Mitchell*

## Core Idea
Mitchell traces film's theological reception from censorious suspicion to a resource that can provoke ethical questioning, illustrate doctrine, and even catalyse prayer, arguing directors function as "visual storytellers" crafting genuine cinematic theologies through image and sound, not merely words on a screen.

## Detailed Explanation
Cinema's rapid twentieth-century rise was initially met with severe ecclesiastical hostility and moral censorship — the Catholic "Legion of Decency" and Pope Pius XI's 1936 encyclical Vigilanti Cura policed film content directly. From the 1960s-70s, North American scholars (Hurley, Wall, May) began serious exploratory theological engagement with film, culminating in a late-century academic boom and film's integration into liturgical worship itself. Mitchell insists theologians must develop genuine visual sensitivity and engage actual film theory, not merely treat movies as illustrated sermons.

## Example
Larry Kreitzer's "reversing the hermeneutical flow" — deliberately using a film adaptation to shed fresh light back onto the biblical text it is based on, rather than only using the Bible to judge the film — has become a concrete, widely-used classroom method in theology-and-film courses.

## Illustration
Early Christian suspicion of cinema treated it like a stranger at the door to be screened and often turned away. Contemporary theological film criticism treats it more like a foreign-language conversation partner: you have to actually learn the grammar of camera angle, edit, and sound before you can hear what it is really saying, rather than assuming it is only saying, badly, what a sermon could say better.

## Key Terms
- **Auteur Theory**: The film-criticism framework privileging the director's personal, overarching vision as a film's primary organizing "author."
- **Reversing the Hermeneutical Flow**: Larry Kreitzer's method of bringing a biblical text, a literary work, and its film adaptation into dialogue, letting the film illuminate the Scripture.
- **Interpretive Arch**: Robert Jewett's two-way exegetical method moving between a film's world and the biblical text's world, treating both with equal exegetical seriousness.
- **Transcendental Style**: Paul Schrader's term for a sparse, non-manipulative filmic style (Ozu, Bresson, Dreyer) that rejects Hollywood convention to facilitate direct encounter with the sacred.
