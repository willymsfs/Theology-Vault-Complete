# Unit 2 — James 5:14-15 — The Locus Classicus

Everything in the Catholic theology of this sacrament turns on one passage, so it must be known in detail.

> **"Is any among you sick? Let him call for the presbyters of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord; and the prayer of faith will save the sick man, and the Lord will raise him up; and if he has committed sins, he will be forgiven."** (James 5:14-15)

## Element-by-Element Exegesis

| Element | Greek | Observations |
|---|---|---|
| "Is any **sick**" | *asthenei* | "To be weak, without strength" — general debility, not necessarily terminal |
| "Let him **call for**" | *proskalesasthō* | **The sick person takes the initiative** — the sacrament is requested, not imposed |
| "the **presbyters** of the church" | *presbyterous tēs ekklēsias* | **Plural, and an office** — not simply "older men," which is the exegetical point Trent defends in canon 4 |
| "let them **pray over** him" | *proseuxasthōsan ep' auton* | The preposition suggests hands extended over the sick person |
| "**anointing** him with oil" | *aleipsantes elaiō* | Note *aleiphō* (ordinary/medicinal anointing), not *chriō* (ritual/messianic anointing) — a genuine exegetical difficulty for those who want a purely ritual reading |
| "**in the name of the Lord**" | *en tō onomati tou Kyriou* | The act is done in Christ's authority, distinguishing it from folk medicine |
| "will **save**" | *sōsei* | Bears both "heal" and "save" — the ambiguity is theologically fertile, not a defect |
| "will **raise him up**" | *egerei* | Likewise both "raise from the sickbed" and the resurrection verb |
| "**if** he has committed sins" | *kan hamartias ē pepoiēkōs* | The conditional is important: sin is **not assumed** to be the cause of the illness |

## The Central Interpretive Question

Does James describe **bodily healing** or **spiritual salvation**? The honest answer is that the vocabulary deliberately holds both, and the history of the sacrament is largely the history of the balance tipping — first toward healing (patristic), then decisively toward preparation for death (medieval), then back toward a unified view (modern).

**A crucial historical note for essays.** The objection that James 5 concerns bodily healing rather than a sacrament for the dying was not first raised by the Reformers. **Cardinal Cajetan**, Thomas de Vio — Luther's own opponent at Augsburg and a thoroughly Catholic authority — argued in his commentary that James refers to anointing for *recovery*, not to extreme unction. That a cardinal made the exegetical point independently is a useful corrective to treating it as a merely polemical Protestant claim, and it partly anticipates the twentieth-century recovery.

## Trent's Use of the Text

Trent (Session XIV) reads James 5 as the **promulgation** of a sacrament *insinuated* in Mark 6:13, and draws from it: the matter (oil), the minister (presbyters, i.e. priests), the effects (grace, strengthening, forgiveness of sins, and sometimes bodily health). Canon 4 specifically anathematises the reading that "presbyters" means merely elders by age rather than ordained priests.
