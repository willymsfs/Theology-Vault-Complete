# Ch. 14 — Postliberal Theology
*Chapter author: James Fodor*

## Core Idea
Postliberal theology (the "Yale school") is a "postcritical," non-foundationalist movement treating Christian doctrines as second-order grammatical rules governing a community's discourse and practice, and Scripture as a text so powerful it "absorbs the world" into its own narrative rather than being translated into external, secular categories.

## Detailed Explanation
Inaugurated by George Lindbeck's The Nature of Doctrine (1984) and Hans Frei's biblical-narrative studies, postliberalism responded to the collapse of the mid-century neo-orthodox consensus by rejecting both the "cognitive-propositional" model (doctrines as timeless factual claims) and the "experiential-expressivist" model (doctrines as expressions of a universal inner religious feeling, associated with liberal theology since Schleiermacher). Instead, Lindbeck's "cultural-linguistic" model treats a religion like a language and culture: you learn to live and speak within it, and doctrines are the community's own grammatical rules for speaking correctly, not free-standing propositions to be proved to outsiders. The movement pictures the Church, in a post-Christendom "diaspora" situation, as needing to learn faithful minority survival from Judaism's own long historical experience of exile (galut).

## Example
Stanley Hauerwas's application of Frei's narrative Christology into Christian ethics — insisting Christians are formed as a distinct, counter-cultural community of virtue and character by immersion in the biblical story, rather than by appeal to supposedly universal, culture-neutral moral reasoning — is postliberalism's clearest practical, real-world extension beyond academic doctrine into everyday Christian formation.

## Illustration
Learning Christian doctrine, on this view, is less like memorizing a set of universal facts ("water boils at 100°C") and more like learning to speak a living language fluently — you do not "prove" French is correct to an outsider, you learn to live inside it, and its grammar only makes full sense from within the practice of speaking it.

## Key Terms
- **Intratextuality**: The interpretive practice of letting Scripture "absorb the world" — redescribing all of reality within the Bible's own narrative and linguistic framework, rather than translating Scripture into external categories.
- **Cultural-Linguistic Model**: Lindbeck's account of religion as a comprehensive, narratively embodied culture/language, with doctrines functioning as communally authoritative grammatical rules.
- **Realistic Narrative**: Hans Frei's category for biblical history: meaning is carried directly by the interplay of character and action in the story itself, prioritizing Jesus's concrete narrative identity over speculative theories about his "presence."
- **Galut** (Hebrew) — "exile": The postliberal image of the Church's post-Christendom minority status, requiring Christians to learn from Judaism's long experience of faithful, non-dominant survival.
