# 16. multiple church belongings.pdf

**TITLE**: "MULTIPLE RELIGIOUS BELONGING: OPPORTUNITIES AND CHALLENGES FOR THEOLOGY AND CHURCH." By Peter C. Phan (Professor and Chair of Catholic Social Thought, Georgetown University). Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 64, 2003, pages 495–519.

**MAIN SUBJECT**: The growing pastoral and theological phenomenon of multiple religious belonging (or double belonging), its justification under a theology of "inclusive pluralism," and its practical implications for Catholic seminary education and ministry.

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   Definition of multiple belonging versus double belonging and postmodern syncretism.
*   Contrast between exclusive Western "Religions of the Book" and functional multi-belonging in Asian cultures.
*   Ten theological assertions of "inclusive pluralism" justifying double identity.
*   Lived experiences of Christian pioneers (Abhishiktananda, Bede Griffiths, Hugo Enomiya-Lassalle, Thomas Merton, Raimundo Panikkar, Michael Rodrigo, Aloysius Pieris).
*   The mystical *advaita* experience and the radical doctrinal tension it introduces.
*   The concept of "symbiosis" vs. syncretism (Aloysius Pieris) and collaboration for social justice.
*   Seven concrete recommendations for reforming Catholic seminary education to prepare for religious diversity.

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Multiple Belonging vs. Double Belonging**: Double belonging is a reflective, theological commitment to two distinct traditions resulting from deep inculturation, whereas multiple belonging is often a contemporary, postmodern, syncretistic consumerism where individuals pick elements from a religious "supermarket" based on personal convenience without regard to truth values [1-3].
2.  **Inclusive Pluralism (or Pluralistic Inclusivism)**: A theology of religions (associated with Jacques Dupuis) asserting that non-Christian religions are autonomous ways of salvation containing elements of truth and grace, operating through the activities of the Logos and the Holy Spirit [4-6].
3.  **Constitutive and Relational Uniqueness of Christ**: The Christological framework asserting that Christ is uniquely constitutive of salvation as the climax of God's plan, yet this uniqueness is relational rather than exclusive, meaning it is open to and connected with other religions [7].
4.  **Intrareligious Dialogue**: A deeply personal, interior encounter where a person allows two or more religious traditions to interact within themselves, under the guidance of a spiritual master [8].
5.  **Symbiosis**: Aloysius Pieris's concept of dialogue where members of different traditions live and work together in basic human communities in favor of the poor, discovering more of their own distinct faith through the other [9].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **New Age Syncretism / Consumerist Spirituality**: Preventing double belonging from degenerating into a superficial, individualized "cafeteria-style" selection of incompatible beliefs without regard to truth values [1, 10, 11].
2.  **Theological Incompatibility of Advaita and Christian Doctrine**: The acute, lived tension of trying to reconcile the non-dualistic *advaita* experience (radical loss of finite self in absolute awareness) with Christian concepts of a tri-personal God, creation, and dialogical prayer [12-14].
3.  **Lack of Interreligious and Missiological Training in Seminaries**: Standard Catholic seminary curriculums are often geographically isolated, overly focused on parish administration, and lacking courses on missiology, comparative theology, and religious pluralism [15, 16].
4.  **The Exclusionary Legacy of Western "Religions of the Book"**: Reconciling the absolute and exclusive claims of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam with the reality of religious pluralism and the necessity of interreligious friendship [17, 18].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "Indeed, the very expression 'multiple religious belonging' as understood in the West... is a misnomer in Asia where religions are considered not as mutually exclusive religious organizations but as having specialized functions responding, according to a division of labor as it were, to the different needs and circumstances in the course of a person's life." (Page 498 / Passage 14) - Context: Explaining that multiple belonging is a popular-level norm in Asian countries due to a functional approach to religion.
*   "Jesus is the unique and universal savior does not exclude the possibility of non-Christians being saved." (Page 500 / Passage 19) - Context: Presenting the first of ten theological assertions underpinning double belonging.
*   "...the double identity is an irreducible and unresolvable tension that must be held together throughout a person's life until, in the evocative words of Abhishiktananda, 'dawn may arise.'" (Page 510-511 / Passage 60) - Context: Describing the existential state of living within two traditions.
*   "The experience of the Upanishads is true - I know!" (Page 513 / Passage 70) - Context: Citing Swami Abhishiktananda's existential certainty of his *advaita* experience despite the theological antinomies.
*   "By accepting the stranger as friend, we allow his or her 'otherness' to confront us radically, challenging us with stories we have never heard, questions we have never raised..." (Page 519 / Passage 93) - Context: Advocating for the development of interreligious friendship (*philia*) in ministerial training.
*   "Multiple religious belonging is not for the faint-hearted and the dilettante. Practitioners of multiple religious belonging... face a demanding vocation, a special call to holiness... It is not unlike martyrdom." (Page 519 / Passage 94) - Context: Concluding with a solemn warning that double belonging is a demanding, grace-filled vocation.

**SUMMARY**:
Peter C. Phan argues that multiple religious belonging represents a major, enriching sign of the times that must be engaged with theological rigor rather than dismissed as superficial syncretism. Rooting his framework in the "inclusive pluralism" of Jacques Dupuis, he asserts that the Logos and the Holy Spirit act salvifically within non-Christian traditions, making them valid, autonomous ways of salvation. By examining the lives of Christian pioneers like Abhishiktananda, Bede Griffiths, and Aloysius Pieris, Phan shows that double belonging requires a demanding combination of intellectual mastery, deep commitment to Christ, docility to spiritual guides, and active solidarity with the poor. He concludes that Catholic theological education must undergo a major reform—introducing mandatory courses in comparative theology, multifaith prayer, and interreligious friendship—to prepare future ministers for an increasingly pluralistic world.
