# Unit 6 — The Reformation and Trent

## The Reformers

**Luther** (*The Babylonian Captivity*, 1520) rejects it forcefully on three grounds: James is not of apostolic authority (Luther's low estimate of the epistle is well known); there is no dominical institution; and the promise James attaches — *"the Lord will raise him up"* — manifestly does not happen, since those anointed die. His sharpest point is the mismatch: the Church promises what James promises but administers it precisely to those it expects *not* to recover. He adds, characteristically, that he would not object to prayer and anointing as James describes, provided no one calls it a sacrament.

**Calvin** (*Institutes* IV.19.18-21) makes the **cessationist** argument: the healing described in James and Mark was a **temporary charismatic gift** given to authenticate the apostolic preaching, which ceased with the apostolic age. To continue the outward sign after the gift has been withdrawn is, in his phrase, a "hypocritical stage-play." Calvin's argument is important because it is the one that most directly severs the sacrament from its scriptural warrant.

**Cardinal Cajetan** — worth restating here — had already argued on purely exegetical grounds that James concerns anointing **for recovery**, not extreme unction. His view was not condemned, and it stands as evidence that the exegetical question was live *within* Catholic theology before and during the Reformation.

**Anglican practice** is instructive: the **1549 Book of Common Prayer retained an anointing** in the Order for the Visitation of the Sick; the **1552 revision removed it**. It was recovered in the twentieth century through the Anglican and Catholic liturgical movements alike.

## Trent, Session XIV (25 November 1551)

Trent treats Extreme Unction alongside Penance, in three chapters and four canons.

| Teaching | Content |
|---|---|
| **Institution** | *Insinuated* by Christ in Mark 6:13, *promulgated* by James 5:14-15 |
| **Matter and form** | Oil blessed by a bishop; the prayer of the priest |
| **Effects** | The grace of the Holy Spirit, "wiping away sins if any remain to be expiated, **and the remnants of sin**"; "alleviating and strengthening the soul of the sick person"; and **"sometimes restores bodily health, when expedient for the salvation of the soul"** |
| **Minister** | Priests — canon 4 anathematises the view that "presbyters" in James means elders by age rather than ordained ministers |
| **Recipients** | "Especially for those who lie **so dangerously ill** as to seem near to death" (*praesertim*) |

**The most important word in the decree is *praesertim* — "especially."** Trent says the sacrament is *especially* for those near death; it does **not** define that it is *only* for them. This single adverb is what leaves the door legally open for *Sacrosanctum Concilium* 73's reform four centuries later. A strong exam answer notes that Vatican II did not contradict Trent here but exploited a deliberate reticence in its wording — exactly parallel to how Trent's *aptissime* on transubstantiation left room for later re-expression.
