# Ch. 31 — Postcolonial Biblical Interpretation
*Chapter author: R. S. Sugirtharajah*

## Core Idea
Postcolonial biblical interpretation places colonialism and globalization at the centre of biblical studies, exposing how supposedly neutral historical-critical scholarship and Bible translation were historically mobilized in service of imperial and missionary expansion, and recovering suppressed subaltern voices and readings.

## Detailed Explanation
Sugirtharajah, a Sri Lankan scholar at the University of Birmingham, introduced postcolonial criticism into biblical studies in a landmark 1996 article, drawing on postcolonial literary theorists (Edward Said, Gayatri Spivak, Homi Bhabha) and passing through heritagist, liberationist, and dissentient phases shaped by geopolitical partition and decolonization (the 1947 founding of Israel, the 1967 war, African and Australian colonization). Its central move is refusing the claim that historical-critical exegesis is neutral or value-free, showing instead how it has often quietly served imperial interests.

## Example
Sugirtharajah's critique of Joachim Jeremias's classic study of Jesus's parables — showing how its scholarly apparatus imports "Orientalist" assumptions about a static, exotic Middle East — demonstrates postcolonial method concretely: even celebrated, mainstream biblical scholarship can carry unexamined imperial assumptions.

## Illustration
Reading a colonial-era map of Africa that names rivers and mountains only after European explorers, as though no one had ever lived there or named them before, hides an entire history under a single layer of paint. Postcolonial biblical interpretation does the same work on the biblical text and its scholarship: scraping back the "explorer's" layer of names and assumptions to recover who and what was already there.

## Key Terms
- **Orientalism**: Edward Said's term for a Western discourse of power that flattens the "Other" into fixed, exotic stereotypes in order to dominate and control it.
- **Hybridity / Mimicry**: Homi Bhabha's terms for the unstable, creative in-between spaces where colonizer and colonized cultures interact, destabilizing simple binary oppositions.
- **Subaltern** (from Latin/English: of lower rank): The colonized or marginalized "Other" whose voice has been silenced or spoken for by others — postcolonial reading works to recover it directly.
- **Nativism**: The strategy of reclaiming indigenous, pre-colonial traditions (e.g. Setswana ancestral Badimo) to resist and subvert colonial translations and categories.

## Connections
Shares Lindbeck's non-foundationalist interest in the biblical text (Part II.D) but radically challenges his "intratextual" confidence, insisting the text itself carries imperial and patriarchal violence needing critique, not just inhabiting. Musa Dube extends postcolonial feminist interpretation from Botswana. Challenges John Hick's pluralism and Gavin D'Costa's theology of religions (Part VII) as too abstract, ignoring real political and economic power.
