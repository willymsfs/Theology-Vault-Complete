# Unit 1 — Biblical Foundations I — Repentance and the Forgiveness of Sins

## The Old Testament Matrix

The Hebrew vocabulary shapes everything that follows. The governing term is ***teshuvah*** — literally "**turning/returning**," not "feeling sorry." Repentance in the Old Testament is a change of *direction*, a coming back to covenant, and only derivatively a state of emotion. This is why the prophetic call is always *"Return to me"* (Joel 2:12; Hos 14:1) rather than "regret your actions."

**The sacrificial system** provided for sin in two categories — the ***hattat*** (purification/sin offering) and the ***asham*** (reparation/guilt offering, Lev 4-5) — with the annual **Day of Atonement** (*Yom Kippur*, Lev 16) as the comprehensive rite: two goats, one sacrificed, one — the **scapegoat** (*azazel*) — laden with the people's sins and driven into the wilderness. Note the two distinct mechanisms already present: **expiation by blood** and **removal by transfer**.

**Psalm 51 (the *Miserere*)** is the tradition's central penitential text, and its theology is sophisticated: it asks for interior renewal ("create in me a clean heart"), locates sin primarily against God ("against you, you alone, have I sinned"), and relativises sacrifice itself — "the sacrifice acceptable to God is a broken spirit." The Church prays it every Friday at Morning Prayer for precisely this reason.

**The prophetic and narrative deepening.** Nathan's confrontation of David (2 Sam 12) supplies the pattern of *accusation, acknowledgement, forgiveness, and remaining consequence* — David is forgiven ("the LORD has put away your sin") yet the child dies. Any adequate theology of penance must account for this structure, which is the seed of the later distinction between **guilt** and **temporal punishment**.

## Jesus and the Forgiveness of Sins

**The programmatic announcement.** *"The kingdom of God is at hand; **repent** (μετανοεῖτε) and believe in the gospel"* (Mk 1:15). ***Metanoia*** — literally a change of *nous*, of mind and outlook — is the Greek rendering of *teshuvah* and carries the same directional force.

**The decisive controversy: the paralytic (Mk 2:1-12).** Jesus says "your sins are forgiven"; the scribes object, "who can forgive sins but God alone?"; Jesus heals the man *"so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority **on earth** to forgive sins."* Three points a BTh student must draw out:

1. The scribes' premise is correct — only God forgives sin.
2. Jesus does not deny it; he claims the prerogative.
3. The phrase **"on earth"** is doing deliberate work. Forgiveness is not deferred to a heavenly tribunal; it is exercised *visibly, in history, by a man*. This is the christological foundation of any sacramental — that is, visible and ecclesial — ministry of forgiveness.

**The parables and encounters.** The sinful woman (Lk 7:36-50 — "her many sins are forgiven, for she loved much"), Zacchaeus (Lk 19:1-10, where conversion issues immediately in restitution), and above all **Luke 15** — the lost sheep, the lost coin, and the prodigal son. The last is the tradition's controlling image: the father runs, the confession is interrupted before it finishes, the robe and ring precede any probation. **Grace outruns the penitent's script.**

**Illustration.** Notice what the prodigal never gets to do. He rehearses a speech — "treat me as one of your hired servants" — and the father cuts him off mid-sentence. Any theology of penance that makes the penitent's performance the cause of the forgiveness has inverted the parable. The confession matters, but it is a response to a father already running, not a fee paid at a gate.
