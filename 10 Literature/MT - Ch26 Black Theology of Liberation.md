# Ch. 26 — Black Theology of Liberation
*Chapter author: Dwight N. Hopkins*

## Core Idea
Black theology treats faithful action in the struggle for black humanity as the first step and doctrinal reflection as a self-critical second step, locating its sources in West African heritage, the trauma of slavery, and the ongoing fight against white supremacy, and re-reading the Exodus and the Incarnation as messages of concrete liberation.

## Detailed Explanation
Contemporary black theology dates programmatically to 31 July 1966, when Northern African American clergy published a "black power" statement in the New York Times validating the "black power" chant Stokely Carmichael had launched during the Meredith March. Its founding generation (James H. Cone, J. Deotis Roberts, Albert Cleage, Cecil Cone) fought a real "academic apartheid" in North American seminaries and white-controlled church structures. Since the late 1970s, later generations have pushed the field further: womanist theology (Jacqueline Grant, Delores S. Williams) named the "double" or even "multiple jeopardy" black women face across race, gender, and class simultaneously; Black Roman Catholic theologians (M. Shawn Copeland, Diana L. Hayes) have challenged both white Catholic dominance and Protestant dominance within black theology itself.

## Example
James H. Cone's adaptation of Barth's Christological realism and Tillich's "ultimate concern" (both Part I) to the concrete historical experience of black oppression — arguing God is, in a real and not merely metaphorical sense, "black" because God is unconditionally on the side of the oppressed — is the discipline's founding and most contested theological move.

## Illustration
Ontological blackness, as Victor Anderson criticises it, is like a single stage costume every black character in a play is required to wear regardless of who they actually are underneath — Anderson's push for a non-essentialist black identity is the demand to let each actor's own face show through, rather than requiring one fixed mask to represent an entire community.

## Key Terms
- **Ontological Blackness**: The essentialized, overdetermined concept of black identity constructed by white supremacy — criticised by Victor Anderson, who argues for a non-essentialist, internally diverse black identity.
- **Hoodoo / Sacred Africanity**: The syncretic blend of West African traditional religion and Christian forms forged by enslaved Africans, read by Cecil Cone and Will Coleman as an authentic spiritual undercurrent of black faith.
- **Xodus**: Garth Kasimu Baker-Fletcher's framework for reconstructing African American manhood beyond internalized white supremacy, in ecological, non-sexist partnership with womanist theology.
- **Dark Salutations**: Riggins R. Earl Jr.'s term for stylized greetings of recognition within the black community (e.g. "brotherman," "soul sister") as a lived linguistic ethics of dignity.
