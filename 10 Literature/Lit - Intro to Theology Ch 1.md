## CHAPTER OVERVIEW (The Big Picture)

Chapter I answers one basic question: **What is theology?**

It moves through a clear logic:

1. Start with the **God-Question** — why humans naturally talk about God at all.
2. Ask **how** finite humans can even know or speak of an infinite God.
3. Collect the many **definitions** of theology across history.
4. Notice the built-in **tensions (polarities)** every theologian must balance.
5. Relate theology to the **other sciences**.
6. State the **goals** theology serves.

Keep this map in mind — every section below is one stop on this journey.

## A. THE GOD-QUESTION

### 1. What "the God-Question" Means

**Simple meaning:** Before theology can say anything *about* God, it must face the fact that humans are always *asking* about God. The human being is, by nature, a questioner who reaches for ultimate meaning.

**Exact phrase:**
- Theology is *"the discourse on God or God-talk."*

**The human as a questioner:**
Humans do not only ask "What is for dinner?" They also ask: *Why do I exist? What happens at death? Is there an ultimate meaning?* These are **ultimate questions**. The presence of these questions in every culture shows the human being is oriented beyond the visible world.

**Example:**
A child at a funeral asks, "Where did grandmother go?" That is not a science question — it is a God-Question. It reaches for what lies beyond the physical.

### 2. Theism and the Idea of God

**What is Theism?** Theism = belief in God / an Ultimate Reality.

The book notes God is conceived **differently** across religions and philosophies:
- **Personal God** (Christianity, Judaism, Islam): God is a personal being, *"the origin and destiny of the universe and of humankind."*
- **Impersonal Absolute** (some systems): God as *"an Absolute Power, a Supreme Mind or Will or Spirit, a Pure Act (Actus purus), the Divine, the Sacred, a Higher Power."*

Despite differences, God is understood as:
- *"the Ultimate Reality,"*
- *"the Ultimate Ground of Being,"*
- *"the Ultimate Horizon of Meaning."*

**Key insight (quoted):** Even if old terms for God must change, *"an ultimate point of reference for grasping and interpreting human life and the world will always be needed"* (Gordon D. Kaufman).

### 3. Can We Know God by Reason?

**The book's position:** Yes — in principle, reason can reach God.

**Church teaching (Vatican I, *Dei Filius*), quoted:**
- *"God, the first principle and last end of all things, can be known with certainty from the created world by the natural light of human reason."*

**Thomas Aquinas's Five Proofs:**
- These are **cosmological arguments** — meaning arguments that move *from the world (cosmos) back to its cause*.
- Logic: from **effects** we trace back to the **cause**, arriving finally at the *"Ultimate Cause and the Prime Mover."*
- *Prime Mover* = the first source of all motion/change; the one that moves everything else but is not itself moved.

**Honest limitation (Cardinal Newman), quoted:**
- These proofs are *"abstract and philosophical,"* and can only provide a *"convergence of diverse probabilities."*
- **Simple meaning:** The proofs help someone who *already* believes; they rarely *force* a non-believer to believe. They stack up probabilities pointing toward God rather than delivering a knock-down proof.

**Example:**
Seeing a watch, you infer a watchmaker. That is cosmological reasoning: effect (watch) → cause (maker). Convincing to some, not airtight to all.

### 4. Why Our God-Language Is Limited: ANALOGICAL Knowledge

This is a crucial concept. Explained from scratch:

**Three ways words can apply to God:**

| Way | Meaning | Problem |
|-----|---------|---------|
| **Univocal** | Same meaning as when applied to creatures | Would make God just a bigger creature |
| **Equivocal** | Totally different meaning | Would mean we say nothing real about God |
| **Analogical** | Similar-but-different meaning | The correct middle path |

**Analogical knowledge (simple meaning):**
When we call God *"good"* or *"father,"* the word is neither exactly the same as human goodness/fatherhood (univocal) nor completely unrelated (equivocal). It is **analogical** — truly pointing to God, but always falling short.

**Exact phrases:**
- *"Our human words always fall short of the mystery of God."*
- We must *"continually purify our language"* so we never confuse our human pictures with the incomprehensible God.

**Example:**
Calling God "Father" tells you something true (loving source, protector) — but God is not male, not biological, not limited like a human father. The word points; it does not capture.

### 5. Religious Experience and Symbol

**Why symbols?** Humans are **embodied** (we live through bodies and senses), so our experience of God comes through concrete things — words, images, rituals.

**Religious experience defined (quoted):**
- *"that intimate and immediate awareness of being touched or grasped by the Divine."*

Because this experience is mediated through the body, it is expressed in **symbolic language**.

**What is a symbol (simple)?** A symbol is a visible/concrete thing that carries and communicates a deeper invisible reality. Water in Baptism is not just water — it *symbolizes* cleansing and new life.

**Human openness to God (quoted):**
- Christian theology deals with the human being's *"fundamental openness to God."*

**Example:**
A believer feels overwhelmed by peace during prayer ("touched by the Divine") and later describes it using symbols — "light," "warmth," "a father's embrace" — because there are no literal words for the infinite.

## B. VARIOUS DEFINITIONS OF CHRISTIAN THEOLOGY

Theology has been defined differently in each era. Learn these as a historical series.

### 1. Etymology (Word-Origin)

**From Greek:**
- *Theos* = God
- *Logos* = word, discourse, science / reason

**Literal meaning (quoted):**
- *"discourse on God," "science of God," "God-talk."*

### 2. Greek and Patristic Era

- **Greek philosophers** (Plato, Aristotle): used "theology" for **metaphysics** (study of ultimate reality) and even to critique the poets' myths.
- **Church Fathers** (Clement of Alexandria, Origen): used it for *"Trinitarian and Christological discussions"* and the *"interpretation of the Sacred Scriptures."*

*Christological = about the person and work of Christ. Trinitarian = about God as Father, Son, Spirit.*

### 3. Medieval / Scholastic Era — Anselm's Classic Definition

**The most famous definition of all:**
- *fides quaerens intellectum* = *"faith seeking understanding."*
- Anselm's motto: *"I believe in order to understand"* (*credo ut intelligam*).

**Simple meaning:** Faith comes first and *drives* the search for understanding. You do not understand and then believe; you believe, and belief makes you want to understand.

### 4. The Eastern Approach — Prayer and Belief Together

**Latin dictum (quoted):**
- *Lex orandi, lex credendi* = *"the law of praying is the law of believing."*

**Simple meaning:** How the Church **prays** shapes and reveals what the Church **believes**. Worship and doctrine are inseparable. To know Eastern theology, watch its liturgy.

### 5. Modern and Contemporary Definitions

**a) Theological Anthropology — Karl Rahner**
- *"God and humanity are correlative terms."*
- Theology studies not God in isolation, but the human being and *"the mystery of God's plan for human salvation."*
- *Correlative = they belong together; explain one and you are already explaining the other.*

**b) Critical Reflection on Praxis — Gustavo Gutierrez**
- Theology is *"a critical reflection on Christian praxis in the light of the Word."*
- Shifts focus from **orthodoxy** (right thinking) to **orthopraxis** (right action) and the transformation of the world.
- *Praxis = committed, practical action — not just theory.*

**c) Hermeneutics (Interpretation)**
- Theology is *"the interpretation of Christian faith in the context of contemporary existential realities and the interpretation of contemporary realities in the light of the Word of God."*
- *Hermeneutics = the art/science of interpretation.* It is a **two-way street**: read today's life through the Gospel, and read the Gospel afresh through today's life.

**d) Interpretation of the Christian Story (Mythos)**
- Theology reflects on the Christian *"mythos"* — the *"set of symbols, rituals, narratives and beliefs"* through which a community encounters the Divine.
- *Mythos here does NOT mean "false story." It means the deep foundational story a community lives by.*

### 6. The Book's Own Working Definition (Learn This Well)

The authors synthesize everything into one full definition (quoted):

> *"Christian theology is a systematic and critical interpretation on the meaning of human life and reality in general from the perspective of the Revelation in Jesus Christ on the one hand, and a reinterpretation of Christian Faith on the other, in the light of the new experience and context of the changing realities of the world, in and by the believing community."*

**Unpacking this definition (simple):**

| Part of the definition | What it means |
|------------------------|---------------|
| "systematic and critical interpretation" | Ordered + questioning, not random opinion |
| "meaning of human life and reality" | Theology explains all of life, not just "church topics" |
| "perspective of the Revelation in Jesus Christ" | Christ is the lens |
| "reinterpretation of Christian Faith" | Faith must be re-expressed for each new age |
| "changing realities of the world" | Theology answers today's real situations |
| "in and by the believing community" | It is the Church's work, not a lone individual's |

## C. CREATIVE POLARITIES IN THEOLOGIZING

A **polarity** = two opposite pulls that must be held together in tension. Drop either side and theology goes wrong. The book lists **seven**.

### 1. Mystery and Intelligibility

- Between the errors of **Fideism** and **Rationalism**.
  - *Fideism = faith only, reason has no role.*
  - *Rationalism = reason only, no room for mystery.*
- Reason understands faith *to a limited extent*, but *"faith alone makes it possible to penetrate the mystery in a way that allows us to understand it coherently."*
- The tension stays because *"what we know and speak about is not the whole truth; there is an underlying deeper mystery to which we can only point."*

**Example:** We can explain much about the Trinity, yet never exhaust it. We understand *and* stand before mystery.

### 2. Identity and Change

- Theology's fixed identity rests on the *"Christ-event, its confession, proclamation and witness."*
- But since the Church lives in history, its formulas need continuous reform.
- Result: *"Christian theology thus involves a creative tension between continuity and change."*

**Example:** The truth "Jesus is Lord" never changes; how we explain it to a 21st-century Indian differs from a 5th-century Greek.

### 3. Committed and Critical

- **Committed:** *"theologizing cannot be done without the living experience and practice of faith."*
- **Critical:** theology must *"seriously examine all Christian beliefs... with a critical mind."*
- A true theologian both *loves* the tradition and *questions* it.

**Example:** A son who loves his father can still respectfully question his father's decisions. That double stance is theology.

### 4. Community and Individual Theologian

- The *"Christian community is the real locus theologicus"* — the true "place of theology" where faith is guarded and handed on.
  - *Locus theologicus (Latin) = a "source-place" of theology.*
- Yet *"every reform and renewal is initiated by the inspiration and creativity of individuals who have a prophetic call or gift."*

**Example:** The community carries the faith; but a prophet (an Aquinas, a Rahner) sparks renewal from within it.

### 5. One and Many (Transcendental and Historical)

- The *"Christ-event"* grounds a *"common... universal Christian theology."*
- Yet *"pluralism in Christian theology and Catholic theology is both theoretically and practically accepted."*
- Theology forever moves between *"the one and the many, the universal and the particular."*

**Example:** One Gospel; many valid theologies (African, Asian, Latin American) — like one melody sung in many languages.

### 6. Universal and Contextual

- *"All theologies... are historically, socially and culturally situated."*
- The strong claim: *"no theology is, in fact, perennial. All theologies are contextual."*
- This gave rise to **Liberation, Feminist, and Dalit theologies**.
  - *Dalit theology = theology from the perspective of the oppressed "Dalit" communities of India, formerly called "untouchables."*

**Example:** A theology written for wealthy Europe cannot simply be copied for poor, caste-divided India; it must be re-rooted in the Indian context.

### 7. Orthodoxy and Heterodoxy

- *"Fidelity to the original and authentic Christian faith or orthodoxy has been always a perennial concern."*
  - *Orthodoxy = right/accepted belief.*
  - *Heterodoxy = belief that departs from the accepted teaching.*
- Yet heterodoxy, though condemned, *"may contain new insights for doctrinal and theological developments."*

**Example:** Some ideas once judged "heterodox" later pushed the Church to clarify and deepen doctrine. History is a tension between guarding truth and testing new insight.

## D. THEOLOGY AND THE OTHER SCIENCES

**Is theology a science?** Yes — it is the *"systematic study of Christian faith"* with its own proper method.

**Why theology can dialogue with all sciences (quoted):**
- Because *"all truth and reality come from God."*
- And: *"methodological research in all branches of knowledge... can never conflict with the faith, because the things of the world and the things of faith derive from the same God."*

### Theology and Philosophy
- Philosophy was traditionally called the *"handmaid of theology"* (*ancilla theologiae*).
  - *Handmaid = servant/helper. Philosophy serves theology by supplying tools of reasoning.*
- Difference: philosophy uses **reason alone**; theology is *"guided by the revealed Word of God and faith."*
- Theologians borrow philosophical categories — Greek *logos*, Indian *Vedanta* — to express faith in each culture.

### Theology and the Social Sciences
- Contextual and Liberation theologies use sociology, economics, politics.
- Goal is not only to *"understand reality"* but to *"transform reality"* by exposing the root causes of oppression.

### Theology and the Religious Sciences
- Theology draws on the psychology, sociology, and phenomenology of religion to analyze its data.

**Example:** To preach effectively against caste injustice, a theologian may use sociology (to see the problem clearly) and Scripture (to judge and transform it).

## E. THE GOALS / FUNCTIONS OF THEOLOGY

The book lists complementary goals — theology is not for its own sake but serves faith and the world.

1. **Awakening Faith** — to *"awaken, strengthen and communicate the faith-experience"* of the community.
2. **Assimilation** — to explain and systematize faith for a *"better understanding of faith-experience"* in an *"ongoing and continuous process."*
3. **Biblical Discovery** — to *"sift the human words in the Bible and to discover within them and beyond them the real Word of God."*
4. **Relevance** — an *"interpretative and prophetic role"* to *"make the faith relevant and meaningful for today."*
5. **Systematic Exposition** — to show the *"unity and the interconnectedness"* of doctrines, all rooted in the mystery of Christ.
6. **Pastoral Empowerment** — a *"pastoral role of strengthening the faith and empowering Christian life."*
7. **Missionary Witness** — to *"witness to Christ, to proclaim the Gospel and thus to continue Jesus' mission."*
8. **World Transformation** — to promote the *"transformation of the whole world into the Kingdom of God"* through justice, equality, and harmony.

**Example:** Good theology should end not merely in a clever idea but in a stronger believer, a more faithful Church, and a more just world.
