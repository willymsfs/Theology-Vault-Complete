# Ch. 37 — Judaism and Christian Theology
*Chapter author: Peter Ochs*

## Core Idea
Ochs maps the post-Shoah recovery of Christian theology's relationship to Judaism, promoting "non-supersessionism" — the conviction that God's covenant with Israel remains permanent and unrevoked — and "scriptural reasoning," a tripartite reading practice linking any Christian doctrine back to its grounding in Israel's own story.

## Detailed Explanation
The catastrophe of the Shoah drove Jewish and Christian theologians alike to repent of centuries of Christian anti-Judaic theology, including the traditional "supersessionist" claim that the Church simply replaced Israel in God's plan. Ochs co-authored the landmark 2001 Jewish statement on Christianity, Dabru Emet ("speak the truth"), and champions "scriptural reasoning" — reading a doctrine simultaneously through an Old Testament text, a Gospel text, and a community's own lived situation — as a method that keeps Christology rooted in Israel's ongoing story rather than floating free of it.

## Example
Robert Jenson's argument that the risen Christ remains, permanently, "the risen Jew" — so that Christ's own concrete Jewish identity is not incidental but theologically load-bearing — gives non-supersessionism a sharply concrete Christological form rather than leaving it as an abstract ethical commitment about tolerance.

## Illustration
Supersessionism pictured Israel's covenant like an old, cancelled contract, fully replaced by a new one. Non-supersessionism insists it is more like an original, still-valid contract that a second, related agreement (the New Covenant) has joined and extended, not torn up — both remain binding and real, read together rather than one erasing the other.

## Key Terms
- **Non-Supersessionism**: The conviction that God's covenant with Israel is permanent and enduring, rejecting the traditional claim that the Church has replaced Israel in salvation history.
- **Tripartite Reading**: A postliberal hermeneutical practice reading any doctrine through an Old Testament text, a Gospel text, and a community's lived context together.
- **Jeremianic Turn**: John Howard Yoder's category for early rabbinic Judaism's shift (modeled on Jeremiah 29) toward pacifist, landless exilic existence, taken as a model for Anabaptist ecclesiology.
- **Shalom**: Kendall Soulen's eschatological model: God's final redemption does not erase the difference between Israel and the nations but brings them into mutual, interdependent blessing.

## Connections
Barth's Christocentrism (Part I) once called the synagogue "idolatrous," yet his own scriptural obedience forced him to affirm Israel's enduring election — a "Barthian-yet-post-Barthian" foundation later German non-supersessionists built on. George Lindbeck (Part II.D) links healing the Jewish-Christian schism to Catholic-Protestant reconciliation itself. John Milbank (Part II.E) rejects tripartite reading as anachronistic, which postliberals read as a return to supersessionism.
