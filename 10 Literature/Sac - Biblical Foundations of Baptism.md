# Unit 1 — Biblical Foundations of Baptism

## Old Testament Prefigurements (Types)

The Church has never read Baptism as an invention of the New Testament. It reads it as the fulfilment of a pattern God had been rehearsing throughout Israel's history — water as the place where God destroys, delivers, and re-creates. All six classic types appear in the Easter Vigil's Blessing of Water, which is itself a compressed course in biblical theology.

| Type | Text | What it prefigures |
|---|---|---|
| The Spirit over the waters | Gen 1:2 | Water as the womb of creation; Baptism as *new* creation |
| The Flood | Gen 7-8; **1 Pet 3:20-21** | Judgement on sin, salvation of the few "through water" |
| The Red Sea crossing | Exod 14; **1 Cor 10:1-2** | Liberation from slavery; "baptised into Moses" |
| Crossing the Jordan | Josh 3 | Entry into the promised inheritance |
| Circumcision | Gen 17; **Col 2:11-12** | Covenant incorporation, now "not made with hands" |
| Naaman's washing | 2 Kgs 5:10-14 | Cleansing of the Gentile through obedient washing |

The single most important Old Testament text for baptismal theology is **Ezekiel 36:25-27** — "I will sprinkle clean water upon you... A new heart I will give you, and a new spirit I will put within you." Note the structure: **water and Spirit together**, cleansing *and* interior transformation. This is precisely the two-fold structure Jesus names in John 3:5.

**Second Temple background.** Two practices were already in place when John the Baptist appeared: *proselyte baptism* (a self-administered immersion by which a Gentile entered Israel) and the repeated ritual ablutions of Qumran. John's baptism differs from both — it is **once-for-all**, **administered by another**, **eschatological**, and, scandalously, **demanded of Jews**, not just Gentiles.

**Illustration.** Think of the Old Testament types as a composer's sketches. The Flood, the Red Sea, the Jordan, circumcision — each is a fragment of melody. Baptism is not a new tune; it is the symphony in which every fragment finally finds its place.

## John's Baptism and the Baptism of Jesus

John's baptism *prepares*; Christ's baptism *empowers*. The distinction is not academic — Luke stages it as a real pastoral problem in **Acts 19:1-7**, where Paul finds disciples at Ephesus who have received "John's baptism" and have "not even heard that there is a Holy Spirit." They are baptised again "in the name of the Lord Jesus." This is the only unambiguous re-baptism in the New Testament, and it works precisely because John's rite was never Christian baptism at all.

**Jesus's own baptism** (Mk 1:9-11; Mt 3:13-17; Lk 3:21-22) raises the question the Fathers immediately asked: *why would the sinless one submit to a baptism of repentance?* Three classical answers:

1. **Solidarity** — he stands in the queue with sinners (the whole logic of the Incarnation in miniature).
2. **Sanctification of the waters** — he did not need the water; the water needed him. He does not take away its impurity; he leaves behind his holiness.
3. **Trinitarian manifestation** — Father's voice, Spirit as dove, Son in the water. Every Christian baptism participates in *this* scene, which is why the Trinitarian formula is description, not decoration.

**And there is a second "baptism."** Jesus uses the word for his death: *"I have a baptism to be baptised with, and how I am constrained until it is accomplished"* (Lk 12:50; cf. Mk 10:38). This is the hinge on which Pauline baptismal theology turns — baptism is not primarily a washing, it is a **drowning**.

## The Dominical Command

**Matthew 28:19** — "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit."

Three things a BTh student must be able to say about this verse:

- The **singular "name"** (*eis to onoma*, not "names") is a monotheistic confession, not tritheism — the same grammatical point that anchors patristic Trinitarian argument.
- *Eis to onoma* is commercial-legal Greek for transfer of ownership into an account. Baptism transfers you into the possession of the Triune God.
- **Critical honesty:** many exegetes hold the developed triadic formula reflects the liturgical practice of Matthew's community rather than *ipsissima verba*. This does not undermine the sacrament — the Church's practice *is* the apostolic tradition — but a BTh student should not pretend the historical question does not exist.

## Pauline Theology: Baptism as Death and Resurrection

For Paul, baptism is not chiefly about *getting clean*. It is about *dying*.

**Romans 6:3-11** — "Do you not know that all of us who have been baptised into Christ Jesus were baptised into his death? We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, so that as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life."

The three-fold movement — **into his death / buried with him / raised to newness of life** — is why the early Church used immersion in a pool with steps down and steps up, often cruciform or octagonal. You descend into a grave and climb out of a womb.

The other great Pauline texts:

- **Gal 3:27-28** — "As many of you as were baptised into Christ have *put on* Christ (*enedysasthe*). There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female." Baptism is the ground of Christian social equality. This verse has done more work in liberation, feminist, and Dalit theology than any other sacramental text.
- **1 Cor 12:13** — "In one Spirit we were all baptised into one body." Baptism is *ecclesial* before it is individual.
- **Titus 3:5** — "the washing of regeneration (*loutron palingenesias*) and renewal in the Holy Spirit."
- **Eph 4:4-5** — "one Lord, one faith, one baptism." The verse on which the entire modern ecumenical movement rests.
- **Col 2:11-12** — baptism as "the circumcision of Christ," made without hands.

## Baptism in Acts: The Untidy Evidence

Acts refuses to give a single tidy pattern — and that untidiness is theologically important.

| Episode | Sequence | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Pentecost (2:38) | Preaching, repentance, baptism, Spirit | The "normal" pattern |
| Samaria (8:14-17) | Baptism, *then* apostolic hand-laying, Spirit | **The seed-text of Confirmation** |
| Ethiopian eunuch (8:36-38) | Instruction, immediate baptism | No delay, no catechumenate |
| Cornelius (10:44-48) | **Spirit first**, water afterward | God is not bound by the sacraments he binds us to |
| Lydia, jailer (16:15, 33) | "she and her household" (*oikos*) | The disputed foundation of infant baptism |
| Ephesus (19:1-7) | Re-baptism plus hand-laying | John's baptism is not Christian baptism |

**Teaching point.** Cornelius (Acts 10) is the text every good sacramental theology must reckon with. Peter's argument — *"Can anyone withhold the water?"* — assumes the Spirit had already been given. The sacraments are the *ordinary* means God has bound himself to; they are not a cage in which God is trapped. Aquinas formalises this as *"God is not bound to the sacraments"* (*ST* III q.64 a.7) — which is exactly what licenses the later doctrines of baptism of desire and blood.

## Key Terms

| Term | Language | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *baptizo* | Greek | to dip, plunge, immerse — a dyer's and shipwreck word, not a sprinkling word |
| *loutron palingenesias* | Greek | "washing of rebirth/regeneration" (Titus 3:5) |
| *eis to onoma* | Greek | "into the name" — transfer of ownership |
| *oikos* | Greek | household — the disputed basis of paedobaptism |
| *sphragis* | Greek | seal, brand, ownership mark |
