# Unit 3 — The Patristic Era — Canonical Penance

## The Problem: Post-Baptismal Sin

Baptism forgives all sin. What, then, of grave sin committed *after* baptism? The early Church had no obvious answer, and its first instinct was severe. **Hebrews 6:4-6** and **10:26-27** appeared to exclude a second repentance outright, and this rigorist reading was widely held.

## The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140)

The decisive text. Hermas reports a revelation permitting **one — and only one — post-baptismal repentance**. The concession is explicitly framed as an act of mercy for a Church that had discovered its members sinning gravely, and as strictly non-repeatable.

This "second plank after shipwreck" (later formalised by Trent as ***secunda tabula post naufragium***) is the origin of the whole penitential system. Note its logic: it is a *concession*, granted reluctantly, and its non-repeatability is the price of granting it at all.

## The Three Capital Sins and the Structure of Canonical Penance

The system addressed three grave, publicly-known sins: **apostasy, murder, and adultery**. Its shape:

1. **Confession to the bishop** — sins were disclosed privately to the bishop, who determined the penance. (The persistent claim that patristic confession was itself public is a **misconception**; what was public was the *penance*, not usually the disclosure.)
2. **Enrolment in the *ordo paenitentium***, the "order of penitents" — a distinct canonical state, with its own place in the assembly, distinctive dress, sometimes shorn hair, and often prostration.
3. **The penance itself** — fasting, almsgiving, prayer, sexual continence, exclusion from the Eucharist, often lasting **years or decades**. Nicaea's canons 11-13 legislate terms of several years for various categories.
4. **Reconciliation by the bishop's imposition of hands**, ordinarily on **Holy Thursday**.
5. **Permanent consequences** even after reconciliation: in many churches, penitents were barred for life from marriage or remarriage, from military service, from public office, and from ordination.

***Exomologesis*** is Tertullian's term for the whole process — the public "confessing out" of one's penitent condition through this disciplined life.

## Tertullian's Two Positions

A superb illustration of the tension in the early Church, since the same author argues both sides at different periods:

- ***De Paenitentia*** (Catholic period, c. 203) — permits the "second repentance," describes *exomologesis* vividly, and urges sinners not to delay out of shame.
- ***De Pudicitia*** (Montanist period, c. 217) — reverses course, denying the Church has any power to forgive adultery and fornication, and attacking a bishop (probably **Callistus of Rome**) for a decree extending forgiveness to sins of the flesh.

## The Fatal Practical Consequence

Because canonical penance was **once-only**, **public**, **severe**, and carried **lifelong disabilities**, the entirely predictable result was mass **deferral to the deathbed** — the same pattern that afflicted baptism in the same period (compare the Sacraments course, Unit 2). Bishops themselves often counselled the young to wait. Augustine's blunt assessment of deathbed penance is worth quoting in essays: he says of such a person that we cannot be sure he is condemned, but neither can we praise the security of his repentance.

**By the sixth century the system had effectively collapsed** — not because the Church rejected it, but because almost nobody used it. This vacuum is what the Irish penitentials would fill.
