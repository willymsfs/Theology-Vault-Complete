# Part 9 — Contemporary Systematic Debates and Recommended Study Approach

## Divine Simplicity versus Social Trinitarianism

**Classical/scholastic defense:** patristic, medieval, and Reformation orthodoxies uniformly held the divine nature is simple, incomposite, ineffable — essence identical to existence, "without accidents, without movement, and without becoming" (Stephen Holmes). For the whole classical tradition, divine simplicity is not a philosophical obstacle to work around but, per Holmes, the very "heart of Trinitarian doctrine."

**Social trinitarian rejection:** modern social trinitarians (Moltmann, Boff) reject the classical starting point of a single, monadic divine "substance," arguing this collapses into practical modalism or authoritarian "theism"; they define God's unity as perichoretic "unitedness" instead. **The critique** (Holmes; Veli-Matti Kärkkäinen): social trinitarians are forced to "sacrifice traditional accounts of divine unity... before [the dogma] can generate 'acceptable' political content for today"; because contemporary proposals take threeness for granted, "the unity of God has become a major challenge for most contemporary trinitarian proposals."

## The Psychological Analogy versus the Social/Relational Analogy

**Psychological analogy** (Augustine, refined by Aquinas): risks treating God as a "supra-subjectivity"; Moltmann and others charge it "jeopardizes the *interpersonal* relations in the Trinity" and fosters "practical modalism" among ordinary believers. **Social analogy** (Moltmann, Zizioulas, Boff): risks tritheism; **Kenneth Surin's** critique of David Brown's social model — three "distinct centres of consciousness" makes God "more like a family than an individual." **Rowan Williams** counters that Aquinas's "subsistent relations" make it "strictly impossible to speak of any kind of independent agency or... independent subjectivities."

## Is "Person" Still a Coherent Modern Category?

Patristic/medieval *persona*/*hypostasis* carried no psychological connotations; Boethius's "an individual substance of a rational nature" signified objective subsistence; Aquinas's *relatio subsistens* redefines person as constituted wholly by relation of origin. The modern Cartesian-Lockean shift redefined "person" as an autonomous center of self-consciousness — applying this to the Trinity implies three separate wills and intellects, tritheism by definition. Rahner's own warning: *"when nowadays we hear of 'three persons' we connect, almost necessarily... the idea of three centres of consciousness and activity, which leads to a heretical misunderstanding of the dogma."* Two responses: Barth and Rahner drop "person" for "modes of being" (*Seinsweisen*)/"modes of subsistence" (*Subsistenzweisen*); Zizioulas and social trinitarians embrace "person" but redefine it relationally.

## Critiques of "Trinitarianizing" Politics and Ecclesial Structure

**Karen Kilby's "vicious hermeneutical circle":** projecting favored modern ideals onto God's inner life as *perichoresis*, then reversing the analogy to declare those projected values divine norms — risking conceptual idolatry. **Kathryn Tanner's** critique of political vagueness: appeals to *perichoresis* yield only "very vague recommendations," leaving concrete policy unresolved. **Tendentious applications:** Zizioulas's monarchy of the Father underwrites hierarchical episcopacy; Volf's alters the grammar for egalitarian free-church democracy; Boff appeals to the Trinity for socialist liberation; Novak for democratic capitalism. **David Fergusson's** verdict: such correlations are "highly tendentious." The disanalogy is basic — in God, three persons share one undivided will and act *ad extra* as one; human communities are made of really distinct wills that must *choose* cooperation, not divine unanimity.

## Recommended Study Approach

**Reading order:** start with the biblical/pre-Nicene material (Part 1) to see real theological pressure, not abstract speculation, generating the doctrine; read Nicene-Constantinopolitan material (Part 2) alongside the actual Creed text; read Augustine (Part 3) before the medieval scholastics (Part 4); treat the *filioque*/Florence material (Part 5) as a case study in how technical grammar carries ecclesial-political weight; read the 20th-century revival (Part 6) as reactions against a shared starting point (Rahner's axiom), then Volf and Emery (Part 7) as two opposed correctives to the revival itself; read Panikkar and the PCID (Part 8) last among the historical material; use Part 9's debates as a running index across every earlier Part.

**Key tensions to master:** divine simplicity vs. social trinitarianism is the deepest fault line beneath nearly every debate in Parts 6-9; the monarchy of the Father as essential ground of unity (Nazianzen, Zizioulas) or root of illegitimate hierarchy (Moltmann, Volf); economic/immanent Trinity as strict identity (Rahner) or careful non-identity (Emery, classical tradition).

**Essay questions:** (1) Trace "person" from Tertullian through Boethius, Richard of St Victor, Aquinas, and Rahner — where, if anywhere, does the term become incoherent applied to the Trinity? (2) Compare Zizioulas's and Volf's uses of the "monarchy of the Father" to ground opposite ecclesiologies. (3) Does Emery's Thomistic *ressourcement* answer Jowers's critique of Rahner, or merely restate the pre-Rahnerian position? (4) Using Kilby's "vicious hermeneutical circle," evaluate one social-trinitarian political application in detail. (5) Does Panikkar's cosmotheandric vision develop or (per D'Costa) invert the *vestigia Trinitatis* tradition?

**Debate-preparation topics:** "Divine simplicity and social trinitarianism are incompatible starting points, not compatible emphases." · "The psychological analogy is pastorally dangerous regardless of its philosophical defensibility." · "Rahner's *Grundaxiom* was a necessary corrective, whatever its technical problems." · "Trinitarian doctrine cannot avoid being 'politicized' — the only question is whether it is politicized honestly."
