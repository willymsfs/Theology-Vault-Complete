# Unit 13 — Practical and Pastoral Application — The Indian Context

## Arranged Marriage and the Theology of Consent

This is the most theologically interesting pastoral question in the Indian context, and it deserves careful treatment rather than either dismissal or uncritical endorsement.

**The doctrinal position is clear:** consent is the efficient cause of marriage and must be a **free act of the will** (c. 1057). Marriage contracted through **force or grave fear inflicted from outside**, even indirectly, from which one can only escape by choosing marriage, is **invalid** (c. 1103).

**But arrangement is not, in itself, coercion.** A marriage arranged by families in which both parties freely consent is entirely valid, and the Church has never taught otherwise — European Catholic marriage was largely arranged for most of its history. The pastoral task is therefore **discernment of the actual freedom of the consent**, not opposition to the custom. Practical markers: was there a genuine right of refusal? Was refusal survivable socially and economically? Was there adequate knowledge of the other party?

**The genuinely difficult cases** are those where refusal, though formally possible, would carry consequences (disinheritance, ostracism, the sister's prospects) severe enough to raise the question of grave fear. These require real pastoral judgement, and c. 1103 exists precisely for them.

## Dowry

Prohibited by the **Dowry Prohibition Act 1961** and persistent nonetheless, including among Christians. The theological objection is direct: **dowry treats the bride as a transaction**, which contradicts the covenant of mutual self-gift that constitutes the sacrament. Several Indian episcopal conferences and dioceses have legislated against clergy witnessing dowry marriages. The pastoral difficulty is that the family, not the couple, typically drives the practice.

## Caste Endogamy

The same argument the vault has already made for baptism, the Eucharist, and orders applies here with equal force: if baptism incorporates into a body in which there is "neither slave nor free" (Gal 3:28), caste-endogamous marriage among Christians — and the matrimonial advertisements that specify it — contradicts what the earlier sacrament effected. **George Soares-Prabhu's** judgement that caste in the Church parodies the Eucharist extends naturally: marriage is precisely where caste boundaries are policed most effectively, which makes it the practical mechanism by which caste is reproduced in the Christian community generation after generation.

## Mixed Marriage and Disparity of Cult in a Plural Society

Statistically far more common in India than in most of the Catholic world, and the pastoral load is correspondingly heavier:

- **Disparity of cult** with Hindu, Muslim, or Sikh partners requires dispensation, and the promise regarding the children's baptism raises real difficulties where the other family expects otherwise.
- **Interchurch marriages** between Latin, Syro-Malabar, and Syro-Malankara Catholics raise questions of **ritual church membership** — the children's ascription to a *sui iuris* Church, and which liturgical tradition the family will practise. This is a genuinely Indian pastoral question with little Western literature.
- **Civil law**: the **Indian Christian Marriage Act 1872** governs the solemnisation of Christian marriages, and the **Indian Divorce Act 1869**, substantially amended in **2001**, governs dissolution — the amendment having removed the grossly discriminatory requirement that a wife prove adultery *plus* an additional ground. A pastor must know that a civil decree has no effect on the canonical bond.

## Marriage Preparation

The FABC has consistently emphasised family ministry as central to the Asian Church's mission, and **Amaladoss's** rule on inculturation applies: preparation programmes transplanted from Europe address a companionate-marriage model that may not describe the couple's actual situation. Effective preparation in this context engages the **families**, not only the couple.
