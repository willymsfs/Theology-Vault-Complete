# Ch. 30 — Contextual Theology in East Asia
*Chapter author: Archie Chi Chung Lee*

## Core Idea
East Asian theology develops a "cross-textual hermeneutics," reading East Asian cultural, literary, and religious texts alongside the Bible as equally active sources, rejecting Western binary and "Germanic captive" modes of thought in favour of frameworks rooted in Confucian, Buddhist, and shamanistic heritage.

## Detailed Explanation
Formed through severe twentieth-century crises — the Opium Wars' humiliation, Japanese colonization of Korea (1910-45) and Taiwan (1895-1945), Korea's division, China's 1949 communist takeover and Cultural Revolution (1966-76), and Hong Kong's 1997 handover — East Asian theology channels historical trauma directly into theological method. Korean minjung ("the people") theology centres the collective, unjust suffering (han) of history's exploited masses, while Kazoh Kitamori's Theology of the Pain of God (1946) uses the Japanese Buddhist-influenced concept of tsurasa (redemptive pain born of love) to describe how God's love overcomes divine wrath at the cross.

## Example
Wang Weifan's "life-birthing God" (sheng sheng), drawn from the ancient Chinese Yijing (Book of Change), pictures God not as a static, distant substance but as an ever-generating life-force continuously giving birth to, sustaining, and protecting creation — offering Chinese Christians a genuinely indigenous doctrine of God rather than a translated Western one.

## Illustration
Cross-textual hermeneutics works like a bilingual conversation between two equally fluent speakers rather than a translator quietly converting everything into just one language. The Bible speaks, and the Asian cultural text (a Confucian classic, a shamanistic ritual, the Yijing) speaks back as an equal partner — neither is simply reduced to explaining or confirming the other.

## Key Terms
- **Cross-Textual Hermeneutics**: Archie Lee's method of reading an Asian cultural/literary text and the biblical text in active, mutually critical dialogue, generating new postcolonial meaning.
- **Han** (Korean: 한/恨): Deep-seated collective grief and indignation from unjust suffering — the central category of Korean minjung theology.
- **Sheng Sheng** (Chinese: 生生) — "ever-generating / life-birthing": Wang Weifan's model of God, drawn from the Yijing, as an unceasing life-force rather than a static substance.
- **Minjung** (Korean: 민중/民衆) — "the people / the masses": The oppressed, marginalized subjects of history whose collective social biography is the locus of Korean minjung theology.
- **Tsurasa** (Japanese: つらさ/辛さ) — "pain / hardship": Kazoh Kitamori's Buddhist-influenced concept of redemptive pain born of love, explaining how God's love overcomes wrath at the cross.

## Connections
Minjung theologians reject Barth's Pauline Christ-kerygma in favour of the historical Jesus and the people's own social biography (Part I). C. S. Song and Kosuke Koyama adapt Tillich's correlation (Part I) using local "buffalo-eye" stories rather than Western philosophical categories. John B. Cobb's process theology (drawing on Mahayana Buddhist sunyata and anatman) is a key Western dialogue partner.
