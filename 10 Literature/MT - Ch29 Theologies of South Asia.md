# Ch. 29 — Theologies of South Asia
*Chapter author: Felix Wilfred*

## Core Idea
South Asian theology is a contextual, interreligious discipline privileging space (the concrete social and cultural context) over abstract linear history, developing through active "core-to-core" dialogue with Hindu, Buddhist, and Islamic traditions and through Dalit theology's confrontation with caste hegemony.

## Detailed Explanation
Christians are a small minority across South Asia's vast multi-religious landscape. The Church of South India's 1947 union of Anglican, Methodist, Congregationalist, and Presbyterian traditions offered an early ecumenical model responding to national independence movements; Sri Lanka's 1971 youth revolt briefly united Christians, Buddhists, and Marxists in common struggle; ongoing persecution (Pakistan's blasphemy laws, Bishop John Joseph's 1998 protest suicide) and the martyrdom of Michael Rodrigo for grassroots interreligious work mark the discipline's real costs. Dalit theology, developed against caste discrimination, deliberately reads the suffering of Jesus through the historical experience of India's "crushed" outcastes, rejecting theologies adapted from classical, caste-structured Hinduism.

## Example
Aloysius Pieris's "core-to-core dialogue" — going beyond comparing religious concepts to seeking an actual, mutually transforming meeting between Christian agape (self-giving love) and Buddhist gnosis (liberating wisdom) — has shaped concrete interreligious monastic and ashram communities across South and Southeast Asia that practice, not just theorize, this encounter.

## Illustration
Comparing religions at the level of doctrine alone is like comparing two rivers only by looking at maps of where they are drawn — you learn their shape but never get wet. "Core-to-core dialogue" insists on actually stepping into both rivers, letting each tradition's deepest current — Christian love, Buddhist wisdom — genuinely touch and change the other, not just be mapped side by side.

## Key Terms
- **Cosmotheandric Vision**: Raimon Panikkar's holistic framework integrating the cosmic, divine, and human dimensions of reality as radically interrelated, not separable.
- **Dalit Theology** (Sanskrit/Marathi: dalit) — "crushed / broken": A contextual theology reading the Gospel and Christ's suffering through the historical experience of caste-oppressed "outcastes," rejecting theologies borrowed from caste Hinduism.
- **Core-to-Core Dialogue**: Aloysius Pieris's method of interreligious engagement seeking mutual, symbiotic transformation between traditions, not merely conceptual comparison.
- **Dialogue of Everyday Life**: A theology of religions starting from concrete daily relationships among ordinary believers of different faiths, rather than from abstract doctrinal deduction.

## Connections
Sebastian Kappen, Samuel Rayan, and M. M. Thomas combine socialist commitment to the poor with critique of both caste hegemony and institutional Christianity. Adapts and criticises Tillich's correlation and Tracy's revised correlation (Part I, Part II.C) as too Western in scope. Jacques Dupuis adapted Rahner's transcendental openness (Part I) into a pluralistic theology of religions that provoked controversy in Rome — directly continuing the Roman Catholic Theology after Vatican II chapter's themes (Part II.D).
