# Unit 4 — The Carolingian Shift — From Healing to Dying

The most consequential transformation in the history of this sacrament, and — as with penance — it happened through reform legislation rather than doctrinal decision.

## What the Carolingian Reformers Did

The ninth-century reform, seeking uniformity and the suppression of superstition, made the anointing of the sick an exclusively **clerical** act. Bishops' capitularies (Theodulf of Orleans and others) instruct priests to perform the anointing themselves; the practice of lay and self-anointing is progressively suppressed.

Simultaneously, the anointing was incorporated into the **deathbed ordo**, the sequence of rites for the dying: **penance → anointing → viaticum**. Because canonical penance still carried lifelong disabilities (Penance course, Unit 3), being enrolled among the penitents was something one did at the very end — and the anointing, now bound to that sequence, migrated with it.

## The Name Changes

By the twelfth century the sacrament is being called ***unctio extrema*** — "**extreme** (that is, *last*) unction." Peter Lombard uses the term in the *Sentences*, and it becomes standard. The name both records and reinforces the shift: it is now explicitly the *final* anointing.

## The Consequences — A Chain of Pastoral Disasters

| Consequence | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Fear of the sacrament** | To send for the priest was to declare the patient dying; families delayed |
| **The penitential disabilities attached** | A popular and sometimes canonical belief held that one who recovered after anointing must live as though dead — abstaining from meat, from marital relations, sometimes from legal acts and bearing arms |
| **Deferral to the last possible moment** | The sacrament was often administered to the unconscious or the barely-living |
| **Loss of the healing dimension** | Recovery came to be seen almost as an anomaly rather than as something the rite prayed for |

**Illustration.** Consider the trap this created. The sacrament exists to strengthen the sick — but calling for it announces that you are dying, and surviving it may impose the obligations of a penitent for life. So you wait. And because you wait, the sacrament is only ever seen at deathbeds, which confirms everyone's belief that it is for the dying, which makes the next person wait longer. **A self-reinforcing pastoral trap, generated not by doctrine but by the accidental bundling of rites.** Untangling precisely this knot is what *Sacrosanctum Concilium* 73 sets out to do.
