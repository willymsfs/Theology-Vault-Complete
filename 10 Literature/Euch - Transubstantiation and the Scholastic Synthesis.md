# Unit 6 — Transubstantiation and the Scholastic Synthesis

## The Word and the Council

The term ***transubstantiatio*** appears in the mid-twelfth century (commonly attributed to Hildebert of Lavardin, and in wide use by the 1140s-50s), and enters conciliar language at the **Fourth Lateran Council (1215)**, c. 1 *Firmiter*: Christ's body and blood are "truly contained in the sacrament of the altar under the species of bread and wine, the bread being **transubstantiated** (*transsubstantiatis*) into the body and the wine into the blood by divine power."

Note carefully: Lateran IV uses the word but does not define the Aristotelian metaphysics. That elaboration is Aquinas's.

## Aquinas (ST III qq. 73-83)

**What transubstantiation asserts.** The whole *substance* of bread is converted into the whole substance of Christ's body, while the *accidents* (or **species**) of bread — colour, taste, texture, extension, nutritive properties — remain, now sustained miraculously without inhering in any substance.

**What it denies**, and this is where exam answers usually go wrong:

| Rejected account | Why Aquinas rejects it |
|---|---|
| **Annihilation** — the bread is destroyed and Christ's body put in its place | There is no *conversion* then, merely a substitution |
| **Consubstantiation** — the substance of bread remains alongside Christ's body | "This is my body" would then be false; the bread would still be bread |
| **Impanation** — the Word assumes the bread as it assumed flesh | Confuses hypostatic union with sacramental presence |
| **Local motion** — Christ's body travels from heaven to the altar | Christ is not present *locally*; see below |

**The mode of presence.** This is Aquinas's subtlest and most important move. Christ is present ***per modum substantiae*** — "after the manner of substance," not after the manner of quantity or place. Therefore he is not *in* the host as in a container, is not squeezed or divided when the host is broken, does not move when the host moves, and is **wholly present in every part of every fragment**. This single distinction defuses nearly every crude objection.

**Concomitance.** Because the risen Christ is indivisible, where the body is, there also by natural concomitance are the blood, soul, and divinity. Hence **the whole Christ is received under either species alone** — the doctrinal basis on which the medieval West could withdraw the chalice from the laity without (in its own terms) depriving them.

**The threefold distinction applied:**

| Level | Content in the Eucharist |
|---|---|
| *sacramentum tantum* | The species of bread and wine |
| *res et sacramentum* | The true body and blood of Christ |
| *res tantum* | The unity of the Mystical Body — the grace of ecclesial communion |

Notice that the *res tantum* is **ecclesial**. Aquinas is here entirely at one with Augustine and with 1 Cor 10:17: the ultimate effect of the Eucharist is the Church.

## Devotional and Liturgical Consequences

The high medieval period externalised the doctrine in practice:

- **The elevation** of the host after the consecration (Paris, c. 1200) — for a laity that rarely communicated, *seeing* became the dominant mode of participation ("**ocular communion**"). People ran between churches to catch elevations.
- **Corpus Christi** — instituted for the universal Church by **Urban IV**, bull *Transiturus de hoc mundo* (1264), with an office traditionally ascribed to **Aquinas**, who composed *Pange Lingua*, *Lauda Sion*, *Verbum Supernum*, *Sacris Solemniis*, and *Adoro Te Devote*. Aquinas is thus simultaneously the doctrine's greatest technician and its greatest poet — a useful corrective to the idea that scholastic precision kills devotion.
- **Lateran IV, c. 21 *Omnis utriusque sexus*** — obligatory annual confession and Easter communion, legislated precisely *because* actual reception had become so rare.
- **Withdrawal of the chalice** from the laity, general by the thirteenth century, later defended at **Constance (1415)** against the Hussites/Utraquists, who made *utraquism* — communion under both kinds — their defining demand.

## The Alternatives

**Duns Scotus** and **William of Ockham** both held that transubstantiation is not demonstrable by reason and that consubstantiation is *philosophically* more economical — but accepted transubstantiation on the authority of the Church. This is historically important: it establishes, well before Luther, that a Catholic theologian could regard the Aristotelian apparatus as *one* account rather than the only conceivable one.
