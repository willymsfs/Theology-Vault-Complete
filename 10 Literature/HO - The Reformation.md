# Unit 6 — The Reformation

## Luther: The Priesthood of All Believers

Luther's *To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation* (1520) makes the foundational move: **all baptised Christians are truly priests**, and there is no difference of *status* between clergy and laity, only of *office* (*Amt*).

> "All Christians are truly of the spiritual estate, and there is no difference among them except that of office... For baptism, gospel and faith alone make [us] spiritual and a Christian people."

Consequences:
- **Ordination is not a sacrament** but the public calling and commissioning of a man to exercise, on behalf of the community, an office that belongs to the whole priestly people.
- **No indelible character**, no *ontological* change. A minister removed from office returns to being a layman — Luther says so explicitly.
- **The sacrificing priesthood is rejected outright**, on Hebrews' *ephapax*. If there is no sacrifice of the Mass, there is no need for a sacrificing priest.
- What remains, and what Luther holds very high, is the **ministry of Word and sacrament**, divinely instituted and necessary — he is not a congregationalist and insists no one may exercise it without a proper call.

## Calvin: Four Offices

*Institutes* IV.3 sets out **pastors, teachers, elders (presbyters), and deacons** as the permanent offices, derived from Ephesians 4:11. Ordination by laying on of hands is retained and valued — Calvin calls it useful and apostolic — but not counted a sacrament. Election by the community and examination of doctrine and life are essential; the *presbyterial* rather than *episcopal* structure follows.

## The Anglican Settlement

The **Ordinal of 1550** (revised 1552) retained the **threefold ministry** of bishops, priests, and deacons, and its preface asserts that these orders have existed "from the Apostles' time." But the Edwardine rite removed the *traditio instrumentorum* and much of the sacrificial language — which is exactly what Rome would later fasten on in *Apostolicae Curae* (Unit 12).

## The Radical Reformation

Rejected ordained ministry as an order altogether, in favour of congregational election, lay preaching, and — in some groups — the immediate leading of the Spirit.

## The Real Question Underneath

Every Reformation dispute about orders reduces to one question: **is ministry constitutive or functional?** Does ordination make a man *something* he was not (an ontological change, a character, a capacity to act *in persona Christi*), or does it *authorise* him to do something the community could in principle authorise otherwise? Catholics and Orthodox say the former; classical Protestantism says the latter. Everything else — celibacy, apostolic succession, the validity of Anglican orders, women's ordination — is downstream of this.
