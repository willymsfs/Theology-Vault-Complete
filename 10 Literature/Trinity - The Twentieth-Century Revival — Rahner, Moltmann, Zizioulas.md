# Part 6 — The Twentieth-Century Revival — Rahner, Moltmann, Zizioulas

## Karl Rahner's Grundaxiom

**Rahner's Rule:** *"The economic Trinity is the immanent Trinity and the immanent Trinity is the economic Trinity."* God's historical self-communication in Christ and the Spirit simply *is* God's eternal inner life, not a mere external manifestation of a separately existing "hidden" inner life. Rahner's target was the classic manualist ordering of *De Deo Uno* strictly prior to and separate from *De Deo Trino*, which he judged had reduced the Trinity to a practically irrelevant "museum piece" of dogmatics.

**Dennis Jowers's critique:** (1) if God is classically simple, God cannot "become" anything in history without the immanent relations themselves undergoing real change, undermining the very identity-claim the axiom asserts; (2) the biblical anointing of Christ at the Jordan follows an economic Father→Spirit→Son pattern, reversing the standard Western immanent order of Father→Son→Spirit — if economic strictly equals immanent without qualification, this either forces an unorthodox conclusion or falsifies the axiom's inferential power.

**Legacy:** the shared premise nearly every subsequent revival either builds upon (Moltmann, LaCugna) or explicitly reacts against (Emery — Part 7).

## Jürgen Moltmann's Social Trinity

*The Trinity and the Kingdom of God* (1980) deliberately reverses the traditional Western procedure, beginning "with the trinity of the Persons" rather than divine oneness — unity is constituted in and through **perichoresis** (mutual indwelling), not a prior substance underlying the three persons. Politically: monarchical, single-subject models of God have historically legitimated hierarchical, authoritarian structures; Moltmann proposes the co-equal, non-hierarchical communion of the divine persons as the ground for "the democratic community of free people" and a "fellowship church."

**Joy Ann McDougall's critique** (*Pilgrimage of Love*): three "acting in concert" divine subjects risk becoming three gods loosely allied rather than one God (tritheism); Moltmann covertly needs something like the Father's monarchical priority to secure genuine unity after all, in tension with his own egalitarian aims. **Karen Kilby's** sharper charge: a **"vicious hermeneutical circle"** — projecting preferred political ideals onto God, then reversing the analogy to claim divine warrant for them (fully developed in Part 9).

## John Zizioulas's Relational Ontology

*Being as Communion*: *"being means life, and life means communion"* — personhood, not impersonal substance, is the true location of being. The Cappadocians, properly read, effected an "ontological revolution" by grounding divine being in the Father's free, personal will to exist as Father, rather than impersonal metaphysical necessity. The Father remains sole *aitia* (cause) of Son and Spirit — a strict monarchy of origin; personhood is ***ekstasis***, an event of freedom realized only in communion, never a self-enclosed individual substance — grounded concretely in Eucharistic ecclesial experience.

**Miroslav Volf's critique** (developed in Part 7): the Father-many causal relation is structurally asymmetrical; projected into Zizioulas's eucharistic ecclesiology, the bishop as monarchical "one" constitutes the "many" of the congregation, leaving the laity in what Volf calls "partially coerced subordination."

## The Three-Way Mutual Critique

Moltmann accuses Rahner of "Idealist modalism" — a single divine subject cannot sustain real interpersonal love within itself. Zizioulas accuses Rahner and the wider Western tradition of prioritizing impersonal substance over person, inverting the Cappadocians' true person-first revolution. Moltmann and Zizioulas — both drawing on the Cappadocians, both "relational" theologians — split irreconcilably over whether the Father's monarchy is essential: Zizioulas says yes, building a hierarchical, episcopally-centered ecclesiology on it; Moltmann says no, regarding the monarchy of the Father as itself the root of illegitimate domination. Each position strains under the other's critique: Moltmann appears covertly to need the Father's monarchy after all (McDougall); Zizioulas's causal asymmetry leaves the equal dignity of Son and Spirit in unresolved tension (Volf).
