# Part 5 — The Filioque Controversy and the Council of Florence

## Origins and the Path to Schism

The ***filioque*** ("and from the Son") is the Western addition to the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed's article on the Holy Spirit. First introduced regionally in Visigothic Spain (Third Council of Toledo, 589) and in Gaul, originally to combat lingering Arianizing tendencies by stressing the Son's full consubstantiality and equal causal dignity with the Father, it was aggressively promoted by Charlemagne for Frankish theological-political reasons, and formally incorporated into the Roman liturgy itself only under **Pope Benedict VIII** in the early 11th century. This unilateral Western addition, made without an ecumenical council, is a major structural catalyst of the Great Schism of 1054.

## The Theological Grammar of the Divide

**East (monopatrism):** the Father alone is *archē* (principle) and *aitia* (cause) of the other two persons; the *filioque* introduces an illegitimate "diarchy" into the Godhead, compromises the Father's monarchy, and depersonalizes the Spirit by subordinating the Spirit's origin to the Father-Son relationship. The Spirit may proceed "through the Son" (*dia tou Huiou*) in the economy of salvation, but eternal hypostatic procession (*ekporeusis*) is from the Father alone.

**West (double procession):** Father and Son together spirate the Holy Spirit "as from one principle" (***tamquam ex uno principio***) and by a single act of spiration; appeals to John 16:15 ("All that the Father has is mine") to argue the Father eternally communicates to the Son the very power to spirate the Spirit. Without the *filioque*, the West argues, nothing distinguishes the Son's and Spirit's processions from one another.

## The Council of Florence (1438-39) — per Ostroumov's History

Byzantine Constantinople faced existential threat from the advancing Ottoman Empire; Emperor John VIII Palaiologos sought Western military aid, accepting a unionizing council with Pope Eugenius IV as the price. The council opened in Ferrara (1438) and transferred to Florence (1439) — officially due to plague, but Ostroumov notes a decisive practical factor: the Greek delegation, running out of money, agreed to relocate when the Pope promised to pay their overdue stipends.

**Ferrara phase:** **Mark of Ephesus (Eugenicus)**, leader of the unyielding Orthodox faction, insisted the prior question was *legality*, not doctrine — on 16 October 1438 he had the acts of the early Ecumenical Councils read aloud, citing Canon VII of the Council of Ephesus (431), forbidding composition or use of "another faith" (creed) besides the Nicene. The Latins, led by **Cardinal Giuliano Cesarini**, countered that the *filioque* was not an illegitimate "addition" (*additio*) but a legitimate doctrinal "explanation" (*explicatio*) of existing consubstantiality.

**Florence phase:** debate shifted, at Latin insistence, to the doctrinal question itself. Principal disputants: **John of Montenegro** (Dominican) for the Latins, Mark of Ephesus for the Greeks. John of Montenegro argued Greek *dia tou Huiou* and Latin *ex Patre Filioque* are equivalent in meaning, since Greek and Latin Fathers, both divinely inspired, cannot really contradict one another. Mark of Ephesus insisted "through the Son" names only the Spirit's economic manifestation, not eternal hypostatic origin, and accused the Latins of relying on corrupted or forged patristic texts.

## The Compromise and Its Collapse

Moderates **Bessarion of Nicea** and **Isidore of Kiev** brokered a middle path: the Spirit proceeds eternally from Father and Son "as from one principle and by a single spiration"; Greek "through the Son" is declared equivalent to Latin "and the Son"; Latin azymes and Greek leavened bread both valid Eucharistic matter; papal universal primacy affirmed, with the ambiguous saving clause "saving the rights and privileges of the Eastern Patriarchs." The Decree of Union, ***Laetentur Caeli*** ("Let the Heavens Rejoice"), was signed 5 July 1439 and publicly promulgated the next day.

**Mark of Ephesus alone among the Greek bishops present refused to sign.** On hearing this, Eugenius IV reportedly exclaimed, *"Therefore, we have done nothing!"* — a premonition that proved exactly correct. Total repudiation followed almost immediately: fierce popular and clerical rejection in Constantinople (Mark hailed as a national hero); the three Eastern Patriarchs (Jerusalem, Alexandria, Antioch) formally condemned the union as "vile" at Jerusalem in April 1443, deposing the pro-union Patriarch Metrophanes II; Metropolitan Isidore of Kiev, proclaiming the union in Moscow (1441), was arrested as a heretic, triggering the Russian Church's declaration of autocephaly from Constantinople; Constantinople fell to the Ottomans on 29 May 1453 regardless of the promised Western aid, and the Council of Constantinople formally annulled the Union of Florence outright in 1484.
