# 20. the church as a woman or women being church.pdf

**TITLE**: "THE CHURCH AS A WOMAN OR WOMEN BEING CHURCH? ECCLESIOLOGY AND THEOLOGICAL ANTHROPOLOGY IN FEMINIST DIALOGUE." By Natalie Knödel. Published in *Theology & Sexuality*, Vol. 7, 1997, pages 103–119.

**MAIN SUBJECT**: A critical deconstruction of the Roman Catholic "nuptial mystery" and "gender complementarity" ecclesiologies (associated with Pope John Paul II, Hans Urs von Balthasar, and Monica Migliorino Miller), proposing a feminist reconstruction of the Church as a sacramental space for bodily, sexuate subjects.

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   The papal pronouncements on the dignity of women (*Mulieris Dignitatem*) as tools for retaining patriarchal power [56].
*   The theology of "sex complementarity" (Prudence Allen) and its conversion into a refined form of sex polarity [57].
*   Monica Migliorino Miller’s *Sexuality and Authority in the Catholic Church* and the "nuptial mystery" [58, 59].
*   The Eucharist as the sublimation of disembodied heterosexual intercourse [60].
*   Deconstructing the "nuptial mystery" as an ontological analogy vs. a flexible metaphor [61, 62].
*   The exclusion of women from the ministerial priesthood based on biological essentialism [59, 63].
*   The "exaltation of disembodied female sexuality" and "parenthood without pregnancy" [64, 65].
*   Feminist poststructuralist anthropology (Luce Irigaray, Julia Kristeva, Rosi Braidotti, Judith Butler) applied to ecclesiology [66].
*   Incarnational Christology as the divine affirmation of (sexual) particularity [67].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Sex Complementarity**: A philosophical concept (articulated by Prudence Allen) describing the relationship between male and female as a positive dynamic of equals in interaction; Knödel critiques its actual ecclesial application as a disguised form of patriarchal polarity [57].
2.  **Nuptial Mystery / Nuptial Theology**: A theological framework asserting that the universe is nuptially structured, where Christ is the male bridegroom and the Church is the feminine, submissive bride; this is used to justify a male-only priesthood [59, 68, 69].
3.  **Disembodied Sexuality (Sexuality Without Sex)**: The patriarchal idealization of clerical celibacy and female archetypes (like the virginal, maternal Church) which exalts "parenthood without pregnancy" and "childbirth without children," alienating real, sexuate women from their bodies [64, 65, 70].
4.  **Metaphor vs. Analogy**: Knödel proposes replacing the ontological "analogy" of the nuptial covenant with "metaphor" (suggestive, figurative speech), allowing patriarchal nuptial imagery to be replaced when other, more adequate metaphors are found [61, 62].
5.  **Strategic Essentialism**: A poststructuralist method (adapted from Braidotti and Irigaray) that temporarily advocates for "women" as a category to assert their discursive presence and subjectivity in the Church, while remaining conscious of their internal diversity [71].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Rhetorical Use of the "Feminine" Church**: Exposing how the Marian and feminine descriptions of the Church (used by Balthasar, Ratzinger, and John Paul II) are rhetorical devices to keep women in a permanent "object" position while retaining an exclusive male hierarchy [72-74].
2.  **Compulsory Heterosexuality of the Universe**: Challenging a nuptially structured theology that renders all non-heterosexual human sexualities and relationships (such as friendship and lesbian identities) deviant or non-existent [75, 76].
3.  **The Somatic Exclusion of the Female Body**: Confronting the traditional view that renders the female body ritually unclean and incapable of physically representing Christ, which separates the action *in persona Christi* from *in persona ecclesiae* [53, 60, 77].
4.  **The Sacramental Silence of Women's Eucharistic Piety**: Reclaiming the historical aspects of food, nourishment, and bodily life (the core of women's medieval eucharistic piety) which are ignored in Miller's narrow, sacrificial eucharistic theology [78].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "...the church is understood as essentially female or rather 'feminine'." (Page 103 / Passage 259) - Context: Outlining the traditional Roman Catholic gendered analogy.
*   "Sex complementarity considers the opposition of male and female a positive dynamic of equals in interaction..." (Page 106 / Passage 269) - Context: Quoting Prudence Allen's definition of complementarity.
*   "The Eucharistic sacrifice can only be valid if it is made by a male priest who is exercising and physically resembling the masculine authority of Christ." (Page 107 / Passage 271) - Context: Detailing Miller's argument for the ontological exclusion of women from priesthood.
*   "The concepts of ecclesial maternity and priestly paternity are the exaltation of parenthood without pregnancy, and childbirth without children." (Page 112 / Passage 286) - Context: Critiquing the disembodied nature of patriarchal sexual symbolism.
*   "Feminine imagery is only usable as long as it sanctions the male order of the absence or at best the subordination... of women..." (Page 114 / Passage 294) - Context: Showing how the "bride" metaphor is dropped for the "body" of Christ to keep authority male.
*   "...incarnation into particularity is an essential aspect of the narrative of redemption and enables human particularity... as an essential feature of women being church." (Page 118-119 / Passage 307-309) - Context: Proposing an incarnational Christology to validate the sexuate human body.

**SUMMARY**:
Natalie Knödel delivers a devastating critique of Roman Catholic "nuptial theology" and "sex complementarity," demonstrating that they function as rhetorical traps that exalt a disembodied, asexual "femininity" to justify the subordination and exclusion of real, living women from ecclesial authority. She deconstructs Monica Migliorino Miller’s narrow, heterosexualized view of the Eucharist as a sublimation of intercourse. Drawing on feminist poststructuralist theorists like Luce Irigaray, Julia Kristeva, and Rosi Braidotti, Knödel proposes a reconstructed, non-patriarchal ecclesiology that views the Church as a sacramental space of dialogue for bodily, sexuate subjects. Rooted in an incarnational Christology that affirms sexual particularity, and a Trinitarian Pneumatology that enables creative plurality, she asserts that women must be recognized as active subjects of enunciation without whom the Church cannot truly be the Church.
