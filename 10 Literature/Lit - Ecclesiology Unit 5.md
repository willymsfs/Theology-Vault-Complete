# Unit 5 — From Apostolic Church to Vatican I

## Early Catholicism and the Threefold Ministry
"Early Catholicism" names the process by which the charismatic, Spirit-led freedom of the first generation gradually settled into fixed offices as the expectation of Christ's imminent return receded and the apostolic generation died out. The threefold ministry — episkopos (bishop), presbyteros (elder-priest), diakonos (deacon) — is attested emerging in the Pastoral Epistles and is firmly fixed by Ignatius of Antioch (d. c. 110), who insists: "do nothing without the bishop."

## The Great Church and the Rise of Patriarchal Sees
By the fourth and fifth centuries, five major sees had become centres of authority: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem — the later pentarchy. Rome's primacy grows from its apostolic foundation (Peter and Paul), its status as imperial capital, and its record of doctrinal steadiness through the christological controversies.

## Gregorian Reform
In the eleventh century, Pope Gregory VII's Dictatus Papae (1075) asserts sweeping papal authority — the pope alone can depose bishops, all princes owe him obedience, he can depose emperors — as part of the investiture-conflict struggle against lay control of church appointments (libertas ecclesiae, the Church's "freedom" from secular domination). The Reform marks a decisive step toward the centralized, juridical papacy of the Latin Middle Ages.

## Two Ruptures: East-West and Reformation
The Great Schism (1054) split East and West over primacy, the filioque, and liturgical and cultural difference. The Reformation (sixteenth century) split Western Christianity over justification, the authority of Scripture, and the sacraments.

## Vatican I and the Four Marks of Pre-Conciliar Ecclesiology
Convened by Pius IX amid the loss of the Papal States and rising modern secularism, Vatican I (1869-70) dogmatically defined papal primacy (full, supreme, ordinary, immediate jurisdiction) and papal infallibility (under strict conditions — see Unit 12) in Pastor Aeternus. The Council was interrupted by the Franco-Prussian War and the fall of Rome before it could address the bishops or the laity, leaving its picture of the Church incomplete until Vatican II supplied the missing chapters.

The resulting pre-conciliar model carries four marks: clericalism (clergy alone as active agents; laity reduced to passive recipients), juridicism (Church life read through canon-law categories of rights, offices, jurisdiction), triumphalism (the Church pictured as an army arrayed against a hostile secular world), and patriarchalism (a wholly male-governed structure in which women have no institutional voice). Taken as a whole, this model carries real theological gains — a strong doctrinal and structural endorsement, and a powerful, visible corporate identity continuous with patristic tradition — and real losses: weak grounding directly in Scripture and earliest communal practice, and a tendency for power to control rather than serve, stifling the Church's own charismatic diversity under rigid, legalistic obedience.

## Illustration
Picture the pre-Vatican-I Church as a well-fortified medieval city: strong walls (juridical structure), a single gate controlled by the garrison (clericalism), banners proclaiming victory over every siege (triumphalism), and a council chamber to which only men are admitted (patriarchalism). The city is genuinely well-defended — but a besieged city cannot also be a hospitable, welcoming home; Vatican II will have to open its gates without demolishing its walls.
