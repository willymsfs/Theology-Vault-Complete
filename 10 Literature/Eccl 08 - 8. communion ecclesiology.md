# 8. communion ecclesiology.pdf

**TITLE**: "Communion Ecclesiology and Black Liberation Theology." By Jamie T. Phelps, O.P. (Ph.D., Catholic University of America; Associate Professor of Theology, Loyola University of Chicago / Institute for Black Catholic Studies, Xavier University of Louisiana). Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 61, 2000, pages 672–699 [22, 23].

**MAIN SUBJECT**: The theological compatibility and teleological convergence of communion ecclesiology and Black liberation theology, demonstrating how both seek the ultimate realization of an inclusive, reconciled human community ("the beloved community") by actively dismantling systemic racism, sexism, and classism [22, 24, 25].

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   The 1985 Extraordinary Synod's definition of *communio* as the central idea of Vatican II [26].
*   The historical compromise of U.S. Christian churches with white supremacist ideology and slavery [27, 28].
*   The continuity of communal values between traditional African religions and the historical Black Church [29, 30].
*   The contribution of Howard Thurman and Martin Luther King, Jr. to the concept of inclusive community [31, 32].
*   The emergence of formal Black liberation theology through James Cone [27, 33].
*   A comparison of the liberationist Christologies of James Cone and Gustavo Gutiérrez [34, 35].
*   The "collective, loud silence" of Euro-American Catholic theologians regarding race [36, 37].
*   The Lonergan-based analysis of "intellectual bias" (Shawn Copeland) and "ethical fantasy" (Paul Wadell) [38, 39].
*   James Cone’s five essential ecclesiological characteristics of the Church as the Body of Christ [40].
*   Pope John Paul II's millenarian call for ecclesial repentance, conversion, and social solidarity [41, 42].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **The Beloved Community**: Martin Luther King, Jr.’s theological and ethical metaphor for a qualitatively inclusive global community rooted in a Judeo-Christian love-ethic, where social boycott and non-cooperation with evil are directed toward reconciliation, redemption, and mutual kinship [32].
2.  **Intellectual Bias (Copeland)**: A Lonergan-derived cognitive concept describing the conscious, strategic refusal of theologians from the dominant culture to accept corrective insights, choosing instead to persist in error to sustain patriarchal and white supremacist social orders [39, 43].
3.  **Ethical Fantasy (Murdoch/Wadell)**: A chronic, self-deceptive distortion of moral vision that deliberately misreads other people and social systems because seeing them truthfully would demand personal and structural conversion [38].
4.  **James Cone's Five Ecclesial Marks**: The systematic blueprint asserting that to be the authentic Body of Christ, the Church must: (1) suffer with the suffering, (2) preach the kerygma of liberation to the oppressed, (3) actively combat oppressive political and economic structures, (4) visibly embody what it preaches, and (5) challenge both Black and White congregations to reject dehumanization [40].
5.  **Pentecost Communion**: A local parish reality (conceptualized by Richard Marzheuser) that welcomes all Catholics regardless of nation, tribe, or language, embodying the Pauline truth that "Christ is all in all" (Col 3:11) [44].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Structural Marginalization of Black Agency**: Confronting the historical refusal of U.S. Catholic seminaries and religious orders to accept Black candidates, thereby treating Black Catholics as passive objects of white missionary charity rather than active, self-determining subjects of mission [45-48].
2.  **The Silence of the Catholic Theological Academy**: Addressing the historical omission of racism and Black theology within major academic guilds (like the CTSA) and systematic journals (like *Theological Studies*) during decades of racial crisis [36, 37].
3.  **Methodological Abstraction in Moral Ethics**: paul Wadell's critique that Catholic moral theology collapses concrete Christian ethics into an abstract, unhistorical "human ethics," rendering itself blind to concrete, contextual sins like white supremacy [38].
4.  **The Precondition of Freedom for Communion**: Demonstrating that true communion is impossible without liberation, as authentic unity requires both partners to be fully free, equal, and capable of saying an uncoerced, reciprocal "yes" [49, 50].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "Commitment to communion is integrally related to commitment to liberation. Human freedom or liberation is a precondition of ethical living since persons cannot form an authentic visible community unless they are free." (Page 673-674 / Passage 131) - Context: Outlining the logical necessity of liberation before unity can occur.
*   "To be human is to belong to the whole community, and to do so involves participating in the beliefs, ceremonies, rituals and festivals of that community." (Page 677 / Passage 145, quoting John Mbiti) - Context: Highlighting the traditional African religious roots of communal value.
*   "The majority of church leadership and members remained neutral or silent about the social and moral evil of slavery as well as the subsequent segregation..." (Page 681-682 / Passage 161) - Context: Documenting the historical failure of the hierarchy to address racism as a moral sin.
*   "the silence of U.S. Catholic theologians about racism is parallel to the silence of leading German theologians and intellectuals during the Nazi atrocities..." (Page 691-692 / Passage 192) - Context: A sharp ethical critique of the academic theological guild’s neglect of Black suffering.
*   "The Church... consists of people who have been seized by the Holy Spirit and who have the determination to live as if all depends on God." (Page 692 / Passage 193, quoting James Cone) - Context: Defining the pneumatological starting point of Black liberation ecclesiology.
*   "Oneness cannot be built on lies, denial, or the pretense of reconciliation." (Page 698-699 / Passage 215) - Context: Rejecting superficial expressions of ecclesial peace that ignore past systemic injustices.

**SUMMARY**:
Jamie T. Phelps argues that communion ecclesiology and Black liberation theology are fundamentally non-opposing, mutually dependent frameworks that share the teleological goal of realizing a unified human family [22, 24, 50]. She contrasts the deep, communitarian values of traditional African religions and the prophetic "beloved community" of King and Thurman with the historical complicity, segregation, and "collective silence" of the U.S. Catholic Church and its theological academy regarding white supremacy [27, 29, 32, 37]. Drawing on Shawn Copeland's analysis of intellectual bias and James Cone's five marks of a liberationist Church, Phelps asserts that true communion requires a radical conversion: the dominant theological culture must break its silent complicity, seek forgiveness, and empower marginalized Black Catholics to act as primary, active subjects in ecclesial decision-making and theological scholarship [39, 40, 51, 52].
