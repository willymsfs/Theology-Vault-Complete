# Ch. 23 — Theology and Spirituality
*Chapter author: Mark A. McIntosh*

## Core Idea
McIntosh addresses the modern, artificial split between theology and spirituality — for most of Christian history one indissoluble unity — arguing true theology is a sharing in God's own wisdom through contemplation, realized only through lived moral and spiritual formation, not through procedural "method" that brackets out the investigator's own person.

## Detailed Explanation
British scholar Evelyn Underhill's early-twentieth-century writings first challenged William James's reduction of spiritual experience to a neutral, pre-thematic "interiority" stripped of theological content. Anselm Stolz's landmark Theology of Mysticism (Theologie der Mystik, 1937) then critiqued early-modern neoscholasticism's own separation of dogmatic theology from spiritual practice, retrieving a trinitarian dynamism in which grace is not an external scholastic cause but active participation in the Spirit's work of conforming the believer to the Son and leading them to the Father. Simone Weil's contemplative, non-possessive attentiveness and von Balthasar's Ignatian-formed "kneeling theology" (Part I) supply the movement's mature constructive expressions.

## Example
Sarah Coakley's and Rowan Williams's contemporary work — showing how doctrines like the Trinity and Christology actually took shape historically within the praying, liturgical life of the early Church, not first as abstract philosophical propositions later applied to worship — puts McIntosh's whole argument into concrete historical-theological practice.

## Illustration
Studying spirituality with modern "method" alone is like trying to understand what it feels like to fall in love purely by reading a chemistry textbook on hormones — you can gather real, accurate data while missing the actual thing entirely, because the phenomenon only fully discloses itself to someone personally involved in it, not to a detached outside observer.

## Key Terms
- **Contemplatio** (Latin) — "contemplation": A sharing in God's own wisdom, realized only within an intentional apprenticeship of moral and spiritual formation, not by detached observation.
- **Formatio** (Latin) — "formation": The ancient/medieval pedagogical practice of exposing the whole of human existence to God, contrasted with modern "method," which brackets out the investigator's own subjectivity.
- **Theologia Mystica** (Latin) — "mystical theology": The classical, premodern integration of prayer, liturgy, and doctrine as one single existential knowledge taught directly by God.
- **Trinitarian Dynamism**: Stolz's model of grace as active participation in the unfolding trinitarian life, rather than an externally applied scholastic cause.

## Connections
Takes von Balthasar's Ignatian-formed "kneeling theology" (Part I) as its primary modern exemplar. Henri de Lubac (Part I) is a fellow nouvelle théologie figure retrieving patristic resources against neoscholastic nature-grace dualism. William James's Varieties of Religious Experience serves as the chapter's negative contrast-case.
