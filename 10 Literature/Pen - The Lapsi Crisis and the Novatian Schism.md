# Unit 4 — The Lapsi Crisis and the Novatian Schism

The single most important test case for the Church's power to forgive, and a standing example of how doctrine gets clarified under pressure.

## The Decian Persecution (250-251)

The Emperor Decius required all citizens to sacrifice to the gods and obtain a certificate (*libellus*) proving they had done so. The scale of Christian compliance was catastrophic and unexpected — in Carthage, Cyprian reports, the crowds rushed to comply.

**The categories of the fallen (*lapsi*) mattered enormously** for how they were later treated:

| Category | What they did |
|---|---|
| ***Sacrificati*** | Actually offered sacrifice — the gravest |
| ***Thurificati*** | Burned incense before the idols |
| ***Libellatici*** | Did not sacrifice but **obtained a certificate**, usually by bribery, falsely stating they had |
| ***Acta facientes*** | Had a false declaration entered in the public records |

## The Crisis of the Libelli Pacis

**Confessors** — those who had suffered for the faith and survived — began issuing ***libelli pacis***, "certificates of peace," to the lapsed, on the strength of their own merits, demanding their immediate readmission. Some were issued in bulk and blank.

This posed the real question sharply: **is reconciliation a charism of the heroic, or an act of the Church's ordained ministry?**

## Cyprian's Resolution: De Lapsis (251)

**Cyprian of Carthage** navigated between two extremes:

- **Against the confessors' laxity** — the *libelli pacis* cannot bypass the bishop; reconciliation belongs to the ordinary ministry of the Church, not to private spiritual authority however heroically earned.
- **Against rigorism** — the lapsed *can* be forgiven after appropriate penance, graded by the gravity of the lapse (the *libellatici* more leniently than the *sacrificati*).
- **The practical settlement**, reached at the Council of Carthage (251) and confirmed subsequently: penance proportioned to the offence, with reconciliation available — and, when a new persecution threatened under Gallus, reconciliation accelerated so that the penitents could face it fortified by the Eucharist.

## The Novatian Schism

**Novatian**, a Roman presbyter and able theologian (his *De Trinitate* is substantial), took the rigorist line: the Church has **no power** to reconcile the lapsed, who must be left to God's mercy. When Cornelius, a moderate, was elected Bishop of Rome in 251, Novatian was set up as a rival — the first significant **antipope** — and the schism persisted for centuries.

**Why this matters doctrinally.** The Church's rejection of Novatianism established three principles that the whole later sacrament presupposes:

1. **No sin is beyond the Church's power to forgive.** This is the direct ancestor of the Catechism's flat statement that "there is no offence, however serious, that the Church cannot forgive" (*CCC* 982).
2. **Reconciliation is ministerial**, exercised through the ordained, not charismatic or self-authorising.
3. **The Church is a *corpus permixtum*** — a mixed body of saints and sinners — not a society of the pure. This is the same argument Augustine would deploy against the Donatists over baptism a century and a half later, and the two controversies should be studied together.

**Teaching point.** Novatianism and Donatism are the *same instinct* applied to two different sacraments: the demand that the Church be visibly pure, met in both cases by the Church's insistence that holiness is Christ's gift rather than the members' achievement.
