# Unit 2 — Biblical Foundations II — Jesus and Paul

## Jesus on Divorce

The most-attested teaching of Jesus on any ethical question, appearing in **four independent strands**: Mark 10:2-12, Matthew 19:3-9 (and 5:31-32), Luke 16:18, and **1 Corinthians 7:10-11**, where Paul explicitly attributes it to the Lord rather than to himself.

**The structure of the argument in Mark 10 is what matters most.** Asked whether divorce is lawful, Jesus does not adjudicate between Shammai and Hillel. He goes **behind Moses to creation**: "**from the beginning of creation** 'God made them male and female'... 'the two shall become one flesh'... What therefore God has joined together, let no one separate." Moses' permission was "**for your hardness of heart**" (*sklērokardia*) — a concession, not the divine intention.

**This move — appealing behind the concession to the order of creation — is the foundation of the doctrine of indissolubility.**

Mark adds a detail absent from Jewish law: "if **she** divorces her husband and marries another, she commits adultery" (10:12) — presupposing a Gentile legal context where women could initiate divorce, and establishing the **symmetry** of the obligation.

## The Matthean Exception Clause

**Matthew 5:32 and 19:9** add ***parektos logou porneias*** / ***mē epi porneia*** — "except for *porneia*." This is one of the genuine cruxes of New Testament exegesis, and you should be able to state the main readings:

| Reading | Content |
|---|---|
| **Unlawful union** | *Porneia* means marriage within the forbidden degrees (Lev 18) — such unions were never valid, so this is a *nullity* clause, not a divorce clause. Favoured in Catholic exegesis, and consistent with Acts 15:20's use of *porneia* |
| **Adultery** | *Porneia* means marital infidelity, permitting separation — the traditional Orthodox and Protestant reading, grounding the possibility of remarriage |
| **Separation without remarriage** | The exception permits *separation from bed and board* but not a new marriage — the Western Catholic canonical position |
| **Betrothal infidelity** | Refers to unfaithfulness during the Jewish betrothal period, as in Matthew's own infancy narrative (1:19) |

**Honest assessment for essays:** the Catholic tradition's position is *coherent* and defensible, but the exception clause is a real difficulty, not an invention of opponents, and Matthew's community evidently found some qualification necessary.

## Paul: 1 Corinthians 7

Paul's own contribution, distinguished carefully from the Lord's command:

- **Celibacy is preferred** in view of the "impending distress" and undivided devotion (7:26, 32-35) — but marriage is unambiguously good and not sin (7:28).
- **Mutual conjugal rights** (7:3-4) stated with striking reciprocity: "the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does; **likewise** the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does." In its context this is a remarkable statement of mutuality.
- **The Pauline privilege** (7:12-16): if an unbelieving spouse refuses to remain with a believer, "the brother or sister is not bound." This is Paul's *own* ruling, not the Lord's, and it becomes the canonical basis for dissolving a non-sacramental marriage in favour of the faith (Unit 12).

## Ephesians 5:21-33 — The Sacramental Text

The passage on which the entire sacramental theology rests:

> "Husbands, love your wives, **as Christ loved the Church** and gave himself up for her... 'For this reason a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh.' **This is a great mystery** (*to mystērion touto mega estin*), and I am applying it to Christ and the Church."

Three observations:

1. The Vulgate renders *mystērion* as ***sacramentum*** — the linguistic bridge on which the Western tradition built, and the reason this text is cited in every magisterial document on marriage.
2. The passage is framed by **v. 21**: "**be subject to one another** out of reverence for Christ." The mutual subjection governs everything that follows, and omitting it inverts the passage's meaning.
3. The husband's charge is not authority but ***self-gift unto death*** — "as Christ loved the Church and **gave himself up** for her." The analogy makes headship costly, not privileged.

**John 2:1-11 (Cana)** is traditionally cited as Christ's blessing of marriage by his presence, though the text itself makes no statement about marriage — a point worth conceding in an essay before making the traditional argument.
