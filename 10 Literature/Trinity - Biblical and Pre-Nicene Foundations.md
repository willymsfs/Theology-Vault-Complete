# Part 1 — Biblical and Pre-Nicene Foundations

## Old Testament: Hints of Plurality within Strict Monotheism

Israel's uncompromising monotheism — the **Shema** (Deut 6:4, "Hear, O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one") — is the fixed starting point for every later development; the Old Testament contains no explicit doctrine of the Trinity, but it does carry real "hints" of plurality within the one God: the plurals of Genesis (1:26, 3:22, 11:7), Isaiah 6:3's threefold *Sanctus*, theophanies (the Angel of YHWH, the three visitors at Mamre in Genesis 18), and royal psalms (Ps 2:7, 110:1) later read christologically.

## Second Temple Judaism: Hypostatization of Wisdom, Word, and Spirit

Well before Christianity, Second Temple Judaism had already **hypostatized** divine Wisdom (*Chokhmah*/*Sophia*, Prov 8:22-31, Sirach 24, Wisdom of Solomon), Word (*Dabar*, rendered *Memra* in the Aramaic targums), and Spirit (*Ruach*) as God's own extended agencies acting in the world — without treating them as separate created intermediaries. This furnished the conceptual scaffolding the earliest Christians would use for Christ and the Spirit.

## Richard Bauckham's "Divine Identity" Christology

Bauckham argues the earliest Christians included Jesus within YHWH's unique **divine identity** — not as a demigod or created intermediary — by attributing to him the unique divine prerogatives of creation, sovereign rule over all things, and the reception of worship. This let the New Testament affirm Christ's full deity while still standing inside strict Jewish monotheism, without yet possessing the later ontological vocabulary (*ousia*, *hypostasis*) to explain how.

## New Testament Triadic Material

The New Testament is thick with triadic material but theologically undeveloped: the baptismal formula (Matt 28:19 — note the *singular* "name" shared by three), the apostolic benediction (2 Cor 13:14), and Christological high points (John 1:1, Phil 2:5-11, Col 1:15-20, Heb 1:1-3). These texts raise the theological question urgently but do not yet supply the technical vocabulary to answer it.

## The Hellenistic Bridge: Logos

Christian theologians translated the biblical material into Greek philosophical categories through the term **Logos**, drawing on Stoic (*logos endiathetos* — inward thought — versus *logos prophorikos* — outward, uttered speech) and Middle Platonic (Logos as mediating *Nous* between the transcendent God and the world) resources. **Philo of Alexandria** had already synthesized Hebrew Wisdom/Word with the Platonic-Stoic Logos; his grammatical distinction between *ho theos* (God, with the definite article) and *theos* (a god-quality being, without the article) directly illuminates John 1:1's *theos ēn ho logos* ("the Word was God/divine").

## The Apologists

**Justin Martyr** was the first to insist the Logos is a "**Second God**" (*deuteros theos*), numerically distinct from the Father — guarding against modalism (the idea that Father, Son, and Spirit are merely successive masks of one undifferentiated God). He explained generation without division or diminishment through the images of kindling one fire from another, of speech, and of sunlight and its rays.

**Theophilus of Antioch** coined ***Trias*** and applied the Stoic inward/outward Word distinction — *Logos Endiathetos* / *Logos Prophorikos* — to Christ's eternal generation within God and his temporal "utterance" for the work of creation.

## Tertullian: The Latin West's Permanent Vocabulary

Writing in Carthage (c. 200-212) against the modalist **Praxeas** (who held the Father himself suffered on the cross — *patripassianism*), Tertullian coined ***Trinitas*** and formulated the enduring Western formula: ***una substantia, tres personae*** — one substance, three persons. His *persona* drew on the theatrical mask and the legal role of a party having standing before the law, and was defended through **prosopological exegesis** — reading Old Testament dialogue passages (e.g., Ps 110:1, "The LORD said to my Lord") as evidence of distinct divine speakers.

His *substantia* was still materially conceived (in his peculiar Stoic-influenced view, *Deus corpus est* — "God is a body"), and his scheme remained mildly subordinationist: the Father as "whole substance," the Son "a derivation and portion" of it, and the Spirit "from the Father *through* the Son" — the earliest formal integration of the Spirit into Western trinitarian logic, and crucial background to the later *filioque* controversy (Part 5).
