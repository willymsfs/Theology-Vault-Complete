# Unit 7 — Trent and the Seminary Reform

## Session XXIII (15 July 1563) — Doctrine on the Sacrament of Order

Trent's teaching, in four chapters and eight canons:

| Point | Teaching |
|---|---|
| **A sacrifice requires a priesthood** | Since the New Law has a visible sacrifice (the Eucharist), it must have a **visible and external priesthood**, into which the old was translated |
| **Institution by Christ** | At the Last Supper, in the words "Do this in remembrance of me," Christ instituted the priesthood and the power to consecrate and to forgive sins |
| **Order is a true sacrament** | Instituted by Christ, conferring grace by the imposition of hands and the words (canon 3) |
| **Character** | Confers an **indelible character**; one ordained cannot become a layman again (canon 4) |
| **Hierarchy by divine ordinance** | There is in the Church a hierarchy "instituted by divine ordination, consisting of bishops, priests, and ministers" (canon 6) |
| **Bishops superior to priests** | Bishops are superior to priests, and have the power to confirm and ordain (canon 7) |
| **Against the priesthood of all as sufficient** | Canon 1 condemns the view that there is no visible and external priesthood in the New Testament |

## What Trent Deliberately Left Open

Two questions were fought over and **not resolved**, and knowing this is worth marks:

1. **Is the episcopate a distinct sacramental order, or a dignity added to the priesthood?** Trent says bishops are superior and can ordain, but does not define the episcopate as a distinct degree of the sacrament. **Resolved only at Vatican II** (*LG* 21).
2. **Does episcopal jurisdiction come directly from God, or through the pope?** The Spanish and French bishops pressed hard for the former (which would ground residence obligations in divine law); the Curial party for the latter. **Trent left it undecided**, and it resurfaced at Vatican I and Vatican II as the collegiality question.

## The Seminary Decree — Trent's Most Consequential Act

**Session XXIII, chapter 18** (*Cum adolescentium aetas*) required **every diocese to establish a seminary** for the formation of candidates from adolescence: a residential college with instruction in grammar, chant, computation, Scripture, homiletics, and the administration of the sacraments, funded by a levy on benefices.

**Why this matters more than the doctrinal canons.** Before Trent, most clergy were trained by apprenticeship, if at all, and clerical ignorance was the era's most visible scandal. The seminary system created — for the first time — a **uniformly educated clergy**, and it shaped Catholic priesthood more decisively over the next four centuries than any doctrinal definition. It also created the characteristic modern problems: a clergy formed in enclosure, separated from the laity from adolescence, with a distinct culture — which is precisely what *Optatam Totius* and later *Pastores Dabo Vobis* would try to reform, and what current critiques of **clericalism** target.
