# Unit 8 — The Medieval Settlement of Confirmation

Given that Confirmation is now visibly separate, the scholastic question becomes: *is it a distinct sacrament, and what does it add to baptism?*

**Aquinas, *ST* III q.72** gives the classic answer, using an analogy from natural life: Baptism is **spiritual regeneration** (*generatio spiritualis*) — birth into life. Confirmation is **spiritual growth to maturity** (*augmentum*, *robur*) — the coming of age.

| | Baptism | Confirmation |
|---|---|---|
| **Analogy** | Birth | Coming of age / enlistment |
| **Effect** | New life, remission of sin | *Robur* — strength for public witness and combat |
| **Matter** | Water | **Chrism** (olive oil plus balsam), consecrated by the bishop |
| **Form** (medieval Latin) | "I baptise you..." | *"Signo te signo crucis, et confirmo te chrismate salutis..."* |
| **Minister** | Anyone in necessity | **The bishop** (ordinary); a presbyter only by faculty/delegation |
| **Character** | Yes | **Yes** — hence also unrepeatable |
| **Necessity** | Necessary for salvation | **Not** necessary for salvation, but not to be despised |

**Council of Florence, Decree for the Armenians (1439)** codifies exactly this: matter = chrism of olive oil (signifying conscience's brightness) and balsam (the odour of good reputation); form = the signing formula; ordinary minister = the bishop; effect = the Holy Spirit given for strength, "as he was given to the apostles on the day of Pentecost, so that the Christian may boldly confess the name of Christ."

**The drift of age.** Originally infancy (as it still is in the East). Lateran IV-era legislation and later synods pushed for administration by about age 7 (the *aetas discretionis*), but bishops' visitation schedules meant the real age crept steadily upward — a drift that continues to this day.
