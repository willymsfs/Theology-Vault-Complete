# 5. church as structure and communitas.pdf

**TITLE**: Church as Structure and Communitas: Victor Turner and Ecclesiology. By Carl F. Starkloff, S.J. (Professor of Theology, Regis College, Toronto School of Theology) [120]. Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 58, 1997, pages 643–668 [121, 122].

**MAIN SUBJECT**: The application of anthropologist Victor Turner’s concepts of "structure" and "communitas" to Christian ecclesiology, examining how the dynamic tension between stable institutional order and marginalized, liminal religious experiences can foster ecclesial reform, inculturation, and a deeper communion (*koinonia*) [121, 123, 124].

**KEY TOPICS Covered**:
*   The dialogue between the social sciences and theology (Möhler, Dulles, Boff, Geertz) [336–338, 340, 342].
*   Victor Turner's ethnographic fieldwork on Ndembu rituals (rites of passage, circumcision/*Mukanda*, and women's fertility/*Isoma* and *Wubwang'a*) [125-127].
*   The fundamental categories of Structure, Communitas, Anti-Structure, and Liminality [355–360].
*   The tripartite division of Communitas: Spontaneous (existential), Normative, and Ideological [128, 129].
*   Historical applications to religious orders, specifically the Franciscan movement [370–372].
*   The anthropological and spiritual analysis of pilgrimages (Our Lady of Guadalupe, Lough Derg) [130-132].
*   Contemporary liminal groups: New Tribal Religious Movements, Basic Ecclesial Communities, and "Women-Church" [133].
*   Jean-Marie Tillard’s "Ecclesiology of Communion" and a "Church of Churches" [134].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Structure**: The patterned arrangements of roles, status sequences, consciously recognized status sets, and legal-practical norms regularly operative in a given society, representing stability and social order [135].
2.  **Communitas**: A relational quality of spontaneous, full, and unmediated communication (or communion) between definite and determinate identities, serving as the source (*fons et origo*) and critical corrector of all social structures [136].
3.  **Liminality**: The middle, transition state in a "rite of passage" characterized by secular powerlessness and sacred power, representing a realm of pure possibility, primitive hypothesis, and symbolic creativity [137, 138].
4.  **Liminoid**: A voluntary, individual form of liminality occurring in historical or salvation religions (such as pilgrimages) where individuals seek release from the sins of the structural world, as opposed to mandatory, group-focused tribal rites of passage [130].
5.  **Flow**: A state of total involvement (borrowed from Csikszentmihalyi) where participants lose their focus on the ego, lose track of time, and experience a deep sense of wholeness and unmediated communion [139].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Risk of the Church Freezing into Static Structures**: Preventing institutional rigidification and spiritual "ossification" by remaining open to dynamic, creative corrections from the margins [122, 140].
2.  **The Historical Tension Between Charismatic Origins and Institutional Organization**: Analyzing how the spontaneous, radical poverty of St. Francis of Assisi's early circle was forced to transition into a "normative communitas" under Brother Elias to survive historically [141, 142].
3.  **The Problem of "Theological Messiness" in Inculturation**: Managing the tension when marginalized tribal or feminist groups incorporate native or non-traditional symbols into Christian worship, which mainstream structures often label as "syncretism" or "heterodoxy" [143-145].
4.  **The Loss of Meaningful Rites of Passage in Modern, Secular Society**: Addressing how modern churches struggle to practice effective, spiritually transforming life-crisis and initiation rituals because of a fragmented sense of community [146, 147].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "society is always a dynamic process, consisting of two mutually interdependent and interactive poles that he came to call 'structure' and 'communitas.'" (Page 645 / Passage 342) - Context: Outlining Victor Turner's central anthropological insight.
*   "Liminality may perhaps be regarded as the way to all positive structural assertions, but as in some sense the source of them all, and more than that, as a realm of pure possibility..." (Page 652 / Passage 366) - Context: Quoting Turner on the creative necessity of the liminal transition state.
*   "Structureless communitas can bind and bond people together only momentarily." (Page 654 / Passage 373) - Context: Highlighting the inevitable historical necessity for organization and structure.
*   "The ever-pressing question to be put to Christians, especially Catholics with their highly structured system, would be: What does the Spirit wish to achieve through this tensive unity between communitas and structure?" (Page 658 / Passage 387) - Context: Framing the core theological question of the essay.
*   "As communitas goes, so goes the Church." (Page 657 / Passage 383) - Context: Rephrasing the traditional Catholic maxim "As Mary goes, so goes the Church" to emphasize the vital role of the margins.

**SUMMARY**:
Starkloff argues that Victor Turner’s anthropological concepts of structure, communitas, and liminality provide an invaluable interpretive lens for contemporary ecclesiology, particularly in balancing institutional continuity with dynamic, creative change [121, 123, 124]. Exploring the history of the Franciscan movement, the phenomenon of pilgrimages, and modern marginalized movements (such as African Independent Churches, Latin American basic communities, and Women-Church), he demonstrates that a healthy Church must exist as a constant, tensive dialectic between structure and communitas [130, 133, 141, 148]. While structure provides the necessary historical organization to prevent sectarian dissolution, spontaneous liminal experiences on the margins serve as the vital source of spiritual renewal, liturgical creativity, and authentic inculturation [149, 150]. Starkloff concludes that by embracing these "processual dynamics," the Church can realize Jean-Marie Tillard’s vision of a "Church of churches" and deepen its overall *koinonia* [134, 151].
