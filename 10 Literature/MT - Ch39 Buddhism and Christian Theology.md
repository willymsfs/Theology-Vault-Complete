# Ch. 39 — Buddhism and Christian Theology
*Chapter author: Paul O. Ingram*

## Core Idea
Ingram traces Christian-Buddhist engagement from competing claims to salvation toward "mutual creative transformation," in which conceptual, socially engaged, and contemplative "interior" dialogue reshape Christian understandings of selfhood, ultimate reality, and even the Trinity, revealing wisdom and love as interdependent practices of liberation in both traditions.

## Detailed Explanation
Catalyzed by the 1980 East-West Religions in Encounter conference in Hawaii and the founding of the Society for Buddhist-Christian Studies, the dialogue runs on several tracks at once: John B. Cobb's process-theological "creative transformation" (Christians temporarily indwelling Buddhist concepts before returning home changed); Lynn de Silva's retrieval of Buddhist "non-self" (anatta/anatman) to show the biblical self, too, is an interdependent psychophysical unity rather than a static Greek-style immortal soul; and Raimon Panikkar's contemplative "interior dialogue," seeking ultimate reality beyond either tradition's conceptual boundaries altogether.

## Example
Thomas Merton's and Paul Knitter's "socially engaged dialogue," grounding Buddhist-Christian encounter directly in the preferential option for the poor (Part V), shows Buddhist wisdom (prajna) and Christian love (agape) converging in shared practical action, not just shared philosophical concepts — most visibly in Merton's own late-life monastic dialogue with Buddhist teachers before his death in Thailand in 1968.

## Illustration
"Creative transformation" works like a musician who spends real time immersed in an entirely different musical tradition — not merely borrowing a few exotic notes, but coming home changed, playing their own tradition's music with a genuinely new depth they could not have reached by staying only within their original training.

## Key Terms
- **Creative Transformation**: John B. Cobb's process-theological method: Christians indwell Buddhist concepts and return "creatively transformed," with Christ as the ultimate image of this relational process.
- **Anatta / Anatman** (Pali / Sanskrit) — "non-self": The Buddhist doctrine of the shifting, non-substantial self, used by Lynn de Silva to argue the biblical self is likewise an interdependent psychophysical unity, not a static soul.
- **Interior Dialogue**: Raimon Panikkar's contemplative, monastic method of seeking ultimate reality beyond either tradition's own conceptual and dogmatic boundaries.
- **Sunyata** (Sanskrit) — "emptiness": The Mahayana Buddhist concept of ultimate reality as absolute emptiness, used by John P. Keenan to construct a Mahayana-Buddhist reading of Mark's Gospel.

## Connections
Tillich (Part I) held a landmark dialogue with Zen master Shin'ichi Hisamatsu; Moltmann (Part II.A) integrated Buddhist-influenced ecological and co-suffering themes into his creation theology. Rahner's "anonymous Christianity" and Küng's view of Buddhism as an "extraordinary way of salvation" (Part I, Part II.D) are respected but criticised here for treating Buddhism as mere preparation for the Gospel rather than a valid path in its own right.
