# 4. church - pol.mission.pdf

**TITLE**: The Church's Religious Identity and Its Social and Political Mission. By Francis Schüssler Fiorenza (The Catholic University of America). Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 43, 1982, pages 197–225 [85, 86].

**MAIN SUBJECT**: An exploration of how the Church’s political and social mission relates to its unique religious identity, proposing a hermeneutical, Christological, and historical framework to show that a commitment to social justice is constitutive of—rather than accidental to—Gospel proclamation [85-87].

**KEY TOPICS Covered**:
*   Critical analysis of four traditional models of social mission: Improper, Substitutive, Unofficial, and Partial [88-91].
*   Hermeneutical evaluation of post-Vatican II magisterial statements (e.g., *Justice in the World*, *Evangelii Nuntiandi*, and John Paul II's encyclicals) [92-95].
*   Methodological critique of defining the Church’s mission through "specific difference" [96-98].
*   Wittgenstein’s "family resemblance" (game analogy) and the phenomenological category of "appresentation" applied to religious acts [99-101].
*   The historical connection between the belief in a transcendent God and the emergence of human personhood [102, 103].
*   Wisdom Christology (*Sophia* / *Logos*) as the theological bridge between creation and redemption [301–303].
*   The historical intertwinement of the Church’s diaconal care for the poor and secular political structures (Constantine, Middle Ages, and the industrial revolution) [104-106].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Specific Difference**: A traditional logical method of defining a reality's essence by isolating its unique, distinct traits from everything else, which Fiorenza argues leads to a distorted, dualistic separation of the religious from the ethical, social, and political [97, 98].
2.  **Family Resemblance**: Ludwig Wittgenstein's linguistic concept showing that complex realities (like games or religion) cannot be reduced to a single common denominator, but are instead defined by a complicated, overlapping network of similarities and crisscrossings [99, 100].
3.  **Appresentation**: A phenomenological concept (adapted from Husserl) describing how the full, unseen meaning of an action (such as its religious dimension) is indirectly but concordantly grasped as being "co-present" with the visible, physical action itself [101, 107, 108].
4.  **Wisdom Christology**: A post-biblical trajectory that identifies Jesus with the divine order, power, and wisdom through which the universe was created, thereby bridging the theology of creation with the theology of redemption and justifying a concrete social ministry [301–303].
5.  **Constitutive Dimension**: The theological assertion (proclaimed by the 1971 Synod of Bishops) that action on behalf of justice and world transformation is an integral, non-accidental part of preaching the Gospel [93, 109].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Threat of Secularization and Loss of Identity**: Addressing critics who claim that focus on social/political advocacy causes churches to lose their religious message and turn into mere secular lobbying or social welfare groups [85, 110, 111].
2.  **The Inadequacy of the "Substitutive" Model**: Countering the view that the Church should run schools or hospitals only as temporary substitutes until secular state agencies can take them over, which splits the religious from the secular [112-114].
3.  **The Split Between "Official" and "Lay" Missions**: Rejecting Karl Rahner’s model that restricts the official hierarchy strictly to religious duties while relegating the social mission entirely to "unofficial" lay action [115, 116].
4.  **The Tension Between "Conversion of Heart" and "Systemic Change"**: Comparing Pope Paul VI's structural emphasis on combatting political systems of injustice with Pope John Paul II's personalist emphasis on the conversion of individual conscience within state-communist or Latin American contexts [117].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "Action on behalf of justice and participation in the transformation of the world appear to us as a constitutive dimension of the preaching of the gospel..." (Page 205 / Passage 256) - Context: Quoting the ground-breaking statement from the 1971 Synod document *Justice in the World*.
*   "a church that is nothing more than a benevolent socially useful group can be replaced by other groups not claiming to be churches: such a church has no justification for its existence." (Page 210 / Passage 274) - Context: Citing Paul Tillich's warning on the necessity of maintaining a distinctive religious identity.
*   "the religious is distorted... if it is defined precisely in its specific difference from the ethical, social, or political... As human nature is not a pure but an incarnate rationality, so religiosity manifests itself within the ethical, social, and political." (Page 211-212 / Passage 280) - Context: Critiquing the theological dualism that separates religion from life.
*   "The more the social or political ministry of the Church is related to Christianity's interpretative function as a religion to exhibit and to proclaim Jesus as the Power and Wisdom of the universe, the more constitutive, essential, and distinctive this ministry is." (Page 219 / Passage 308) - Context: Formulating the article's core "rule of praxis" for integrating social ministry with religious identity.
*   "Its obligation to speak out on social and political issues does not stem from a 'better knowledge' of economic problems... but from its option for those in misfortune." (Page 224 / Passage 326) - Context: Clarifying that the Church's political authority is grounded in solidarity with the marginalized rather than technical expertise.

**SUMMARY**:
Fiorenza addresses the critical theological challenge of how the Church can engage in robust social and political advocacy on behalf of justice without losing its distinctive, supernatural religious identity [85, 86]. Rejecting dualistic models that isolate the "proper" religious sphere from the "improper" political sphere, he uses Wittgenstein's "family resemblance" and Husserl's "appresentation" to argue that the religious dimension naturally crisscrosses and manifests itself within social realities [97-99, 101]. Grounded in a Wisdom Christology that links creation with redemption, and drawing upon a long historical tradition of episcopal oversight of social legislation, Fiorenza proposes a core guideline: the Church’s social and political ministries are intrinsically proper and constitutive to its mission to the exact degree that they are integrated with its central proclamation of Jesus as the Power and Wisdom of the universe [87, 105, 118, 119].
