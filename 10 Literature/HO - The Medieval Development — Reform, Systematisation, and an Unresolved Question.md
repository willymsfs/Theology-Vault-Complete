# Unit 5 — The Medieval Development — Reform, Systematisation, and an Unresolved Question

## The Gregorian Reform (11th century)

Under **Gregory VII** (1073-85) the Church attacked three intertwined abuses:

- **Simony** — the sale of office.
- **Nicolaitism** — clerical marriage and concubinage.
- **Lay investiture** — the appointment and installation of bishops and abbots by secular rulers, who handed over ring and crozier.

The resulting **Investiture Controversy** (Gregory VII vs Henry IV; Canossa, 1077) was settled at the **Concordat of Worms (1122)**: the Church confers the spiritual symbols, the emperor the temporal regalia. The lasting effect on the theology of orders was to sharpen the distinction between the **sacramental power** conferred by ordination and the **jurisdiction and temporalities** attached to an office.

## The Seven Orders — and the Missing Episcopate

**Peter Lombard** and the scholastic tradition list **seven orders**, ascending:

| Minor orders | Major (sacred) orders |
|---|---|
| Porter · Lector · Exorcist · Acolyte | **Subdeacon** · **Deacon** · **Priest** |

**Note what is absent: the episcopate.** For most scholastics — Aquinas included — the **bishop is not a distinct sacramental order** but a *dignity* or office added to the priesthood, carrying greater jurisdiction and the power to ordain and confirm.

**Why they reasoned this way.** Orders is defined by its ordination to the **Eucharist**, the greatest sacrament. A priest can already consecrate the Eucharist. Therefore the priesthood is the summit of the sacrament of orders, and episcopacy adds power over the *Mystical* Body (jurisdiction) rather than over the *sacramental* Body.

**This is the medieval question that Vatican II would finally settle** (Unit 9, *LG* 21) — and knowing that it was genuinely open for eight centuries is essential to appreciating what the Council did.

## Aquinas on Orders (ST Suppl. qq. 34-40)

| Category | Content |
|---|---|
| **Effect** | An indelible **character**, conferring a *spiritual power* ordered to the Eucharist |
| **Matter** | Following the practice of his time, the ***traditio instrumentorum*** — the handing over of the chalice and paten to the priest, book to the deacon |
| **Form** | The words accompanying that handing over |
| **Character** | Configures the ordained to Christ as **minister**, giving power to act *in persona Christi* in confecting the Eucharist |
| **Distinction** | The **power of orders** (from ordination, unlosable) versus the **power of jurisdiction** (from office, losable) |

**Florence's Decree for the Armenians (1439)** enshrined the *traditio instrumentorum* as the matter of the sacrament — "the priesthood is conferred by the handing over of the chalice with wine and the paten with bread."

**This turned out to be wrong**, and it is a superb case study for essays on the limits of magisterial formulations: the *traditio instrumentorum* is a **medieval Western innovation**, unknown to the East and to the first millennium of the West, where the matter was always the **imposition of hands**. **Pius XII's *Sacramentum Ordinis* (1947)** corrected it definitively (Unit 8). A conciliar decree of instruction to a particular church described the practice of its own time and got the theology of the essential rite wrong — which is precisely why such documents are read for their doctrinal intent rather than as timeless definitions.

## The Late Medieval Distortions

The **benefice system** tied ordination to income rather than to a community; **absolute ordinations** revived in practice despite Chalcedon; the proliferation of **private and votive Masses** produced "mass priests" whose ministry was largely the offering of stipendiary Masses; and pluralism and absenteeism separated the office from the pastoral care it existed for. These are the abuses the Reformation attacked and Trent legislated against.
