# 14. half cent of ecclesiology.pdf

**TITLE**: "A HALF CENTURY OF ECCLESIOLOGY." By Avery Dulles, S.J. (Fordham University). Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 50, 1989, pages 419–442 [97].

**MAIN SUBJECT**:
A comprehensive historical and theological survey of Catholic systematic ecclesiology over a fifty-year period (1940–1989), divided into the 25 years leading up to the end of Vatican II (1940–1965) and the 25 years of post-conciliar development (1965–1989) [97-99].

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   Pre-Vatican II Neo-Scholastic manualists (Billot, Zapelena, Salaverri) and their focus on juridical power [99, 100].
*   Charles Journet's ontological, Aristotelian ecclesiology [101, 102].
*   The revival of Mystical Body theology (Mersch, Tromp) and a return to patristic sources [103-105].
*   Pope Pius XII's encyclical *Mystici corporis Christi* (1943) and its controversies [106-108].
*   The rise of the "People of God" image (Grosche, Koster, Ratzinger) [109].
*   The massive pre-conciliar and conciliar influence of Yves Congar [110-112].
*   Conciliar shifts: episcopal collegiality, laity, sacraments, and the "pilgrim Church" [113-116].
*   The ecclesiology of Vatican II (Lumen Gentium) and post-conciliar hermeneutical debates [117-119].
*   Major post-conciliar theologians: Karl Rahner [120], Hans Küng [121], Heribert Mühlen [122], Louis Bouyer [123], and Jean-Marie Tillard [124].
*   Secular/political theology (Metz, McBrien) and Latin American Liberation theology (Gutiérrez, Boff, Azevedo) [125-129].
*   The Extraordinary Synod of 1985 and its integration of the five "Models of the Church" [130, 131].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Perfect Society (Societas Perfecta)**: A classic pre-Vatican II manualist concept depicting the Church as a visible, hierarchical, and self-sufficient society that possesses all necessary legal power and resources to govern its members and achieve its ends [100, 132].
2.  **Subsistit In (Subsists In)**: The historic formulation adopted in Lumen Gentium (replacing the strict identification of *Mystici corporis*), allowing for the presence of authentic ecclesial elements in non-Catholic communities [108, 133].
3.  **Sacrament of Christ (Ursakrament)**: The post-conciliar retrieval of the idea that the Church itself is the primary sacrament of Christ, with the seven individual sacraments acting as concrete historical actualizations of the Church [115, 134].
4.  **Una Mystica Persona (Mühlen)**: Heribert Mühlen's systematic personalist formula defining the Church's essential reality as "one person [the Holy Spirit] in many persons [Christ and us]" [122].
5.  **Ecclesiogenesis (Boff)**: Leonardo Boff’s concept describing the "reinvention of the Church" from below, where spontaneous lay base communities reconstitute the Church without centralized hierarchical dominance [129, 135].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Over-Juridicism of Manuals**: The pre-conciliar reduction of the Church's functions to legal categories of jurisdiction and power, which downplayed its inner, mystical mystery [100, 136].
2.  **The Visibility Paradox of *Mystici Corporis***: Resolving how validly baptized non-Catholics relate to the Mystical Body, reconciling the strict papal claim of absolute visibility and Roman identification with patristic and sacramental realities [108, 137].
3.  **Universalism vs. Regionalism**: The ongoing post-conciliar tension concerning the theological status of episcopal conferences, local autonomy, and the role of the Roman Curia [138, 139].
4.  **The Politicization of the Church**: The debate between the Platonizing "neo-Augustinian" school (Ratzinger, Balthasar, de Lubac) who reject reducing the Church to an instrument of socio-political change, and secular/liberation theologians who see the Church as a servant organization dedicated to historical justice [125, 135, 140].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "...ecclesiology had not yet assumed the central position in Catholic theology that it has enjoyed in our own century." (Page 419 / Section 1) - Context: Introducing the prophetic statement of Otto Dibelius calling the 20th century the "century of the Church" [97].
*   "Generally speaking, these Latin manuals showed a predilection for juridical categories... Christ was seen as the founder of the Church; the Church was presented as a 'perfect society'..." (Page 420 / Section 1) - Context: Describing the preconciliar state of Neo-Scholastic manual theology [100].
*   "Where Pius XII had said that the Mystical Body and the Roman Catholic Church were one and the same thing, Vatican II contented itself with saying that the Church of Christ 'subsists in' the Roman Catholic Church..." (Page 430 / Section 1) - Context: Highlighting the major ecumenical and dogmatic shift of the Council [133].
*   "In the last analysis, therefore, Rahner seemed to favor the charismatic over the institutional." (Page 431 / Section 2) - Context: Summarizing Karl Rahner's theological prioritization of grace over structure [120].
*   "True reform, these theologians [neo-Augustinians] maintain, is interior and spiritual. It requires humility and obedience, respect for authority and for tradition." (Page 440 / Section 2) - Context: Explaining the backlash of conservative ressourcement scholars against secularized and democratic models of reform [135].
*   "The Church as communion is a sacrament for the salvation of the world." (Page 442 / Section 2) - Context: Quoting the central, integrating thesis of the 1985 Extraordinary Synod's Final Report [131].

**SUMMARY**:
Avery Dulles offers a magisterial synthesis of the dramatic evolution of Catholic ecclesiology from 1940 to 1989 [97]. He charts the transition from the highly defensive, hierarchical, and juridical "perfect society" manuals of preconciliar Neo-Scholasticism to the rich, patristic, and scriptural "Mystical Body" and "People of God" models of Vatican II [100, 106, 109, 117]. Dulles profiles the diverse post-conciliar theological landscape—ranging from Rahner's charismatic personalism and Tillard's communion ecclesiology to Boff's liberationist ecclesiogenesis—showing how the field split into competing progressive, neo-Augustinian, and political camps [120, 124, 127, 129, 135]. He concludes that the 1985 Extraordinary Synod represents a vital effort to harmonize these disparate schools by weaving them into a balanced, sacramental, and communion-centered vision of the Church's mission to the world [130, 131, 141].
