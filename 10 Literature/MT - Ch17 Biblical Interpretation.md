# Ch. 17 — Biblical Interpretation
*Chapter author: Anthony C. Thiselton*

## Core Idea
Thiselton traces biblical hermeneutics from Enlightenment "value-neutral" historical exegesis toward self-involving, existential, and canonical models of reading, defending a "textual realism" in which Scripture has genuine, objective, life-shaping power rather than being reducible to the reader's own projection.

## Detailed Explanation
The post-Enlightenment split between historical-critical exegesis (treating the canon as a purely human, historically contingent product) and dogmatic theology suffered a crisis of confidence in WWI, prompting Barth's break with his liberal teachers (Part I) and his fresh, dialectical re-reading of Scripture "as though he had never read it before." The twentieth century then absorbed existentialism (Bultmann's use of Heidegger), the linguistic and narrative turns (Ricoeur, Wittgenstein), and Brevard Childs's "canonical" movement, which redirects attention from a text's speculative pre-history back to its final, received shape and its ongoing history of effects (Wirkungsgeschichte).

## Example
James Barr's critique of "illegitimate totality transfer" — the common preacher's error of reading every historical meaning a Greek or Hebrew word ever carried, across many different passages, back into one single verse — remains a standard caution taught in seminary exegesis courses, directly traceable to this chapter's concerns about disciplined, historically responsible interpretation.

## Illustration
Reading a text with real "pre-understanding" (Vorverständnis) is like tuning a radio: you cannot receive any signal at all with the dial at pure static, but you also cannot hear the actual broadcast if your own preset assumptions drown it out. Good interpretation, on this model, keeps adjusting the dial — bringing real questions to the text, but staying genuinely open to what the text itself is broadcasting back.

## Key Terms
- **Vorverständnis** (German) — "pre-understanding": The inevitable framework of questions and interests a reader always brings to a text — for Bultmann, the necessary starting condition for any understanding at all.
- **Canonical Context**: Brevard Childs's approach directing attention to a text's final, received canonical shape and its history of effects, rather than its speculative reconstructed pre-history.
- **Logic of Self-Involvement**: The view, drawn from language philosophy, that certain speech-acts and biblical texts do not merely inform but actively commit and transform the reader who receives them.
- **Illegitimate Totality Transfer**: James Barr's term for the exegetical error of reading a word's whole cumulative range of historical meanings back into one specific, single occurrence.

## Connections
Barth and Bultmann (Part I) represent the two great twentieth-century "theologians of the Word": Barth treats the Bible's authority as an objective "free power," Bultmann demythologizes it toward the reader's existential self-understanding. Draws heavily on Paul Ricoeur's hermeneutic of "suspicion and retrieval" and on Hans-Georg Gadamer's fusion of historical horizons, which also underlies Childs's canonical method.
