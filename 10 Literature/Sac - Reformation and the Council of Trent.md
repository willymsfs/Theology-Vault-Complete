# Unit 4 — Reformation and the Council of Trent

## The Reformers

All the magisterial Reformers kept baptism; they disagreed violently about *how* it works and *what* it does.

**Martin Luther.** In *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church* (1520) he reduces the sacraments to two (baptism and the Supper; penance hovers). But he is fiercely *pro*-baptism, and his theology of it is arguably the richest of the sixteenth century:

- Baptism is God's **promise** attached to a sign; faith does not *make* it work, faith *receives* it.
- He retains infant baptism, appealing to *fides infantium* (infant faith) and the faith of the Church.
- **Large Catechism (1529)**: baptism signifies "that the old Adam in us should, by daily sorrow and repentance, be drowned and die with all sins and evil lusts." Luther's pastoral counsel in temptation was literally *"Baptizatus sum"* — **"I am baptised."** Baptism is not a past event but a standing identity.

**Huldrych Zwingli.** Baptism is a **covenant sign and pledge of allegiance** — the Christian counterpart to circumcision — but it does not confer grace. This is the memorialist line.

**John Calvin (*Institutes* IV.15-16).** A middle path: baptism is a "sign and seal" of engrafting into Christ, genuinely instrumental in the hands of the Spirit but never automatic. He defends infant baptism strongly on **covenant continuity** with circumcision.

**The Radical Reformation / Anabaptists.** The genuinely revolutionary position: **only believer's baptism** is baptism at all, because faith must precede the sign. The **Schleitheim Confession (1527)**, Art. 1, is the classic statement. Their opponents called them *Anabaptists* ("re-baptisers"); they replied they were not *re*-baptising, since infant baptism was never baptism. Both Catholics and magisterial Protestants executed them for it — often, with grim irony, by drowning.

## The Council of Trent, Session VII (3 March 1547)

Trent responds with three sets of canons: on the sacraments **in general** (13), on **baptism** (14), and on **confirmation** (3). The key baptismal canons:

| Canon | Condemns the view that... |
|---|---|
| 2 | ...true and natural water is not necessary (against Luther's metaphorical readings) |
| 3 | ...baptism in the Roman Church differs in truth from that of other churches |
| 4 | ...baptism given by heretics in the Trinitarian formula with intent to do what the Church does is not true baptism |
| 5 | ...baptism is optional, not necessary for salvation |
| 8 | ...the baptised are free from all the precepts of the Church |
| 13 | ...infants are not to be baptised, or should be rebaptised at the age of reason |
| 14 | ...children so baptised should, on growing up, be asked whether they will ratify it |

From the general canons, **canon 8** defines *ex opere operato* and **canon 9** defines the **character** on baptism, confirmation, and orders. Trent's **Session V (1546) on Original Sin** supplies the anthropological premise: baptism truly removes the *reatus* (guilt) of original sin, though *concupiscence* remains — not itself sin, but "of sin and inclining to sin."

**Teaching point for exams.** Trent's canon 4 is a masterpiece of unintended ecumenical consequence. In defending the ancient rule against the Donatists, Trent committed the Catholic Church to recognising the validity of Protestant baptism — which is exactly what *Unitatis Redintegratio* 3 and 22 would cash in four centuries later.
