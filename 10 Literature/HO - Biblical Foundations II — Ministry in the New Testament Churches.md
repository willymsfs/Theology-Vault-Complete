# Unit 2 — Biblical Foundations II — Ministry in the New Testament Churches

## The Vocabulary Problem

This is the single most important observation in the biblical unit, and every good essay makes it.

**The New Testament never calls a Christian minister *hiereus* (priest/sacerdos).** The word is used of Jewish priests, of pagan priests, of Christ (in Hebrews), and of **all the baptised** (1 Pet 2:5, 9; Rev 1:6, 5:10) — but never of an office-holder in the Church.

The words actually used for ministers are:

| Term | Meaning | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ***episkopos*** | overseer, supervisor | A secular administrative term, not a cultic one |
| ***presbyteros*** | elder | From synagogue eldership |
| ***diakonos*** | servant, agent, go-between | Applied to Paul himself, and to Christ (Rom 15:8) |

**The theological consequence.** The sacerdotal vocabulary applied to Christian ministers is a *post-biblical development* (Unit 3). This does not make it illegitimate — doctrine develops — but it means the Catholic position must be argued as **development**, not as plain reading, and it explains why the Reformers thought they were returning to Scripture in rejecting it.

## The Fluidity of the Earliest Structures

**Acts 20:17 and 20:28** are decisive: Paul summons the ***presbyteroi*** of Ephesus and then tells those same men that the Spirit has made them ***episkopoi***. **Titus 1:5-7** does the same thing within two verses. In the New Testament these are **not yet two distinct offices**.

Other data points:
- **Acts 6:1-6** — the Seven are appointed with prayer and the laying on of hands for the daily distribution. The text never calls them *diakonoi*, and two of them (Stephen, Philip) immediately preach and baptise. The traditional identification with the diaconate is later.
- **Acts 14:23** — Paul and Barnabas "appointed presbyters in every church."
- **1 Cor 12:28 and Eph 4:11** — lists of ministries (apostles, prophets, teachers, evangelists, pastors) that do not map onto the later threefold pattern at all.
- **Phil 1:1** — greets "the *episkopoi* and *diakonoi*," plural, in one community.

## The Pastoral Epistles: Structure Emerging

The Pastorals show the transition. **1 Tim 3:1-13** and **Titus 1:5-9** give **qualifications** — and note what kind: they are overwhelmingly about *character and household management* (above reproach, hospitable, not a drunkard, managing his own household well), not about cultic capacity.

Crucially, **1 Tim 4:14** and **2 Tim 1:6** describe the conferral: *"the gift (*charisma*) that is in you, which was given you through prophecy with the **laying on of hands** by the council of elders"* / *"the gift of God that is within you through the laying on of my hands."* This is the New Testament root of ordination as a **sacramental gesture conferring a gift**, and it is the rite Pius XII will identify in 1947 as the sole essential matter (Unit 8).

## Women in New Testament Ministry

The evidence must be stated accurately, since it bears directly on Unit 10's debates:
- **Rom 16:1** — Phoebe is called ***diakonos*** of the church at Cenchreae (the masculine form, of a woman) and *prostatis*, patron or leader.
- **Rom 16:7** — Junia, "outstanding among the apostles," whose name was masculinised to "Junias" in some later manuscripts and translations, a reading now generally abandoned.
- **1 Tim 3:11** — *gynaikas*, "women likewise," in the middle of the qualifications for deacons: either deacons' wives or women deacons, and the Greek does not settle it.
- **1 Tim 2:12** and **1 Cor 14:34-35** — the restrictive texts, the latter with genuine text-critical questions about its placement.

**Teaching point.** The New Testament gives us: a *charism* conferred by the laying on of hands with prayer; a set of *functions* (oversight, service, teaching, presiding); a *vocabulary* that is deliberately non-cultic; and *structures still in motion*. It does not give us the threefold ministry as later fixed. That comes next.
