# Unit 2 — Biblical Foundations II — The Power of the Keys and Apostolic Practice

## The Three Foundational Texts

| Text | Recipient | Words |
|---|---|---|
| **Matthew 16:19** | **Peter** | "I will give you the **keys** of the kingdom of heaven, and whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven..." |
| **Matthew 18:18** | **The disciples / community** | "Whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven" — set in the context of fraternal correction (18:15-17) |
| **John 20:22-23** | **The apostles, Easter evening** | "He **breathed** on them and said, 'Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained'" |

**Exegetical observations that matter for the doctrine:**

- **"Binding and loosing"** is a rabbinic technical pair covering both *doctrinal* decision (declaring something permitted or forbidden) and *disciplinary* action (excommunicating or restoring to fellowship). The Church's later reading — the authority to forgive or withhold forgiveness — falls squarely within the second sense.
- **Matthew 18 sets the power in a communal, procedural context**: private correction, then two or three witnesses, then the assembly, then treating the unrepentant "as a Gentile and a tax collector." This is the New Testament's clearest picture of **ecclesial discipline**, and it is the direct ancestor of canonical penance (Unit 3).
- **John 20:22 deliberately echoes Genesis 2:7** — God breathing life into Adam — and Ezekiel 37. The forgiveness of sins is presented as an act of **new creation**, and it is bound to the gift of the Spirit.
- **"If you retain"** is the crucial half for the later doctrine. The authority is *discretionary*, which presupposes some means of **knowing** what is to be forgiven or retained — the exegetical basis Trent will use for the necessity of confessing sins (Unit 9).

## Apostolic Practice in the Epistles

- **2 Cor 5:18-20** — God "gave us the **ministry of reconciliation** (*diakonian tēs katallagēs*)... we are ambassadors for Christ." Reconciliation is entrusted to a ministry, not left to private transaction.
- **1 Cor 5:1-5 and 2 Cor 2:5-11** — the single clearest apostolic case: Paul orders a grave sinner excluded from the community, and later urges that he be **forgiven and restored** lest he "be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow." Exclusion, repentance, readmission — the shape of canonical penance is already visible.
- **James 5:14-16** — "**confess your sins to one another** and pray for one another, that you may be healed," set immediately alongside the anointing of the sick by the presbyters, whose effect includes "if he has committed sins, he will be forgiven."
- **1 John 1:9** — "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us"; and **1 Jn 5:16-17**, the puzzling distinction between "sin unto death" and sin "not unto death," which foreshadows the later **mortal/venial** distinction without settling it.

**Teaching point.** The New Testament does not give a rite. What it gives is: the authority to forgive and retain, entrusted to a ministry; a communal disciplinary procedure; and the practice of confession. Every later form of the sacrament — canonical, tariffed, scholastic, modern — is an attempt to house those three elements in a workable pastoral structure. Understanding this saves you from the two opposite errors of claiming the confessional box is apostolic and of claiming the sacrament was invented in the Middle Ages.
