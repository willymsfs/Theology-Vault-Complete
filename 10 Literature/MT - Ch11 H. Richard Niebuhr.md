# Ch. 11 — H. Richard Niebuhr
*Chapter author: Stanley Hauerwas*

## Core Idea
H. Richard Niebuhr's "radical monotheism" centres faith on God's absolute sovereignty alone, condemning "Christomonism" — the defensive absolutizing of any finite thing, even Christ, the church, or a nation — as idolatry, and reframes moral life as the "responsible self" discerning the historically "fitting" response to God's ongoing action.

## Detailed Explanation
Younger brother of Reinhold Niebuhr, H. Richard studied at Yale under the pragmatist social psychology of G. H. Mead and wrote his dissertation on Ernst Troeltsch's response to historical relativism — a lifelong theme. Rather than resolving relativism by appeal to a fixed, external authority, he located an answer in radical monotheism itself: because God alone is absolute, every finite human perspective (including the Church's own) is properly relative and provisional, held humbly rather than defended absolutely. He taught at Yale Divinity School from 1931 until his death in 1962, and famously debated his brother Reinhold publicly in 1931 over how Christians should respond to the Manchurian crisis.

## Example
Niebuhr's own typology in Christ and Culture (his best-known book) — Christ against culture, Christ of culture, Christ above culture, Christ and culture in paradox, Christ the transformer of culture — became one of the most widely taught frameworks in American theological education for classifying how churches relate to their surrounding society, still assigned in seminaries worldwide.

## Illustration
"Christomonism," for Niebuhr, is like insisting a single instrument (however beautiful) is the entire orchestra — isolating Christ from the wider trinitarian symphony of Creator and Sustainer risks turning devotion to Christ into a defensive, idolatrous absolutizing of one part standing in for the whole.

## Key Terms
- **Radical Monotheism**: Centring loyalty strictly on the one transcendent source of all being, refusing to absolutize any finite value, nation, or even a Christological claim taken in isolation.
- **Theocentric Relativism**: The view that all human knowledge, being historically and communally situated, is properly relative — a finitude validated, not threatened, by God's own absolute sovereignty.
- **The Responsible Self**: Niebuhr's model of moral agency: not pursuing an abstract good or obeying fixed rules, but responding fittingly to God's action as read within an ongoing historical narrative.
- **The Fitting**: The moral criterion of the historically appropriate, responsive action, rather than conformity to a predetermined abstract code.

## Connections
Challenges Barth's Christological objectivism (Part I) as risking "Christomonism." His account of historical relativity directly anticipated Hans Frei's narrative theology and the whole postliberal "Yale school" (Part II.D). Engaged in a lifelong dialogical debate with his brother Reinhold Niebuhr (next chapter). Later sharply criticised by John Howard Yoder for implicitly ranking "Christ the transformer of culture" above the concrete nonviolent ethics of Jesus.
