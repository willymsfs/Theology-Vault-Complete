# Part 2 — The Nicene-Constantinopolitan Crisis and Resolution

## Arius and the Alexandrian Controversy (c. 318)

The controversy erupted between the Alexandrian presbyter **Arius** and his bishop, **Alexander of Alexandria**. Arius's theology began from a radical apophaticism: God's defining property is unbegottenness (*agennesia*); since the Son is *begotten*, he cannot share that property, and so must belong to the created order, however exalted. His notorious slogan: *"there once was when the Son was not"* (*ēn pote hote ouk ēn*). The theological motive was to protect the absolute transcendence and uniqueness of the unbegotten Father from any compromise.

## The Council of Nicaea (325)

Convened by Constantine to resolve the dispute, Nicaea adopted ***homoousios*** ("of the same substance/essence") to describe the Son's relation to the Father — "true God from true God, begotten not made, *homoousios* with the Father" — with anathemas explicitly condemning Arian slogans. *Homoousios* was theologically loaded: a non-scriptural term, previously associated with Gnostic emanationism, and suspected by many of modalist implications.

## The Post-Nicene Crisis: Marcellus of Ancyra and the "Modalism Scare"

**Marcellus of Ancyra**, a vigorous pro-Nicene ally, read *homoousios* in a way that collapsed the Son's distinct hypostatic existence back into the Father, teaching the Logos would ultimately be "reabsorbed" into the Godhead at the eschaton. Marcellus's condemnation (Constantinople, 336) badly discredited *homoousios* language across the Greek East for a generation, since Easterners now associated the term with modalism rather than orthodoxy.

## The Mid-Century Fragmentation

Three rival post-Nicene factions filled the vacuum: **Homoians** ("like-minded"), who banned *ousia*-language altogether and said the Son is merely "like" (*homoios*) the Father without specifying likeness of substance; **Homoiousians** ("like-in-essence"), who held the Son "like in essence" (*homoiousios*) — differing from Nicene *homoousios* by a single iota, yet theologically decisive; and **Heterousians/Neo-Arians** (**Eunomius** of Cyzicus), who pushed Arius's logic further, holding the Son radically *unlike* (*anhomoios*) the Father, since God's essence — *agennesia* again — is fully knowable and definitionally exclusive to the Father alone.

## Athanasius's Bridge-Building: The Tome to the Antiochenes (362)

**Athanasius**, convening the Synod of Alexandria after his final return from exile, achieved a crucial linguistic breakthrough: Easterners speaking of "three *hypostases*" and Westerners of "one *hypostasis*" could be confessing the identical faith using different technical vocabularies — *hypostasis* had drifted to mean roughly "person" in Greek usage, while its Latin cognate tracked closer to *substantia* ("one nature"). This opened the door to the Cappadocian settlement.

## The Cappadocian Settlement

**Basil of Caesarea** distinguished *ousia* (the common, general nature shared identically by all three) from *hypostasis* (the particular, concrete individual): ***mia ousia, treis hypostaseis*** — "one essence, three hypostases." Against Eunomius, he argued that terms like "unbegotten" and "begotten" name only the persons' distinguishing relational properties (*idiomata*), not the strictly incomprehensible divine essence itself. He maintained a deliberate pastoral "economy" (reticence) about explicitly calling the Spirit *homoousios*, to avoid provoking further schism — a reserve that frustrated both his own brother and Gregory of Nazianzus.

**Gregory of Nyssa** offered the illustrative analogy of three individual men (Peter, James, John) sharing a single common human nature, just as three divine *hypostases* share the one divine *ousia* ("*To Ablabius, On Not Three Gods*"). To block the risk of tritheism this analogy invites, he grounded real unity in the doctrine of **inseparable operations**: every act of the Trinity *ad extra* proceeds as a single, undivided action — "from the Father, through the Son, perfected in the Holy Spirit" — since the persons never act independently of one another.

**Christopher Beeley's revisionist Gregory of Nazianzus** dismantles the standard "harmonious Cappadocian trio" narrative as a later idealization. Gregory was in fact estranged from Basil, resentful of Basil's cautious pastoral silence about the Spirit's full divinity. He preferred concrete, biblical/relational language over Basil's more Aristotelian *ousia*-based metaphysics, and explicitly rejected Nyssa's "three men" analogy as tritheism-prone. For Gregory of Nazianzus, the true ground of trinitarian unity is not an abstract shared *ousia* but **the person of the Father himself**, understood as *archē* (source) and *aitia* (cause) — a monarchy of origin that properly *constitutes*, rather than threatens, the equal divinity of Son and Spirit. His *Five Theological Orations* (Orations 27-31, Constantinople 380) establish **partitive exegesis** — lofty scriptural texts about Christ refer to the divine nature, "lowly" texts (hunger, weeping, ignorance of the Day) refer to the assumed human nature — and finally break Basil's reserve to proclaim the Spirit explicitly *homoousios*, grounding this in the logic of baptismal *theosis*: only God can deify, so if the Spirit deifies believers in baptism, the Spirit must be fully God.

## The Council of Constantinople (381) and the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed

Presidency was fractious: begun under Meletius of Antioch (who died mid-council), passed to Gregory of Nazianzus (who, disgusted by factional politics over his own contested election, resigned mid-council, lamenting the result "an offspring completely unlike its parents"), and concluded under Nectarius. The Council issued the expanded Creed, adding extensive material on the Holy Spirit, but — following Basil's pastoral reserve — described the Spirit in liturgical **co-glorification** language ("who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified") rather than an explicit *homoousios*. The 382 synodical letter to Rome formally equated the Greek "three hypostases" with the Latin "three persons," sealing East-West agreement on Athanasius's earlier linguistic bridge.
