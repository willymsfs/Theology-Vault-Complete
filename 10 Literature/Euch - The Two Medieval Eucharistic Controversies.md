# Unit 5 — The Two Medieval Eucharistic Controversies

Western Eucharistic doctrine was forged in two controversies, two centuries apart. Both asked the same question: **how can the presence be real without being crudely physical?**

## The First Controversy: Paschasius Radbertus vs Ratramnus (9th c.)

Two monks of the same abbey, Corbie, wrote treatises with nearly the same title.

- **Paschasius Radbertus**, *De Corpore et Sanguine Domini* (831) — the first monograph devoted to the Eucharist. Strongly realist: the body on the altar is **the very body born of Mary and crucified**, identical with the historical body, though present in a manner hidden under the figure of bread.
- **Ratramnus of Corbie**, responding at the emperor's request, distinguished *in figura* from *in veritate* and argued the presence is real but **spiritual and figurative in mode** — the elements are not the historical body in the crude sense that would be visible to the senses.

The controversy was not formally settled. Both texts survived, and Ratramnus's would be republished by Reformers in the sixteenth century as evidence that the early medieval Church had not taught transubstantiation.

## The Second Controversy: Berengar of Tours vs Lanfranc (1050s-1079)

**Berengar of Tours**, drawing on Ratramnus (whom he mistakenly attributed to John Scotus Eriugena), argued from dialectic that the accidents of bread cannot remain without their substance — so the bread must remain bread, and the presence must be spiritual.

His principal opponent was **Lanfranc of Bec**, later Archbishop of Canterbury, who defended a change of substance while the appearances remain.

Berengar was made to swear two oaths, and the difference between them is one of the most instructive episodes in the history of doctrine:

| Oath | Wording | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Rome, 1059** (Cardinal Humbert's formula) | The body of Christ is "sensibly handled by the hands of the priests, **broken and crushed by the teeth of the faithful**" | Theologically crude — a **physicalist over-correction** the Church itself later moved away from |
| **Rome, 1079** | The bread is "**substantially changed** (*substantialiter converti*)" into the true body of Christ | Precise and enduring — the direct ancestor of *transubstantiatio* |

**Teaching point.** The 1059 formula is a gift to any essay on the development of doctrine: it shows the magisterium *over-shooting* under polemical pressure and then correcting itself within twenty years toward more careful metaphysical language. Aquinas would later explicitly reject the crude reading, insisting that what is broken and chewed is the **species**, not Christ's body as such (*ST* III q.77 a.7).

**Illustration.** Imagine trying to describe how a person is present in a photograph to someone who insists either "that IS her, in the paper" or "that's just ink." Both are wrong, and the second sounds more reasonable only because the first was stated so badly. The 1059 oath is the first answer shouted too loudly; transubstantiation is the attempt to say something more careful than either.
