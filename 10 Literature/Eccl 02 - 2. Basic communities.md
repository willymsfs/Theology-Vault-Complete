# 2. Basic communities.pdf

**TITLE**: Basic Ecclesial Communities: A Meeting Point of Ecclesiologies. By Marcello de C. Azevedo, S.J. (Gregorian University, Rome, and Centro João XXIII, Rio de Janeiro). Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 46, 1985, pages 601–620 [35, 36].

**MAIN SUBJECT**: An analysis of Latin American Basic Ecclesial Communities (BECs), using Brazil as a primary case study, to show how these communities represent a concrete "way of being Church" that can be read, interpreted, and enriched by several major Western/Northern ecclesiologies [37-39].

**KEY TOPICS Covered**:
*   The defining sociological and theological characteristics of BECs in Brazil [37, 40].
*   The dimensions of communion and participation as a contrast to Western individualism [40].
*   The double meaning of being "at the base" of both the Church and society [41].
*   The relationship of BECs to Avery Dulles’s five models of the Church (Institution, Sacrament, Herald, Servant, Communion) [42, 43].
*   The chronic shortage of priests and the rise of lay ministers [44, 45].
*   The non-naive, relational reading of Scripture in light of daily life (Carlos Mesters) [46].
*   The "soteriological component" of BECs and the three levels of salvation/liberation [47, 48].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Basic Ecclesial Communities (BECs / CEBs)**: Small, flexible grassroots groups (typically gathering 20 to 50 people) that seek to live a pattern of Christian life marked by deep interpersonal communion, shared faith, and active social/political participation [37, 40, 49].
2.  **The Base (de base)**: A term with a double meaning. Ecclesiastically, it refers to the active lay people situated at the bottom of the clerical hierarchy [41]. Sociologically, in developing nations like Brazil, it refers to the structurally poor and marginalized who feel a stronger need for mutual support and societal transformation [41, 49].
3.  **Sacrament of Christ's Kenosis**: The visible sign of Christ's self-emptying (Philippians 2) lived out by the poor and lay members of the BECs, which serves as a powerful means of grace and renewal for the institutional Church and its pastors [50].
4.  **Desobriga**: A traditional Brazilian pastoral paradigm of "sacramentalization" where an overworked, scarce priest would travel to rural areas to rapidly "discharge obligations" (baptisms, marriages, confessions) in a brief period, leaving the faithful with a passive, individualized understanding of faith [51, 52].
5.  **Praxis**: A concrete form of historical commitment and daily involvement in making history, stemming from conscious choices of faith to live out the demands of justice, service, and liberation in time [53, 54].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Scarcity of Ordained Priests**: Managing the daily life of communities in the chronic absence of priests, which has forced a decentralization of church ministries to the laity without breaking communion with the parish or diocese [44].
2.  **The Structural Eucharist Paradox**: The critical problem where active, highly cohesive, word-centered BECs are intensely aware of the central structural role of the Eucharist but find it impossible to celebrate it regularly because of strict clerical celibacy and holy orders regulations [12, 55].
3.  **Subjectivistic and Ideological Bible Readings**: Preventing lay-led Scripture circles from falling into naive, selective, or highly subjective interpretations that strip the biblical text of its historical and material reality [46].
4.  **Combatting Individualistic Western Culture**: Offering a collective, sharing-centered "gift-economy" of salvation to counter the highly competitive, self-interested, and privatized approach of modern Western culture [40, 41].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "the BECs are not simply a movement or association in the Church but rather a way of being Church." (Page 604 / Passage 103) - Context: Establishing the foundational theological premise of Latin American BEC ecclesiology.
*   "Faith is not locked in the mind and even less within the private, individual horizon. Faith is a dynamic factor of personal conversion and societal transformation." (Page 603 / Passage 100) - Context: Describing how BEC members at the sociological base link gospel demands to daily life.
*   "The ordained minister takes his place once again within a community growing increasingly aware of its diverse vocations and functions" (Page 609 / Passage 118) - Context: Describing how lay ministerial expansion repositions the priest within a collaborative church model.
*   "In the living Church embodied by BECs we see, first and foremost, a keen awareness of the structural significance of the Eucharist... but also of the actual impossibility of their having the Eucharist with its full meaning and reality." (Page 610 / Passage 121) - Context: Highlighting the major sacramental crisis confronting multiplying, priestless grassroots communities.
*   "The little patch of the People of God that is living in each BEC, an 'initial cell' as Medellin puts it, is a sign and sacrament of the People of God" (Page 618 / Passage 148) - Context: Demonstrating how the local "Church of the poor" serves as a microcosm of the universal Church envisioned by Vatican II.

**SUMMARY**:
Azevedo argues that Basic Ecclesial Communities (BECs) in Brazil represent an authentic and transformative "way of being Church" that transcends a mere parish association [38]. By testing BECs against Avery Dulles’s five models of the Church, he demonstrates that these communities enrich and embody the institutional, sacramental, kerygmatic, diaconal, and communitarian dimensions of Catholic ecclesiology at the grassroots level [43, 50, 56-58]. They systematically dismantle the passive, clericalized "sacramentalization" of the old *desobriga* model by promoting shared lay leadership, prophetic solidarity with the poor, and a communal reading of Scripture in light of real-life struggles [45, 51, 59]. Azevedo concludes that BECs offer a vital, liberating model of "Christian praxis" that can renew global theology by showing how a structurally poor community can live as a true sacrament of the People of God [54, 60, 61].
