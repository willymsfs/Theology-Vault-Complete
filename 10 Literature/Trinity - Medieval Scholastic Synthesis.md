# Part 4 — Medieval Scholastic Synthesis

## Richard of St Victor: The Argument from Love

Richard's *De Trinitate* argues from the nature of *caritas* itself, not from psychological faculties. Perfect love cannot be self-enclosed — a solitary God's love would be need-dependent on creatures for its object, which is unworthy of God. Two persons loving each other mutually yields only *biunity* (*dilectio mutua*) — still incomplete, since perfect love characteristically desires to *share* its beloved. The summit of love is ***condilectio***: two who are already united in mutual love now uniting together in shared love of a third, loved with equal dignity as a partner in loving, not merely as an object. Richard maps this triadic structure onto the divine persons: the Father is pure, gratuitous, unoriginate love; the Son is love both gratuitous (toward the Spirit) and owed (*debita*, toward the Father, as received); the Spirit is love owed alone — wholly received, the terminus of the Father's and Son's mutual love. Richard replaces Boethius's classic definition of person ("an individual substance of a rational nature") with ***existentia*** — *ex-* (out of, denoting relation of origin) + *sistentia* (standing, existing) — yielding: a divine person is "an incommunicable existence of the divine nature," defined by its unique relation of origin.

## Bonaventure: Fontal Fullness and Self-Diffusive Goodness

Bonaventure's *Disputed Questions on the Trinity* integrates the Pseudo-Dionysian axiom ***bonum est sui diffusivum*** ("the good is by nature self-diffusive") into Western trinitarian theology, arguing Augustine's psychological analogy and even Richard's love-based argument remain incomplete without it. He reframes the Father's *primitas* — not as a bare negative property (mere "unbegottenness") but positively, as ***fontalis plenitudo*** — "fontal fullness," the fecund, overflowing source from which everything else in the Godhead flows. The Son proceeds *per modum naturae* (by way of nature/intellect — generation); the Spirit proceeds from Father and Son together *per modum voluntatis* (by way of will — spiration).

## Aquinas: Processions, Relations, Subsisting Persons

The *Summa Theologiae* (Ia, qq. 27-32) proceeds by strict logical sequence: (1) **Processions** — the Word proceeds by way of intellect (generation); Love proceeds by way of will (spiration). (2) **Relations** — because each procession is really distinct and mutually opposed, four real relations arise: paternity and filiation (from generation), active and passive spiration (from spiration, though active spiration is common to Father and Son together). (3) **Persons** — because in the utterly simple God relation *just is* the divine essence, each divine person is a ***relatio subsistens*** — a subsisting relation, really identical with the one divine essence, distinguished from the others solely by relative opposition. Only three persons exist because active spiration, though shared identically by Father and Son, generates no relation of opposition between them, and so yields no fourth person. Aquinas explicitly **rejects** Bonaventure's positive "fontal fecundity" reading of the Father's innascibility, treating the Father's unbegottenness as a pure negation rather than a positive plenitude — a substantive disagreement between the two greatest scholastic systems.

## The Victorine School: Integrating Theologia and Oikonomia

**Hugh of St Victor** appropriates the triad Power/Wisdom/Goodness to Father/Son/Spirit, and reads creation's *vestigia Trinitatis* (traces of the Trinity) through a corresponding triad: Immensity, Beauty, Utility. **Richard of St Victor** formalizes the broader theory of **trinitarian appropriations** (later taken up systematically by Aquinas and, in the 20th century, by Emery — Part 7), and argues the Trinity's perfect internal self-sufficiency — its love already fully realized *ad intra*, in *condilectio* — is precisely what grounds creation's radical freedom and gratuity: God does not need creatures to have someone to love, so creation proceeds from unnecessitated grace alone. **Adam of St Victor** set the entire scholastic vocabulary — including the *filioque* itself — to liturgical Latin verse, showing how thoroughly trinitarian technical theology had become devotional and liturgical practice, not merely academic debate.
