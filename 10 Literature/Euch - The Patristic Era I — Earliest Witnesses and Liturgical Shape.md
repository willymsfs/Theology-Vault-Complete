# Unit 3 — The Patristic Era I — Earliest Witnesses and Liturgical Shape

## Didache 9-10 and 14

The **Didache** (late 1st/early 2nd c.) preserves the oldest Christian Eucharistic prayers — and they are strikingly close to Jewish table blessings, with thanksgiving over the cup *first*, then the bread. Two features matter:

- **A gathering image of great beauty**: "As this broken bread was scattered over the hills and, when gathered, became one, so let your Church be gathered from the ends of the earth into your kingdom."
- **Didache 14 calls it a sacrifice**: Christians are to confess their sins before breaking bread "so that your **sacrifice** (*thysia*) may be pure," and it cites **Malachi 1:11** directly. The sacrificial vocabulary is therefore present in the very earliest stratum, well before any developed theology of it.

## Ignatius of Antioch (d. c. 107)

Writing to churches while under guard on his way to martyrdom, Ignatius supplies two enduring phrases:

- The Eucharist is the ***pharmakon athanasias*** — "**the medicine of immortality**, the antidote that we should not die but live for ever in Jesus Christ" (*Eph.* 20).
- Against the Docetists: they "abstain from the Eucharist... because they do not confess that the Eucharist is **the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ**" (*Smyrn.* 7). Note the logic — Eucharistic realism follows directly from Incarnational realism. Deny that Christ truly had flesh and you must deny that the Eucharist is that flesh.

## Justin Martyr, First Apology 65-67 (c. 155)

The **earliest complete description of a Sunday Eucharist**, and an examinable text in its own right. Justin gives the shape: readings from "the memoirs of the apostles" and the prophets, homily by the presider, common prayers, kiss of peace, presentation of bread and wine mixed with water, the presider's extended prayer of thanksgiving "to the best of his ability," the people's **Amen**, distribution by deacons including to the absent, and a collection for orphans, widows, and those in need.

And his theological statement: "we do not receive these as common bread and common drink... the food over which thanks have been given by the prayer of the word that comes from him **is the flesh and blood of that incarnate Jesus**."

**Note the structure has not changed in nineteen centuries**: Word, Prayer, Communion, Charity. The 1969 Missal restored a shape Justin would recognise.

## Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

*Adversus Haereses* IV.17-18 develops the **oblation of the new covenant**: the Church offers the firstfruits of creation, not because God needs them but so that we may be neither unfruitful nor ungrateful. His decisive anti-Gnostic argument runs from Eucharist to resurrection: *"our bodies, receiving the Eucharist, are no longer corruptible, having the hope of the resurrection."* If matter can bear Christ, matter can be raised.

## Tertullian, Cyprian, and the Anaphora of Hippolytus

**Cyprian's Ep. 63** is the first sustained treatise on the Eucharistic cup: he insists on the **mixed chalice** (water and wine — the water is the people, the wine is Christ, and they cannot be separated) and speaks plainly of the priest offering a sacrifice "in the place of Christ" that imitates what Christ did.

The **anaphora in the *Apostolic Tradition*** (c. 215) is the single most influential ancient Eucharistic prayer in modern liturgy: it is the direct model for **Eucharistic Prayer II** of the 1969 Roman Missal. Learn its shape — dialogue, thanksgiving for creation and redemption, institution narrative, anamnesis, epiclesis, doxology — because it is the template against which every later development is measured.
