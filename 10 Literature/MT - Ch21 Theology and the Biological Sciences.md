# Ch. 21 — Theology and the Biological Sciences
*Chapter author: Celia Deane-Drummond*

## Core Idea
Deane-Drummond critiques the deterministic "gene myth" that reduces humanity to its genetic blueprint, proposing instead a "wisdom-based" theological ethic in which humans are relational, caring stewards in kinship with creation, not its dominating exploiters.

## Detailed Explanation
Darwin's Origin of Species (1859) first challenged static natural theology by showing species emerge through natural selection and that humans are biologically continuous with other hominids; Watson and Crick's 1953 discovery of DNA's structure then triggered the modern genetic revolution — the Human Genome Project, cloning, biotechnology — forcing theology to confront urgent new pastoral, ethical, and ecological questions. Central flashpoints include animal ethics (are animals owed "theo-rights" as vulnerable creatures God entrusted to human care?), the challenge of a 98% genetic overlap with primates to traditional readings of the imago Dei, and rival visions of the earth as a single, self-regulating system (James Lovelock's "Gaia").

## Example
The sharp public debate between Richard Dawkins's genetic reductionism in The Selfish Gene (humans as "survival machines" for self-replicating genes) and theologian Keith Ward's defence of purposive divine intelligence behind evolution (God, Chance and Necessity) played out in bestselling books and public lectures throughout the 1990s-2000s, making this one of the volume's most publicly visible science-theology confrontations.

## Illustration
The "gene myth" treats a human being like a pre-written computer program simply executing its code. Deane-Drummond's wisdom-based alternative treats a human being more like a jazz musician who has real, given musical training (genetics) but still improvises a genuinely new, responsible performance in relationship with the other musicians (the rest of creation) — biology sets real constraints without dictating the whole performance.

## Key Terms
- **Theo-rights**: Andrew Linzey's concept: because God created animals vulnerable to human domestication and exploitation, they deserve moral and structural protection analogous to that owed to children.
- **Speciesism**: The (originally secular) charge of giving humans automatic moral priority over other species — pressed by Peter Singer, and requiring theologians to re-examine the imago Dei in light of close human-primate genetic kinship.
- **Gaia Hypothesis**: James Lovelock's model of Earth as a single, self-regulating organic system — retrieved by ecofeminists as an image of divine immanence, though its most radical form risks casting humanity as an unwanted "parasite."
- **Omega Point**: Pierre Teilhard de Chardin's eschatological term for evolution's ultimate convergence point, where the cosmos's rising complexity reaches final union with God.
