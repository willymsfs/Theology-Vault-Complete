# Ch. 28 — African Theology
*Chapter author: Tinyiko Sam Maluleke*

## Core Idea
African theology is an enacted, oral, and performed practice — danced, sung, prayed — that serves local Christian communities directly rather than academic debate, seeking to free the African church from "prefabricated" Western dogma and become truly indigenous.

## Detailed Explanation
Written African theology emerged through ecumenical consultations in the 1950s, historically split between "African theology" (cultural inculturation, chiefly north of the Limpopo) and "South African black theology" (political-economic liberation under apartheid) — agendas that have since increasingly converged. The Kairos Document (1985), a prophetic theological challenge to apartheid-era church silence, and the post-Cold War rise of "theology of reconstruction" (moving from reactive critique toward constructive engagement with democracy and nation-building) mark two major turning points. Throughout, the underlying communal ethic ubuntu — "I am because we are" — supplies much of the discipline's philosophical grammar.

## Example
The Kairos Document (1985), signed by South African theologians during the apartheid state of emergency, publicly named "state theology" and even much "church theology" as complicit with injustice, and called instead for a "prophetic theology" of direct resistance — a concrete instance of African theology functioning as urgent, enacted political intervention rather than academic reflection.

## Illustration
Prefabricated housing looks like a real house from outside but was designed elsewhere, for a different climate and family, then simply shipped in and assembled. Maluleke's complaint about "prefabricated" Western dogma is exactly this: a theology designed for Europe's questions and climate, assembled in Africa without being grown from African soil — African theology's task is to grow a house that actually fits the ground it stands on.

## Key Terms
- **Translation Theologies**: Lamin Sanneh and Kwame Bediako's thesis that African Christianity's explosive growth is explained by the Gospel's translatability into vernacular languages, letting it speak in its own right rather than through a colonial tongue.
- **Theology of Reconstruction**: A post-Cold War paradigm (Jesse Mugambi, Charles Villa-Vicencio) urging proactive engagement with democracy, human rights, and nation-building, beyond reactive inculturation or liberation.
- **Ubuntu** (Zulu/Xhosa) — "communal personhood": "I am because we are": the traditional communal ethic underlying much contemporary African and South African theological reflection.
- **Enacted / Narrative Theology**: The conviction that oral, performed theology — prayer, drumbeat, lived grassroots witness — is theology in its own right, not merely raw material awaiting academic systematization.

## Connections

Engages John Mbiti in a foundational debate over what "African theology" even means.
