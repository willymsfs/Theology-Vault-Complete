# Unit 8 — Viaticum and the Order of the Last Rites

A source of persistent confusion among students and among the faithful, and worth stating precisely.

## "Last Rites" Is Not a Sacrament

The phrase names a **sequence**, not a single rite, and the sequence has a proper order:

| Order | Rite | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Penance** (or, if impossible, at least the sacrament's absolution) | Reconciliation first |
| **2** | **Anointing of the Sick** | The strengthening of the sick person |
| **3** | **Viaticum** | **The Eucharist as food for the journey** |

Where time is short, the order of *priority* differs from the order of *celebration*: the **most important sacrament for a dying person is Viaticum**, not anointing. The tradition is emphatic on this and modern pastoral practice often gets it backwards.

***Viaticum*** — from *via*, "way" — is literally "provision for the journey." Its priority follows from the whole logic of the Eucharist: it is Christ himself, and the *pignus futurae gloriae*, the pledge of the glory the dying person is about to enter. The proper form adds the words *"May the Lord Jesus Christ protect you and lead you to eternal life."*

Note that **c. 921** obliges the dying to receive Viaticum, and permits it even to those who have already received communion that day — an unusual dispensation that measures its importance.

## The Apostolic Pardon

A plenary indulgence at the hour of death, granted in the rite, remitting the temporal punishment due to sin (see the Penance course, Unit 7). Where no priest is available, the Church supplies it to anyone who has habitually prayed during life.

## Commendation of the Dying

Prayers, litanies, and brief scriptural verses to be said with and for the dying, and — importantly — the *Prayers for the Dead* immediately afterward. The rite provides for the family and the community to pray, not only the priest, which recovers something of the patristic pattern in which anointing and prayer for the sick were not exclusively clerical acts.

**Teaching point for pastoral practice.** When called to a dying person with limited time, the priest's order of operations is: **absolution → viaticum if the person can still swallow → anointing**. If the person is already unconscious, absolution is given conditionally and anointing conferred; viaticum is impossible. Knowing this sequence, and *why* viaticum outranks anointing, is a standard pastoral examination question.
