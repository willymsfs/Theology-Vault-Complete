# 15. Liberation ecclesiology.pdf

**TITLE**: "LIBERATION ECCLESIOLOGY: PRAXIS, THEORY, PRAXIS." By T. Howland Sanks, S.J. (Canisius College, Buffalo, NY) and Brian H. Smith, S.J. (Woodstock Theological Center, Washington, D.C.). Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 38, No. 1, 1977, pages 3–38 [142].

**MAIN SUBJECT**:
A study of the dialectical relationship between the Church's self-understanding and its socio-political action, utilizing the experience of the Chilean Church (1960–1975) to analyze the practical implementation, strategic choices, and structural limits of Latin American liberation ecclesiology [142, 143].

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   The sociology of knowledge applied to the praxis-theory-praxis loop in ecclesiology [142, 144].
*   The changing socio-political and religious context of 1960s Latin America [145-147].
*   The influence of papal social encyclicals and the 1968 Medellín Conference [148, 149].
*   The "uncentering" of the Church in the ecclesiologies of Juan Luis Segundo and Gustavo Gutiérrez [150, 151].
*   The Church as the "consciousness of humanity" rather than the sole channel of grace [151-153].
*   The integration of political liberation and the eschatological Kingdom [154, 155].
*   The concrete pastoral experience of the Chilean Church (parish decentralization, lay leadership, popular religiosity) [156-159].
*   The rise and subsequent condemnation of the "Christians for Socialism" movement [160-162].
*   The Chilean hierarchy’s pastoral letters on politics and Marxism [163-166].
*   Structural and strategic conclusions regarding the political neutrality and corporate weight of the Church [167-170].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Uncentering of the Church**: The radical shift where the Church ceases to view itself as the exclusive locus of salvation and instead reorients its mission as an instrument of service to humanity, recognizing that grace is actively present in all history and people [150, 151, 171].
2.  **Church as the Consciousness of Humanity (Segundo)**: The definition of the Church as that small, reflective portion of humanity which is fully aware of God's universal plan of salvation-liberation and is sent to live out that love consciously as a sign to the rest of the world [151-153].
3.  **Class Struggle as a Theological Category**: The adoption of Marxist social analysis by liberationists, asserting that in a radically divided world, the Church cannot remain neutral but must actively enter into class conflict to destroy systemic oppression [172-174].
4.  **Populist/Grassroots Church (Iglesia Popular)**: A model of Church emerging from below—specifically from the marginalized, poor, and exploited sectors—mediated by Basic Christian Communities (*CEBs*) [127, 129, 175].
5.  **The Dialectical Praxis-Theory-Praxis Methodology**: A theological method (influenced by Paulo Freire) that begins with the concrete historical situation, reflects critically on it using social science and scripture, and then feeds back into a revised, liberating praxis [142, 144, 176].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Ineffectiveness of Traditional Pastoral Work among the Poor**: The historical failure of the Chilean Church to evangelize the working classes (revealed by 5% Mass attendance) and the subsequent need to decentralize parish structures [157, 177].
2.  **The Obstacle of Popular Religiosity**: The difficulty of connecting the private, saint-centered, and often fatalistic devotions of traditional popular Catholicism with a critical, communal social consciousness [159, 178].
3.  **Prophetic Courage vs. Institutional Survival under Dictatorship**: The compromise and caution of the Chilean hierarchy under the repressive Pinochet military junta (silencing public criticism and resorting to private diplomacy to protect Catholic schools and institutions) [170, 179, 180].
4.  **The Threat of a Parallel Magisterium**: The hierarchy’s fear that organized movements of radical priests (like "Christians for Socialism") act as a "Church within the Church," establishing an independent teaching function that undermines the bishops' apostolic authority [162, 181].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "...man was not made for the Church (to enter and be saved); the Church was made for man." (Page 8 / Section II) - Context: Quoting Juan Luis Segundo's foundational thesis on the relational purpose of the Church [150].
*   "The Church is essentially a sign, 'placed here precisely and exclusively to pass on to men a certain signification...'" (Page 9 / Section II) - Context: Explaining the communicative nature of the Church in Segundo's theology [153].
*   "The growth of the Kingdom is a process which occurs historically in liberation... Without liberating historical events, there would be no growth of the Kingdom." (Page 13 / Section II) - Context: Quoting Gustavo Gutiérrez on the link between temporal liberation and the eschatological Kingdom [155].
*   "Christian love... can only bring about a transformation of society if it is 'lived out in antagonism and confrontation,' because the 'intensification of the class struggle... rules out any pretension to neutrality...'" (Page 30 / Section III) - Context: Quoting the radical political theology of the Christians for Socialism movement [173].
*   "We, for our part, state categorically that the class struggle is not the specific means which Christ gave his Church to contribute to the ultimate triumph of justice in the world." (Page 33 / Section III) - Context: Quoting the Chilean bishops' denunciation of class conflict [174].
*   "The Church has a certain institutional weight... and the association between the Church and the Christian Democratic party... has been much more than indirect." (Page 36 / Section IV) - Context: Critiquing the hierarchy's claim to absolute political neutrality [169].

**SUMMARY**:
Sanks and Smith explore the dialectical relationship between theological theory and pastoral praxis by examining the rise of Latin American liberation theology and its concrete, empirical application within the Chilean Church [142, 143]. They analyze how progressive theologians like Segundo and Gutiérrez "uncentered" the Church, re-orienting it as a Spirit-led sign of historical liberation for the marginalized poor [150, 151, 175]. However, the authors demonstrate that when this theory was translated into the radical "Christians for Socialism" movement, it ran headlong into structural and doctrinal limits: the bishops strongly rejected the theological validation of class struggle, the setting up of a parallel teaching authority, and the alignment of the Church with partisan Marxist parties [162, 173, 174, 181]. Sanks and Smith conclude that while the Chilean hierarchy struggled to maintain a consistent prophetic voice under the repressive Pinochet military dictatorship due to institutional survival instincts, the persistent, decentralized structures of local basic communities continue to serve as vital "zones of survival" where the liberating spirit of faith is kept alive by the poor themselves [179, 180, 182, 183].
