# Part 3 — Augustine's De Trinitate and Its Critics

## The Psychological Analogy

Augustine's *De Trinitate* (Books 9-15) seeks "vestiges" and "images" of the Trinity within the single human mind. An earlier triad — *mens, notitia, amor* (mind, its knowledge of itself, its love of itself) — gives way to a more developed one: ***memoria, intelligentia, voluntas*** (memory, understanding, will). The analogy is explicitly **not** meant to derive or prove the Trinity philosophically — the doctrine is already held by faith and scriptural authority; the analogy only illustrates a unity-in-trinity structure already believed. Augustine repeatedly warns the image is "enigmatic" (*per speculum in aenigmate*, 1 Cor 13:12), marked by real dissimilarity alongside the likeness.

## Natural Image versus Perfect Image

The mind remembering, understanding, and loving *itself* is the **natural image** — present in every human mind as such, structurally unlosable. The mind remembering, understanding, and loving *God* is the **perfect image** — achieved only through grace, turned toward its true object. Without the perfect image, the mind — though it never loses the natural capacity — remains, in Augustine's word, "foolish": a merely formal likeness without its proper content.

## Luigi Gioia: Trinitarian Theology as Epistemology

Gioia reframes the entire treatise as fundamentally an **epistemology of revelation**, not detached metaphysical speculation. Because God is essentially unknowable in himself, revelation is not incidental information delivery but the very means of reconciliation and knowledge — we come to know the Trinity only through the temporal "missions" of the Son (Incarnation) and the Spirit (Pentecost/indwelling). Books 4 (on the Mediator, Christ's saving work) and 13 (on faith and the Incarnation as remedy) are, on Gioia's reading, the treatise's structural hinge, not detours from the "real" philosophical argument of Books 8-15. Augustine's own epistemological maxim — later echoed by the Pontifical Council for Interreligious Dialogue (Part 8) — is: *"you do see the Trinity if you see charity"*: love, not intellectual ascent alone, is what actually opens the way to knowledge of God's inner life.

## Maarten Wisse: Augustine against Contemporary Trinitarian Revivals

Wisse's *Trinitarian Theology beyond Participation* deploys a close, polemical reading of Augustine against modern relational/social trinitarian revivals (Rahner, and by extension Moltmann and Zizioulas — Part 6). He charges that modern theologians "historicize" God's being, collapsing eternity into temporal becoming, and that relational/social models risk reducing the Trinity to a projected "social programme" or mirror of preferred human community — a form of **idolatry through projection**, anticipating Karen Kilby's later, sharper version of the same charge (Part 9). Wisse recovers from Augustine an insistence on the Trinity's radical, irreducible otherness, calling it — in Augustine's own spirit — an **"island of irrationality"** that resists being mapped onto any speculative relational metaphysics of communion. He pairs this apophatic caution with a soteriology centered on moral and epistemic restoration through grace and love, rather than an ontological, participatory *theosis*-model.
