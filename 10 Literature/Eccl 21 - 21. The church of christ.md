# 21. The church of christ.pdf

**TITLE**: "The Church of Christ, The Denomination, and the Council of Churches." By Ronald E. Osborn. Presentation as a Hoover Lecture on Christian Unity under the auspices of the Disciples Divinity House of the University of Chicago, November 20, 1958 [1, 2]. Published in *Encounter*, Vol. 20, pages 72–93 [1, 2].

**MAIN SUBJECT**:
An exploration of the theological and ecclesiological significance of the "denomination" and the "council of churches," utilizing the historic experience and polity of congregational communions to offer a coherent theological validation of ecumenical structures [3].

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   The historical shift from comparative confession-based ecclesiology to biblical ecumenical doctrines [4, 5].
*   The tension between biblical unity and the historical inevitability of denominations [2, 5].
*   An inclusive, congregation-based definition of the Church of Christ [6, 7].
*   The necessity and inevitability of religious institutions to sustain corporate Christian life [8, 9].
*   The theological definition and ecclesial role of the "denomination" as a unifying yet partial instrument [10-12].
*   The systematic sins of denominationalism: "churchism", self-aggrandizement, and programmatic stagnation [13-15].
*   The superior capacity of the "council of churches" to express visible catholicity, mission, and common confession [16-19].
*   Practical implications for the status of local councils, ministerial staff, and ecumenical leadership [20-23].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Congregational Church Authenticity**: The theological principle that any group of believers called by the gospel who bind themselves in a holy covenant under the Word constitutes a true and authentic Church of Christ, capable of administering valid sacraments and ordaining ministers [6].
2.  **The Denomination (as an Instrument of Unity)**: A limited, historical institution that paradoxically acts as a unifying agent for its affiliated congregations by delivering essential services that individual local churches cannot self-generate (such as founding missions, training clergy, and publishing) [10, 11].
3.  **Churchism**: The structural sin where a partial, truncated denominational institution usurps the role of the whole Church, absolutizes itself, demands supreme loyalty, and falsely pretends to be the perfect body of Christ [13].
4.  **The Council of Churches**: An inclusive association of local congregations (at the civic level) or national denominations (at the world level) that serves as a highly comprehensive visible manifestation of the Church's catholicity, mission, and common confession [16, 17, 24].
5.  **Conciliar/Catholic Episcopacy**: The concept that principal council officers carry a more truly episcopal and catholic oversight than denominational bishops because they serve all the churches in a spirit of comprehensive unity, making their orders widely acceptable [23].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Theological Anomaly of the Denomination**: Addressing the lack of a serious, formal "ecclesiology of the denomination," which historically led groups to either claim exclusive legitimacy or falsely equate their partial structure with the universal Church [25-27].
2.  **Anti-Institutionalism vs. Practical Necessity**: Overcoming the common, over-spiritualized "heresy" that rejects organized religious institutions, proving that even the most anti-institutional "free" churches inevitably build non-ecclesiastical agencies to survive [9, 28, 29].
3.  **The Suppressive Power of Programmatic Denominations**: Confronting how modern denominations have shifted from theological confessional tools to self-perpetuating programmatic engines that actively suppress local, cooperative ecumenical actions to protect their own institutional agendas [14, 15].
4.  **The Sub-Ecclesial Status of Councils**: Rejecting the fear that councils are mere "creatures" or "secular administrative chambers" of the denominations, arguing instead for the full imputation of theological, sacramental, and churchly status to conciliar structures [17, 24, 30].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "The existence of the ecumenical fellowship has posed the ecclesiological question for us. In the light of recent ecumenical experience, many of our inherited assumptions concerning the church have proved inadequate." (Page 72 / Passage 1) - Context: Explaining how the real-life experience of ecumenical unity challenges old sectarian assumptions.
*   "Any company of believers whom God has called together by the gospel and who have responded by the pledges of a holy covenant with him as a congregation under the Word constitute an authentic church of Christ..." (Page 74 / Passage 7) - Context: Providing a broad, congregational foundation for defining the true Church.
*   "No existing religious institution is coterminous with the whole church of Christ." (Page 80 / Passage 25) - Context: Rejecting the absolute claims of any single historic church or denomination to be the sole possessor of Christ's body.
*   "A denomination is a partial and truncated form of the church of Christ which is constantly under the temptation to regard itself as the whole church of Jesus Christ." (Page 85 / Passage 38) - Context: Quoting J. Robert Nelson to illustrate the corporate temptation toward the sin of "churchism."
*   "If our ecclesiology permits us in any legitimate sense to say of the denomination: 'church,' there is no sound reason why we may not say of the council: 'This is church.'" (Page 90 / Passage 53) - Context: Elevating the theological status of the council of churches to the same level as the denomination.
*   "The principal officer of a council of churches, if his is a ministerial and not merely a secretarial role, approaches more nearly to truly episcopal status, because of the catholicity of his office, than does the bishop of any one communion." (Page 92 / Passage 59) - Context: Proposing that ecumenical executive officers be consecrated as bishops to serve the whole Church.

**SUMMARY**:
Ronald E. Osborn argues that the classical theological systems have failed to provide a realistic ecclesiology for the actual structures that define modern church life: the denomination and the council of churches [1, 31]. Relying on congregational polity, he asserts that while any local covenant group under the Word is an authentic Church, it must inevitably utilize institutional structures to connect with the universal body of Christ [6, 8, 32]. While the denomination arose as a legitimate, unifying instrument to perform these joint tasks, it has degenerated into a self-perpetuating programmatic machine that falls into "churchism" and shatters Christian unity [10, 11, 13, 15]. Osborn concludes that the council of churches has emerged as a far more adequate, comprehensive, and catholic expression of the Church's true nature than the isolated denomination, demanding that theology formally recognize the council's genuine ecclesial and churchly status [16, 24].
