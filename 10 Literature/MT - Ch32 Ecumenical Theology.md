# Ch. 32 — Ecumenical Theology
*Chapter author: Mary Tanner*

## Core Idea
Ecumenical theology moved from comparing denominational differences to a "consensus/convergence" method retrieving shared biblical and patristic heritage, discovering churches already share a "real though imperfect communion" grounded in the Trinity's own relational life.

## Detailed Explanation
Rooted in early-twentieth-century missionary cooperation (Edinburgh 1910), the movement split into Faith and Order (doctrinal unity, Lausanne 1927) and Life and Work (practical social action, Stockholm 1925), merging into the World Council of Churches in 1948. Roman Catholic participation intensified after Vatican II's Unitatis Redintegratio (1964, Part V's Ecclesiology material). Landmark convergence texts include Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (BEM, 1982) and the Lutheran-Catholic Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (1999). The Santiago Conference (1993) made koinonia (communion) the central biblical paradigm, grounding church unity directly in the Trinity's relational life rather than in institutional uniformity.

## Example
The 1999 Lutheran-Catholic Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification formally resolved a doctrinal dispute dating to the sixteenth-century Reformation, declaring the two traditions' historic condemnations of each other on justification no longer applied — the clearest concrete achievement of the whole consensus method this chapter describes.

## Illustration
The old "comparative method" was like two families each insisting their own family recipe for a dish was the only correct one. The "consensus/convergence method" is like both families going back together to find the original grandmother's recipe both versions actually descended from — discovering a shared root deeper than their more recent differences.

## Key Terms
- **Anamnesis** (Greek: ἀνάμνησις) — "memorial": The biblical concept used in BEM to resolve eucharistic disputes: the Eucharist is not a repetition of Christ's sacrifice but a dynamic, present realization of its benefits.
- **Koinonia** (Greek: κοινωνία) — "communion": The central paradigm (Santiago 1993) grounding church unity in the relational, triune life of God rather than hierarchical or competitive structures.
- **Reconciled Diversity**: A model of unity where denominations achieve full visible communion while remaining distinct, recognizable bodies — plural fellowship over structural merger.
- **Oikoumene** (Greek: οἰκουμένη) — "the whole inhabited world": Konrad Raiser's term shifting ecumenical theology's starting point from narrow church history to the whole "household of life" God sustains.

## Connections
Barth participated directly in the WCC's founding (Amsterdam 1948, Part I). John Zizioulas's Orthodox trinitarian pneumatology (next chapter) shaped the Canberra (1991) and Santiago (1993) turns to koinonia. Moltmann's and Boff's "social Trinity" (Part II.A, Part V) underpinned the WCC's trinitarian panentheism. Chung Hyun Kyung's controversial 1991 Canberra presentation (Part V) dramatized the tension between patristic tradition and radical contextual hermeneutics within the ecumenical movement itself.
