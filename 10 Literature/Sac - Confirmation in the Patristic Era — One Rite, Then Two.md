# Unit 7 — Confirmation in the Patristic Era — One Rite, Then Two

For the first four centuries, what we now call Confirmation was **not a separate sacrament but the concluding moment of a single initiation liturgy**. Understanding *why* it separated is the whole point of this unit.

## The Original Unity

In the *Apostolic Tradition* the sequence runs: water bath, presbyteral anointing, **bishop's hand-laying and anointing on the forehead with thanksgiving**, first Eucharist. One night, one liturgy, one minister presiding. Tertullian (*De Bapt.* 7-8) describes the same: the unction, then the hand-laying "inviting and welcoming the Holy Spirit."

## Then East and West Diverge

The reason is entirely practical, not theological.

| | **East** | **West** |
|---|---|---|
| Who anoints? | The **presbyter**, immediately after baptism | The **bishop** reserves the hand-laying/anointing to himself |
| What preserves the episcopal link? | The ***myron*** (chrism) is consecrated by the patriarch/bishop | The bishop's physical presence |
| Result as dioceses grew | Unity of the rite **preserved** — infants receive Baptism, Chrismation, and Eucharist together to this day | Bishop cannot attend every rural baptism, so the second anointing is **delayed** until his visitation — producing **separation** |

**This is the crucial historical insight:** Confirmation became a separate sacrament in the West by **administrative accident**, not theological decision. The theology was then constructed *retrospectively* to explain a practice that already existed.

## Naming and Rationale

The term ***confirmatio*** appears in Gallican sources — the **Council of Riez (439)** and the **Council of Orange (441)**. The classic rationale comes from a **Pentecost homily attributed to Faustus of Riez (c. 460)**: baptism gives rebirth for innocence; confirmation gives ***augmentum ad gratiam*** — an increase of grace and **strengthening for battle** in the world. From here comes the enduring "**soldier of Christ**" imagery. Passages from this homily later entered the ninth-century **Pseudo-Isidorian (False) Decretals** under the name of a pope, which lent the argument spurious ancient authority that the scholastics inherited in good faith.

**Illustration.** Imagine a family firm where the founder personally signed every contract. As the firm expanded across the country, the founder could no longer be everywhere. The Eastern branch solved it by letting local managers sign using ink the founder had personally blessed. The Western branch insisted the founder sign in person — so contracts piled up waiting for his annual tour, and eventually people came to think of "the signing" as a wholly separate transaction from the deal itself. That is the history of Confirmation in one paragraph.
