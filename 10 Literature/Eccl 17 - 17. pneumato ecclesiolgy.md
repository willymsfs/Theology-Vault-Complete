# 17. pneumato ecclesiolgy.pdf

**TITLE**: "TOWARDS A PNEUMATO-ECCLESIOLOGY: EXPLORING THE PNEUMATOLOGICAL UNION BETWEEN CHRIST AND THE CHURCH." By Greg Liston (PhD Candidate, AUT University and Carey Graduate School). Published in *Colloquium*, Vol. 44, No. 1, 2012, pages 31–58.

**MAIN SUBJECT**: The development of a "Pneumato-Ecclesiology" from a Spirit-Christological foundation, analyzing the pneumatological union between Christ and the Church to derive analogical insights about the Church's nature, identity, and mission.

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   Spirit Christology as a necessary prerequisite to understanding Christ's identity and his relationship to the Church.
*   The Spirit as the unitive mediator that makes the Church the body of Christ.
*   Five pneumatological parallels between Christ and the Church: conceiving, sustaining communion, conforming, directing/empowering, and displaying/mediating.
*   The mutual love model of the Trinity and David Coffey's "incarnate" Spirit theory.
*   Anhypostasia/enhypostasia and onto-relationships in the theology of Thomas F. Torrance.
*   The Church as both the Body and the Bride of Christ in the Spirit.
*   Gary Badcock's theology of otherness and the indivisibility of the Church.
*   Narrative and dramatic ecclesiologies (Stanley Hauerwas, Kevin Vanhoozer, Hans Urs von Balthasar, Michael Horton).

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Pneumato-Ecclesiology**: An ecclesial framework that views the Church's nature, identity, and mission through a pneumatological lens, starting from a prior Spirit-Christology [19, 20].
2.  **Anhypostasia and Enhypostasia of the Church**: Adapted from Torrance; anhypostasia means the Church as the Body of Christ has no independent existence or self-identity apart from atonement and communion through the Spirit, while enhypostasia means the Church is given a real, concrete historical existence and function precisely through incorporation into Christ [21].
3.  **Onto-Relationships**: Torrance’s concept asserting that human existence is fundamentally relational; individuals in the Church undergo an ontological transformation through the Spirit, becoming "personalized persons" whose human being is anchored in the very being of God [22-24].
4.  **Quantitative vs. Qualitative Conformation**: The distinction where Christ’s growth is quantitative (he always had a perfect relationship with the Father but grew within human limits), whereas the Church's transformation is qualitative and individual (individuals must be born again) before being conformed communally [25-27].
5.  **Cruciform Church**: The structural and functional shape of the Church, which participates in Christ's own ministry by pouring out its life in suffering obedience and reconciliation [28, 29].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Neglect of the Spirit in Christology-Ecclesiology Links**: Correcting traditional models that link Christ and the Church purely historically or metaphorically while ignoring the active, unitive role of the Spirit [19, 30].
2.  **The Eucharistic vs. Reformed Bodily Models**: Navigating the tension between Karl Barth’s Reformed view (the Church is fully and always the body of Christ) and John Zizioulas’s Eucharistic view (the Church is rhythmically the body), proposing that the Church is both *being* and *becoming* the body [31-33].
3.  **The Sinfulness and Holiness Dialectic**: Overcoming Karl Barth's sharp division between a holy and sinful Church by deploying a "thick" pneumatology where the Spirit, as seen on the cross, bridges what is alien and unites the sinful parts of the Church with Christ [34-37].
4.  **Ecclesial Mediation Extremes**: Avoiding the dual errors of rejecting all ecclesial mediation (Barth's witness-only model) or overemphasizing it (Zizioulas's model of the Church as the exclusive mediator of the Spirit), establishing instead a real but limited role for the Church's narrative practices [38-41].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "The Church as the body of Christ cannot be considered apart from this [the Spirit's presence], for the ecclesiastical ‘body’ of Christ is something that is mediated by the work of the Spirit, and cannot exist without the Spirit." (Page 32 / Passage 100) - Context: Quoting Gary Badcock on the mediated nature of the Church's body.
*   "...the role of the Spirit being sent to the Church is specifically and uniquely determined by Christ’s humanity and its relation to the human nature of the Church." (Page 36 / Passage 111) - Context: Grounding the specific, Christ-patterned character of the Holy Spirit's mission.
*   "The Church then, should be understood precisely as the pneumatically-enabled relational union between our humanity and that of the incarnate Son’s." (Page 37 / Passage 114) - Context: Formulating a primary ontological definition of the Church.
*   "Through the Spirit, the Church both is the body of Christ, and is becoming what she already is." (Page 45 / Passage 138) - Context: Explaining the dynamic, eschatological tension of the Church’s somatic reality.
*   "For just as the Spirit united Christ with the Father on the cross – a Christ who (at that point at least) was credited with our sinful nature and actions – so the Spirit unites both the sinful and the sanctified parts of the Church with Christ, and consequently with each other." (Page 49 / Passage 150) - Context: Establishing the indivisible constitution of the Church despite its historical failures.
*   "...'the first social task of the Church… is to be the Church.'" (Page 56 / Passage 170) - Context: Quoting Stanley Hauerwas's narrative ecclesiology on the Church's primary task.

**SUMMARY**:
Greg Liston constructs a comprehensive "Pneumato-Ecclesiology" by drawing direct, analogical connections from a Spirit-Christology through five pneumatological parallels. He argues that the Church's identity is fundamentally relational and tripartite—comprising the pneumatological union between Christ and the human community—which is sustained in a filial communion with the Father. By analyzing the Church's life as both the Body and Bride of Christ, Liston highlights a dynamic, eschatological state of being and becoming increasingly Christ-like in appearance. Utilizing Torrance's onto-relationships and Badcock's theology of otherness, he rejects sharp dualistic divisions between the holy and sinful Church, concluding that the Spirit makes the Church an indivisible, cruciform, and narrative community that participates subordinate to Christ's own ongoing ministry of reconciliation.
