# 19. structure of ecclesiology.pdf

**TITLE**: "THE STRUCTURE OF A SYSTEMATIC ECCLESIOLOGY." By Neil Ormerod (Lecturer in Systematic Theology, Catholic Institute of Sydney). Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 63, 2002, pages 3–30.

**MAIN SUBJECT**: Proposing a fivefold structure for systematic ecclesiology—defining it as empirical/historical, critical, normative, dialectic, and practical—modeled on the theological method of Bernard Lonergan and the historical dialectics of Robert Doran.

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   The crisis of identity and fragmentation of contemporary ecclesiology (Avery Dulles's models vs. competing root metaphors).
*   Methodological definitions: empirical/historical, critical, normative, dialectic, and practical ecclesiology.
*   The integration of the social sciences (sociology of structure, identity, authority, and change) without sociological reductionism.
*   Reorienting the standard Weberian account of "institutionalization" as a product of practical intelligence.
*   Two trajectories of social and cultural change: Trajectory 1 (practical insight to cultural change) and Trajectory 2 (cultural change to practical insight).
*   The emergence of threefold ministry (bishop, presbyter, deacon) in the early Church (analyzed via *1 Clement* and the letters of Ignatius of Antioch).
*   A critique of "communion ecclesiology" for its idealizing, non-historical, and functionalist tendencies.

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Empirical and Historical Ecclesiology**: A methodology that treats the concrete, historical reality of the Church (from its origins to its diverse contemporary institutional forms) as its primary range of data, rather than working from an abstract, idealized "Platonic" model [p. 4-6].
2.  **Normative and Evaluative Ecclesiology**: An approach that provides explicit norms for evaluating the historical changes, structures, and teachings of the Church, grounded in the teleological orientation of the Church toward the Kingdom of God [p. 9].
3.  **Dialectic Ecclesiology**: A critical framework that seeks to analyze and understand systemic breakdowns, distortions, and failures in the Church's pursuit of its goal (such as the reduction of the Kingdom to a Platonic "salvation of souls") [p. 10].
4.  **Practical Ecclesiology**: The ultimate, active function of systematic ecclesiology that diagnoses "sickness," proposes concrete courses of action, and guides pastoral planning, communication, and praxis [p. 10].
5.  **Institutionalization as Practical Intelligence**: An alternative to the Weberian "necessary evil" view; it asserts that institutions are developed by practical intelligence to meet the recurrent, internal needs of a human community (e.g., self-reproduction, identity preservation, and regulation of change) [p. 16-17].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Abstract Metaphysical Idealism of Ecclesiology**: Critiquing works that present an idealized *communion* or *perichoresis* model that has little to do with the actual, historical concrete data of the Church, thereby masking real divisions [p. 5].
2.  **Sociological Reductionism vs. Suspicion of Social Sciences**: Finding a middle ground between those who fear the social sciences reduce theology to sociology, and John Milbank’s critique of the social sciences as "policing the sublime" [p. 11].
3.  **The Polarization of Ministry's Origins**: Resolving the historical-critical debate over whether the early Church's threefold ministry was a pure "human invention" born of historical contingency (Trajectory 1) or a "divine establishment" directly instituted by Jesus (Trajectory 2) [p. 15, 21-22].
4.  **The Romanticism of Communion Ecclesiology**: Critiquing modern communion ecclesiology for its romantic, utopian, and functionalist character, which lacks the capacity to account for systemic conflict, sinfulness, and historical change [p. 27-28].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "My answer is that systematic ecclesiology should be empirical/historical, critical, normative, dialectic, and practical." (Page 3) - Context: Outlining the central programmatic thesis of the entire article.
*   "...the range of data with which ecclesiology deals must be concrete, 'not simply with the imagination of an ecclesial ideal.'" (Page 5) - Context: Quoting John Milbank to demand a historically grounded range of data.
*   "The Church is a human community significantly constituted through the actions, intentions, and decisions of a multiplicity of human beings..." (Page 8) - Context: Grounding the Church in historical human agency.
*   "Institutions are a product of practical intelligence, to meet the particular recurrent needs of a human community." (Page 17) - Context: Defending the value and necessity of institutional structures against the standard Weberian decline account.
*   "Of all the theologies, ecclesiology must be the most practical." (Page 10) - Context: Emphasizing that systematic reflection must ultimately guide pastoral planning and change.
*   "...mission, the mission of the Church envisaged in terms of its teleological orientation to the kingdom of God." (Page 27) - Context: Emphasizing that the mission of the Church, rather than inner communion, serves as the primary organizing principle.

**SUMMARY**:
Neil Ormerod proposes a rigorous, programmatic structure for systematic ecclesiology, arguing that it must be empirical, critical, normative, dialectic, and practical. Grounding his method in Bernard Lonergan and Robert Doran, he insists that ecclesiology must treat the actual, historical concrete Church—and its history of reflection—as its primary data set. He critiques "communion ecclesiology" for being an idealized, romantic, and functionalist framework that cannot account for conflict, sinfulness, or change. By examining the emergence of the threefold ministry in *1 Clement* and Ignatius of Antioch, Ormerod demonstrates how the social sciences can be critically integrated to show that Church structures are products of the community's "practical intelligence," guided by the Holy Spirit to meet the recurrent needs of preserving its identity and mission in service to the Kingdom of God.
