# Ch. 19 — Postmodern Theology
*Chapter author: Graham Ward*

## Core Idea
Ward distinguishes "liberal" postmodern theologies, which dissolve transcendence into a secular, nihilistic play of language, from "conservative" postmodern theologies (including his own Radical Orthodoxy), which use postmodern critique itself to retrieve premodern, liturgical, and patristic accounts of a transcendent, triune God encountered in "distance" and "alterity."

## Detailed Explanation
Developed amid globalized consumer capitalism and the French "linguistic turn" (Derrida, Foucault, Lyotard), postmodern theology takes shape against the Holocaust's and Hiroshima's shattering of modernity's confidence in linear progress and rational mastery. Ward — who co-founded the British Radical Orthodoxy movement with John Milbank and Catherine Pickstock — argues postmodern deconstruction, rightly used, need not erase God but can instead open a genuinely non-foundationalist space for contemplating divine transcendence precisely through absence and distance, rather than through modernity's confident, totalizing God-concepts.

## Example
Mark C. Taylor's "a/theology," celebrating the postmodern "death of God" as the final erasure of any transcendent anchor for meaning, and Radical Orthodoxy's opposite move — using the very same postmodern critique of secular reason's pretensions to re-assert a liturgical, participatory, eucharistic account of all reality — are the volume's two starkest real-world uses of identical philosophical tools toward opposite theological ends.

## Illustration
Postmodern critique is like a set of tools that can be used either to demolish a house or to strip it back to sound foundations for renovation. "Liberal" postmodern theology (Taylor, Altizer) uses deconstruction to demolish transcendence altogether; "conservative" postmodern theology (Ward, Milbank, Marion) uses the very same tools to strip away modernity's own overconfident additions and rebuild on older, patristic foundations.

## Key Terms
- **Rhizome**: Gilles Deleuze's botanical image, adopted by Ward, for postmodern knowledge as a root-stock growing in many directions with no single centre or origin.
- **A/theology**: Mark C. Taylor's radical, secularized postmodern discourse celebrating the "death of God" as erasing any transcendent anchor of meaning.
- **The Icon vs. The Idol**: Jean-Luc Marion's phenomenological distinction: the idol reflects only human conceptualization back to the viewer, while the icon offers a "gift of the invisible," drawing the viewer into encounter with divine distance and kenotic love.
- **Radical Orthodoxy**: The movement (Milbank, Pickstock, Ward) combining Augustinian theology, postmodern critique, and radical politics to read all creation as liturgical participation in the triune God.

## Connections
Taylor, Altizer, and Winquist radicalize Tillich's and Bultmann's existentialism (Part I) into secularized "death of God" theology. Shares Lindbeck's cultural-linguistic non-foundationalism and Barth's Christological realism (Part I, Part II.D), which conservative postmodernists expand with explicit liturgical and metaphysical commitment. Jean-Luc Marion and Michel de Certeau retrieve the Greek Fathers to think a God "without Being." John Milbank and Catherine Pickstock are Ward's direct collaborators in Radical Orthodoxy, itself building on Henri de Lubac's nature-grace critique (Part I).
