# 9. domestic church.pdf

**TITLE**: "Domestic Church: A New Frontier in Ecclesiology." By Florence Caffrey Bourg (Ph.D., Boston College; Assistant Professor of Religious Studies, College of Mount Saint Joseph, Cincinnati, Ohio) [53, 54]. Published in *Horizons*, Vol. 29, No. 1, 2002, pages 42–63 [53, 55].

**MAIN SUBJECT**: The post-Vatican II theological recovery, development, consensus, and unresolved ecclesiological implications of the "domestic church" concept, defining the Christian family as the smallest, most basic local manifestation of the Church [53, 56, 57].

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   The patristic and scriptural precedents of the household conversion and house church [58, 59].
*   The re-emergence of the term "domestic church" at Vatican II through Bishop Pietro Fiordelli [60, 61].
*   Comparative evaluation of magisterial documents (*Evangelii Nuntiandi*, *Familiaris Consortio*, *Follow the Way of Love*) [62-64].
*   The debate between grounding the domestic church in the sacrament of marriage vs. the sacrament of baptism [65-67].
*   Karl Rahner’s sacramental theology applied to the family as the "smallest of local churches" [68, 69].
*   The priority of symbolic/analogical interpretations of the domestic church over juridical categories [70, 71].
*   Inclusivity of non-traditional family structures (single-parent, blended, interreligious, and childless families) [72, 73].
*   The public, social mission of the family as an active, "frontline agent" of justice rather than a passive beneficiary [74, 75].
*   The ecumenical cutting edge of interdenominational domestic churches [76, 77].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Domestic Church (Ecclesia Domestica)**: The theological designation emphasizing that the Christian family is not merely a passive recipient of parish ministry, but is the smallest, living cell and concrete manifestation of the Church caught in its daily routines [56, 62].
2.  **Sacramental Vision**: The shared faith-informed ability of family members to recognize, name, and experience the sacred within the seemingly mundane, ordinary moments of daily life (mealtimes, chores, parenting, conflicts) as instruments of encounter with God [78, 79].
3.  **Liturgy of the World**: Karl Rahner's theological premise that God’s self-communication and grace are constantly present and active under the guise of ordinary secular experiences, which are then brought to explicit public expression in official church liturgies [80, 81].
4.  **Church-as-Sacrament**: The model viewing the Church as a visible sign and historical instrument of God's grace; the domestic church acts as a local specification of this model, functioning as a "saving community" in its neighborhood [81, 82].
5.  **Indicative vs. Subjunctive Family Theology**: The methodological tension between deductive, classicist approaches that define rigid, idealized forms of family and label struggling ones as "not true families" (indicative), and inductive, historically conscious approaches that observe actual, diverse family structures as real locations of grace (subjunctive) [65, 72].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **Lay Empowerment vs. Hierarchical Control**: Confronting the tension where some use "domestic church" to reinforce paternal authority and clerical supervision (Stebbins, May), while others use it to authorize broader lay consultation, leadership, and decision-making [83].
2.  **The Marriage/Baptism Paradox**: Overcoming the exclusion of non-traditional families (single-parent, blended) by shifting the theological foundation of the domestic church from sacramental marriage to the universal call of baptism [66, 67, 73].
3.  **The Systematic Ecclesiology "Blind Spot"**: Remedying the historical neglect of the domestic church by major ecclesiologists who dismissively relegate family studies purely to practical or pastoral theology [84, 85].
4.  **The Eucharistic Sharing of Interchurch Families**: Addressing the painful, unresolved eucharistic barriers faced by interdenominational domestic churches, which are otherwise on the cutting edge of ecumenical unity [76, 77, 83].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "The family is, so to speak, the domestic Church." (Page 42 / Passage 221, quoting Lumen Gentium #11) - Context: Documenting the singular, compromise reference that planted the conciliar seed at Vatican II.
*   "As Christian families, you not only belong to the Church, but your daily life is a true expression of the Church." (Page 43 / Passage 226, quoting the U.S. Bishops' *Follow the Way of Love*) - Context: Setting down the core theological shift regarding the holiness of mundane family life.
*   "What happens in the individual parish... renders unmistakably and really present... the existence of the whole Church... It is... the smallest of local churches, but a true one, the Church in miniature." (Page 57-58 / Passage 281, quoting Karl Rahner) - Context: Providing the strongest systematic justification for treating the family as a genuine local church.
*   "The church of the home can live and grow in every family." (Page 57 / Passage 278, quoting *Follow the Way of Love*) - Context: Articulating the inclusive, baptismal grounding that embraces diverse family configurations.
*   "A family's commitment to simplicity of life and a sharing with the poor can speak much more forcefully than the witness of priests or religious." (Page 51-52 / Passage 258, quoting Leonard Doohan) - Context: Pointing out the distinct, uncompromised public witness of family life.
*   "Assessing the living, growing faith of Christian families is far more complex than drawing parish lines, and so it is misleading to consider domestic church to be primarily a juridical category..." (Page 62 / Passage 295) - Context: Arguing for the priority of symbolic meaning over rigid canonical classifications.

**SUMMARY**:
Florence Caffrey Bourg traces the post-conciliar revival of the "domestic church," arguing that the Christian family serves as a foundational and active local embodiment of the Church [53, 56, 86]. She contrasts deductive, classicist family models with inductive, baptism-grounded models, showing how modern theology has expanded the concept to recognize single-parent, blended, and interreligious households as authentic locations of grace [65, 72, 73]. Drawing on Karl Rahner's sacramental theology and the "Liturgy of the World," Bourg asserts that ordinary family routines provide a spiritual discipline where members encounter Christ in bodily form, acting as frontline, public agents of the Church's social and ecumenical mission [74, 81]. She concludes that systematic ecclesiologists must shed their long-standing blind spot toward the family, integrating the domestic church's symbolic and relational richness to renew the institutional structures and patterns of leadership in the wider Church [71, 85, 87].
