# Unit 1 — Foundations: Why the Church?

## Do We Need the Church?
The modern critique of the Church runs on three tracks. The Enlightenment made religion a private, rational affair and questioned why salvation should need an institution at all. Marx went further, calling religion the "opium of the people" and reading the Church as an ideological tool that reconciles the oppressed to their oppression. Secularism simply treats organized religion as an obsolete stage humanity is outgrowing.

The theological response is not to deny the Church's real, historical failures, but to insist that the Church is not incidental to salvation. It is the concrete, historical community through which the Triune God continues to mediate covenant relationship in history. Ecclesiology therefore asks a different question than sociology of religion does: not merely what the Church visibly is, but what the Church reveals about God's own plan of salvation.

## The Meaning of Ekklesia
The Hebrew qahal Yahweh ("assembly of the LORD") named Israel gathered at Sinai and at later covenant renewals — a people constituted by God's call, not by kinship or geography alone. The Septuagint (LXX) translated qahal as ekklesia, from EK ("out of") + KALEO ("to call"): those called out and called together. Four connotations run through this etymology: freedom (called out of bondage, on the Exodus pattern), unity (one assembly, not a scattering of factions), self-rule (a body capable of its own internal governance and voice), and participation (each member has a place, not merely a passive presence).

## The Jesus Movement
Before there was a Church with offices and creeds, there was a movement gathered around Jesus, marked by freedom (table fellowship that crossed purity boundaries), equality (disciples called brothers and sisters rather than master and servants, cf. Mk 10:42-45), sharing (a common purse, Lk 8:1-3, anticipating Acts 2:44-45), and diakonia — service, embodied above all in the foot-washing that Unit 4 will return to in John's Gospel.

## Illustration
Compare Deuteronomy 4:10 ("the day you stood before the LORD your God at Horeb... 'Assemble the people before me'") with Acts 2 at Pentecost. Both qahal and ekklesia name a people called out and constituted by a divine, revelatory event — not a voluntary club that people join on their own initiative, the way one joins a guild or a club.

## Indian Context
The "assembly" image resonates with the Indian panchayat tradition of village gathering for collective deliberation — but ekklesia's radical claim is that its members are gathered not by kinship or caste but by God's call, crossing boundaries an ordinary panchayat would preserve.
