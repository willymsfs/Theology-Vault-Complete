# Unit 11 — Structural Renewal: Hierarchy, Collegiality, Papal Primacy

## Twofold Power, Threefold Hierarchy
The power of orders is received sacramentally, through ordination — the capacity to teach, sanctify, and govern that flows from the sacrament itself. The power of jurisdiction is the canonical authority to exercise that capacity over a determinate territory or group, granted and regulated by church law. LG 18-29 treats the threefold hierarchy of bishops, priests, and deacons in this light; bishops possess the fullness of the sacrament of Order.

## Episcopal Collegiality: Three Conciliar Arguments
(1) Christ's collegial commissioning: Christ gave authority to Peter and the Twelve together as a stable body, and this collegial authority passes to the whole college of bishops with the pope as its head. (2) The sacramentality of episcopal consecration: LG 21 teaches that bishops receive "the fullness of the sacrament of Order" directly in consecration itself, so their offices come from the Spirit through the sacrament, not by delegation from the pope. (3) Communion of local churches: since the universal Church is a communion of local churches (Unit 10), and each bishop is the visible centre of unity of his own local church, collegial authority is structurally grounded in that communion, not superimposed on it from above.

## Structures of Collegiality
The Synod of Bishops (established by Paul VI in 1965 as a standing consultative body meeting periodically with the pope); episcopal conferences — in India, the CBCI (Catholic Bishops' Conference of India) and regional bodies, and internationally the FABC (Federation of Asian Bishops' Conferences); and the principle of subsidiarity (decisions made at the most local level competent to make them, with higher authority intervening only when necessary).

## Papal Primacy: From Leo the Great to Ut Unum Sint
Rome's special authority, recognized from the early Church, was articulated in explicitly juridical, "vicar of Peter" terms by Leo the Great at Chalcedon (5th c.), pressed further by Gregory VII's Dictatus Papae (Unit 5), and dogmatically defined at Vatican I. Vatican II, without retracting Vatican I's definitions, reframes the papal office within collegiality: the pope is head of the college, not separate from or above it in isolation, and his ministry is re-described in terms of service and unity rather than mere jurisdiction. John Paul II's 1995 encyclical Ut Unum Sint went further still, explicitly inviting other Christian leaders and theologians into dialogue "to find a way of exercising the primacy which... is nonetheless open to a new situation" — an unprecedented admission that how the papacy is exercised remains open to ecumenical reconsideration, even while its substance is not in question.

## Illustration
The relationship between the pope and the college of bishops in LG is like a conductor and an orchestra rather than a solo performer with an accompanying audience: the conductor truly leads and is indispensable to the performance's unity, but the music is made by the whole orchestra together, each player exercising a real, non-delegated skill (their "power of orders") under the conductor's real, coordinating authority (his "power of jurisdiction" as head of the college).

## Indian Context
The CBCI and its regional counterparts, together with the FABC, are the concrete, lived structures through which collegiality is exercised in the Indian and wider Asian Church — bodies through which bishops address local pastoral realities (caste, poverty, interreligious relations) collectively rather than each in isolation.
