# Unit 11 — Practical and Pastoral Application

## Common Pastoral Scenarios

| Scenario | Response |
|---|---|
| Family asks for "last rites" | Establish what is needed: absolution, viaticum, anointing — and in what order the person's condition permits. Viaticum has priority for the dying |
| Person is unconscious | Confer conditionally (c. 1005); presume implicit request if they were a practising Catholic (c. 1006) |
| Priest arrives after death | The sacrament is not conferred on the dead; but where death is recent and uncertain, conditional anointing is permitted. Then pray the Prayers for the Dead with the family |
| Person recovers and later falls ill again | **Repeat it** (c. 1004 §2) — this is a positive norm, not a mere permission |
| Scheduled major surgery | Anointing is appropriate (*CCC* 1515) |
| Elderly person, no acute illness but notable frailty | Appropriate — *SC* 73 names **old age** explicitly, independent of illness |
| A person in manifest grave sin, unrepentant | c. 1007: not to be conferred — but note that this concerns *obstinate persistence*, and any sign of repentance changes the case |
| No olive oil available | Another plant oil may be used (*Sacram Unctionem Infirmorum*); a priest may bless it in necessity (c. 999) |
| Non-Catholic Christian requests it | Governed by c. 844; permitted for Eastern Orthodox, and for others in danger of death under the conditions there specified |

## The Indian Context — Healing Devotion and the Sacrament

This is where the sacrament meets one of the most vigorous realities of Indian Catholic life, and the vault's material on **Velankanni** — the Basilica of Our Lady of Good Health, the "Lourdes of the East" — is directly relevant.

**What devotees do.** Pilgrims deposit **wax and metal *ex-votos*** — replicas of limbs, organs, infants, houses, boats, agricultural tools — offered *ex voto suscepto*, "from a vow undertaken." Vows follow the Tamil ***nadai*** pattern: a conditional covenant pledging a specific offering contingent on divine intervention, whose non-fulfilment after healing carries real moral weight. Fulfilment is embodied — **tonsure**, sea-bathing, prostrate advance and *anga pradakshinam* (rolling the body along the path).

**What they seek.** Not chiefly *moksha* but concrete deliverance: healing from paralysis and disease, children, success in examinations, security of livelihood. As Vasudha Narayanan observes, Hindus come to such shrines for **"time-sensitive reasons"** rather than for ultimate liberation.

**Why non-Christians come.** Narayanan's analysis is that pilgrims seek localised divine power — ***shakti***, ***barakat*** — and are drawn to sites established as repositories of it; theological pluralism means that petitioning at a Christian shrine is no betrayal of their own faith. **Selva J. Raj** described the shared ritual space as "**a form of ongoing, authentic interreligious dialogue, arising organically from lived experiences rather than abstracted theologies**." **Susan Bayly's** sharper historical judgement is that at these healing shrines "**the would-be converters were themselves 'converted'**; they were made over into tutelaries and cult saints so as to fit in with south India's existing forms of worship."

**The theological question this poses for the sacrament.** **Susan Visvanathan** notes that Indian Catholics move comfortably between two parallel ritual spaces — **"canonical"** rituals (the official sacraments, performed by the priest inside the church) and **"domestic" or vernacular** rituals (tonsure, sea-bathing, *nadai* vows, managed by family or catechist). The pastoral risk is that the sacrament of anointing is experienced as belonging wholly to the first sphere — clerical, Latin-derived, associated with dying — while the actual religious energy around **healing** lives entirely in the second.

**The opportunity.** The 1972 reform's recovery of the sacrament as a prayer for *healing and strengthening*, addressed to *Christus medicus*, is precisely the bridge. Two further connections are available and underused:
- The patristic pattern (Unit 3) of **blessed oil taken home and used by the household** is strikingly close in structure to the domestic sphere Visvanathan describes — a genuine precedent, not an accommodation.
- Caesarius of Arles's pastoral instinct (Unit 3) — offering the Church's blessed oil as the true alternative to charms and sorcerers — is the same pastoral situation, thirteen centuries earlier.

**The caution.** Michael Amaladoss's methodological rule applies with full force: inculturation must begin from **actual living symbols** — and here the living symbols of healing devotion are abundantly documented. What it must not become is either a dismissal of popular practice as superstition or an uncritical absorption of transactional religiosity into the sacrament.
