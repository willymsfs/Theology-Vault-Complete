# Unit 10 — Contemporary Debates

**1. Where is the threshold?** *SC* 73 and c. 1004 set it at the *beginning* of danger from sickness or old age, and *CCC* 1515 explicitly includes before surgery. But how serious is "serious"? Practice varies from the very restrictive (near-death only, in effect reverting to the medieval position) to the very broad (any illness, any distress). The genuine risk at the broad end is that the sacrament becomes a general blessing and loses its specific character; at the restrictive end, that the reform is quietly nullified.

**2. Communal anointing services.** Now common, and a genuine expression of the ecclesial dimension (*LG* 11). The disputed question is whether the requirement of serious illness is being observed, or whether such services in practice anoint whole congregations regardless of condition — which would make the sacrament something other than what the rite says it is.

**3. Who may anoint?** *CIC* c. 1003 restricts valid administration to priests. The historical argument for extending it — at least to deacons, or in mission territories to lay ministers — appeals directly to **Innocent I** (Unit 3), who permitted all Christians to use the blessed oil. The counter-argument is that the sacrament's forgiveness-of-sins effect ties it to the priestly power of absolution, and that the discipline is settled. Note that **c. 999** already permits a *priest* to bless the oil in a case of necessity, a modest decentralisation of what was once strictly episcopal.

**4. Mental illness, addiction, dementia.** Whether and how the sacrament applies where the "illness" is psychiatric rather than somatic. Most pastoral guidance now answers yes for serious cases, but the question of the recipient's use of reason (c. 1004 §1, c. 1005) and of implicit request (c. 1006) requires care.

**5. The unconscious and the recently dead.** c. 1005's rule — *in doubt, administer* — governs. Where death is certain, the sacrament is not conferred, but the boundary is medically uncertain and the Church's instruction is to err toward conferral, conditionally.

**6. Anointing and the healing ministry.** The relation between the sacrament and charismatic healing prayer is theologically underdeveloped and pastorally live, particularly in Africa, Latin America, and Asia, where independent healing ministries are a major force. The sacrament is not a healing service, but the modern rite prays genuinely for recovery, and pretending otherwise concedes the ground.

**7. Ecumenical dimension.** The Orthodox ***euchelaion*** — ideally **seven priests, seven epistles, seven gospels, seven anointings** — retained the healing orientation throughout, never developing an "extreme unction," and is administered widely, including to the healthy in Holy Week in some traditions. Anglicanism recovered anointing in the twentieth century. The Catholic reform of 1972 substantially narrowed the historic gap with both.
