# Unit 4 — Patristic Controversies — Validity, Worthiness, and Celibacy

## The Donatist Crisis and Sacramental Character

The controversy is treated fully in the Sacraments course (Unit 2), but its consequences for **orders** are specific and must be stated here.

The Donatists held that sacraments conferred by *traditores* — bishops who had handed over the scriptures under persecution — were void, and therefore **re-ordained** clergy coming from the Catholic side.

**Augustine's answer** distinguishes **validity** (which depends on Christ) from **fruitfulness** (which depends on charity and communion), and grounds the unrepeatability of orders in the **character** — the indelible mark, which he compares to a soldier's brand (*nota militaris*): a deserter still bears it, which is exactly why he can be punished as a deserter rather than merely as a civilian.

**Three permanent consequences for Holy Orders:**
1. Ordination is **unrepeatable**; re-ordination is impossible, not merely forbidden.
2. An unworthy minister ordains and celebrates **validly**.
3. Orders can be **valid but illicit**, and a validly ordained man can be **without jurisdiction** — the distinction that governs every modern case from schismatic consecrations to *Apostolicae Curae*.

## Simony

Named for Simon Magus (Acts 8:18-24), who tried to buy the power of conferring the Spirit. The purchase or sale of ordination and ecclesiastical office is condemned from Chalcedon (c. 2) onward, and becomes one of the two great targets of the Gregorian Reform (Unit 5).

## Clerical Celibacy — The Historical Record

Students routinely get this wrong in both directions, so state it precisely.

| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| **c. 306, Elvira, c. 33** | The earliest surviving legislation: married clergy are to **abstain from their wives**. Note it presumes a *married* clergy and requires **continence**, not celibacy |
| **325, Nicaea** | Reportedly declined to impose universal continence; the tradition attributes the intervention to **Paphnutius**, a confessor bishop — the story's historicity is disputed but its currency is itself evidence of the debate |
| **692, Trullo (Quinisext)** | The **Eastern settlement**, still in force: **married men may be ordained** deacon and presbyter and continue in marriage; **bishops must be celibate** (chosen from monastics or widowers); no marriage *after* ordination |
| **1123 and 1139, Lateran I and II** | The **Western settlement**: Lateran II declares the marriages of clerics in major orders not merely illicit but **invalid**, making celibacy a condition of the clerical state |
| **1563, Trent, Session XXIV** | Reaffirms celibacy against Reformation objection |
| **1983, CIC c. 277** | Clerics are obliged to perfect and perpetual continence for the kingdom |

**The point to master:** celibacy is a **discipline of the Latin Church, not a doctrine**. It is not required for validity, it does not obtain in the Eastern Catholic Churches, and exceptions already exist in the Latin Church (permanent deacons; married Anglican and Lutheran clergy received under the Pastoral Provision and the Ordinariates). Any exam answer treating celibacy as doctrinally necessary to priesthood is simply wrong.
