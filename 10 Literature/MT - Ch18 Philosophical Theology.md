# Ch. 18 — Philosophical Theology
*Chapter author: Ingolf U. Dalferth*

## Core Idea
Dalferth reframes philosophical theology as a diverse family of activities using philosophical analysis to clarify conceptual puzzles about belief in God, tracing the collapse of decontextualized natural theology under Hume's and Kant's critiques and its replacement by "recontextualized" programs grounded in the actual grammar of lived religious practice.

## Detailed Explanation
Philosophical theology arose alongside seventeenth-century mechanistic science, seeking a rational theistic consensus independent of Europe's bloody post-Reformation confessional conflicts. This decontextualized "philosophical theism" collapsed under David Hume's empiricist skepticism and Immanuel Kant's refutation of speculative metaphysics in the late eighteenth century. Twentieth-century philosophical theology then had to answer logical positivism's dismissal of religious language as cognitively meaningless, increasingly giving way to a "philosophy of religion" that starts from concrete religious practice rather than abstract proofs of an abstract God.

## Example
Kant's move from trying to prove God's existence to treating belief in God as a necessary postulate of practical (moral) reason — we must act as if a just, moral order underwrites reality, even though we cannot prove it theoretically — still structures how many introductory philosophy-of-religion courses frame the difference between "knowing" and "reasonably trusting" that God exists.

## Illustration
Old-style natural theology tried to prove God the way a lawyer proves a case in court: marshal evidence, construct an airtight argument, force a verdict. After Hume and Kant showed the "evidence" could never be airtight, philosophical theology shifted to something more like learning a language fluently within a community that already speaks it — you come to trust and understand God-talk by living inside a tradition's practice, not by winning a courtroom argument from outside it.

## Key Terms
- **Philosophical Theism**: The decontextualized natural-theology project attempting to prove an abstract, omnipotent, omniscient, benevolent God using universal reason alone.
- **Critical Philosophical Theology**: Kant's alternative: belief in God evaluated not as theoretical knowledge but as a necessary postulate of practical, moral reason.
- **Transcendental Theism**: The idealist approach evaluating God as the necessary transcendental condition making human knowing and believing possible at all.
- **Speculative Philosophical Theology**: A realist reaction to transcendental idealism treating God as the living, absolute Subject who actively integrates finite history into Godself (process/panentheist thought).

## Connections
Hume and Kant supply the foundational "crisis" every subsequent philosophical theologian must navigate. Schleiermacher converted Kant's critical turn into the foundations of modern "fundamental theology." Charles Hartshorne and Whitehead's process theism (also central to the Revisionists and Liberals chapter, Part II.C) supplies the leading "speculative" alternative. Wittgenstein's linguistic philosophy underlies the modern "philosophy of religion" school (D. Z. Phillips), reading God-talk as a rule-governed practice within a language-game.
