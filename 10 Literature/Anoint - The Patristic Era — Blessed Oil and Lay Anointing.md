# Unit 3 — The Patristic Era — Blessed Oil and Lay Anointing

The patristic evidence is genuinely surprising to students formed on the modern rite, and it is the strongest historical argument for the twentieth-century reform.

## The Focus Was the Blessing of the Oil, Not the Anointing

Early liturgical books contain **prayers for blessing oil** rather than rites of anointing. The **Apostolic Tradition** (§5) provides a blessing of oil immediately after the Eucharistic Prayer, asking that it give strength to all who taste it and health to all who use it. The **Euchologion of Serapion of Thmuis** (4th c., Egypt) contains several such prayers, one asking that the oil become "a means of removing every disease and every sickness."

**The key point**: the *blessing* was the reserved, hierarchical act; the *application* was not.

## Innocent I's Letter to Decentius of Gubbio (416)

This is the single most important patristic text on the sacrament, and it should be quoted in any historical essay.

Answering a bishop's query, Pope Innocent I explains that the oil of James 5 is **blessed by the bishop**, and then states that it may be used not only by priests but **by all Christians** — "*omnibus christianis*" — in their own need and in the need of their households. He adds one restriction: it is **not to be given to penitents**, since they are excluded from all the sacraments while under canonical penance (see the Penance course, Unit 3).

Three consequences follow, all of them significant:

1. **Self-anointing and lay anointing were normal and legitimate** for centuries. The oil was taken home; the sick anointed themselves, or family members anointed them. It was also drunk.
2. The **reservation of the sacrament to priests is a later development**, not an apostolic given.
3. The restriction of the oil from penitents is the first hint of the fateful link between anointing and penitential status which would later drive the sacrament to the deathbed (Unit 4).

## Other Patristic Witnesses

- **Caesarius of Arles** (6th c.) urges the sick to go to the church for anointing with blessed oil rather than to amulets, charms, and sorcerers — an early instance of the Church offering a sacramental alternative to folk healing practice, a pastoral dynamic still entirely live in many cultures (Unit 12).
- **Bede** (8th c.), commenting on Mark 6:13 and James 5, is the first major Latin writer to treat the two texts together as describing a single ecclesial practice.

**Teaching point.** For roughly the first eight centuries the sacrament was: **oil blessed by the bishop, applied by anyone, for the recovery of the sick.** Every element of that description would be reversed by the Carolingian reform — which is why *Sacrosanctum Concilium* 73 can present the modern change as a **restoration** rather than an innovation.
