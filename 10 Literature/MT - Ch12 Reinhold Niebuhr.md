# Ch. 12 — Reinhold Niebuhr
*Chapter author: William Werpehowski*

## Core Idea
Reinhold Niebuhr's "Christian Realism" rejects both liberal sentimentalism (believing love alone resolves political conflict) and dogmatic insularity, insisting that because sinful self-interest inevitably taints even collective moral action, justice requires the realistic, sober use of power, coercion, and resistance — even though sacrificial love (agape) remains history's ultimate, if humanly "impossible," norm.

## Detailed Explanation
Raised in the German Evangelical Synod, Niebuhr's thirteen years as a pastor in industrial Detroit (1915-28), amid the harsh realities of Henry Ford's factories, convinced him his earlier "simple little moral homilies" were useless against real structural injustice — an experience he chronicled in Leaves from the Notebook of a Tamed Cynic. He taught at Union Theological Seminary, New York (1928-60), and in the 1930s broke publicly with the sentimental optimism of the Social Gospel movement. His central diagnosis: human beings stand at the anxious intersection of finitude and freedom, and that anxiety tempts every person and, especially, every nation or social group toward prideful self-assertion cloaked in self-righteous, universalizing rhetoric.

## Example
Niebuhr's Christian Realism directly shaped mid-century American foreign policy thinking — statesmen including George Kennan explicitly drew on his argument that national self-interest must be checked by countervailing power and honest self-suspicion rather than either naive moralism or cynical amorality, making him arguably the most politically influential American theologian of the twentieth century.

## Illustration
Sentimental liberalism, for Niebuhr, is like negotiating a hostage situation by simply asking the captor to love their neighbour — sincere, but dangerously naive about how power and self-interest actually operate at scale. Christian Realism instead insists you still need a negotiator, leverage, and a plan — while never forgetting that love, not power, remains the true measure of what a just outcome should look like.

## Key Terms
- **Christian Realism**: A framework insisting justice requires realistic engagement with power and coercion, since sin and self-interest persist even in collective, well-intentioned political action.
- **Anxiety**: The inevitable human condition at the meeting-point of finitude and freedom — not itself sin, but the permanent temptation toward prideful self-assertion instead of trust in God.
- **Impossible Possibility**: Niebuhr's description of sacrificial love (agape): the ultimate norm judging and inspiring all history, yet never fully achievable within history's actual power-struggles.
- **Ideological Taint**: The self-deceiving tendency of social groups and nations to dress up self-interested claims to power in self-righteous, universal moral language.

## Connections
Shares Tillich's method of correlating biblical symbols with existential human analysis (Part I). Engaged in a lifelong public debate with his brother H. Richard Niebuhr (previous chapter). Paul Lehmann criticised his Christology as too cross-focused and under-pneumatological. Fiercely opposed by pacifists John Howard Yoder and Stanley Hauerwas (also this volume's chapter author for H. Richard Niebuhr) for sacralizing state violence and compromising the Church's distinct, nonviolent witness.
