# Ch. 01 — Karl Barth
*Chapter author: Daniel W. Hardy*

## Core Idea
Barth reconstructed Protestant theology around a single object: God's own self-initiated, sovereign act of self-revelation in Jesus Christ, known only through grace and never through general human religious experience.

## Detailed Explanation
Barth broke with the liberal Protestant theology of his teachers (Harnack among them) when, in 1914, they signed a declaration supporting the Kaiser's war policy. That collapse of "the whole world of theological exegesis, ethics, dogmatics, and preaching" he had been trained in drove him back to the Bible and produced his explosive Commentary on Romans (1919, revised 1922), which insisted on the "infinite qualitative difference" between God and humanity. Barth went on to draft the Barmen Declaration (1934) resisting Nazi co-optation of the German churches, was dismissed from his post at Bonn for refusing a loyalty oath to Hitler, and spent the rest of his career at Basel writing the multi-volume Church Dogmatics. Theologically, his "actualism" locates God's very being in God's free, gracious act toward humanity in Christ, rather than in a static set of religious truths humans discover on their own — a decisive rejection of the whole liberal Protestant project of grounding theology in human experience or culture.

## Example
Barth's 1934 refusal to accept that the German Christian movement could fuse the Gospel with National Socialist ideology, and his central role in drafting the Barmen Declaration's confession that Christ alone — not the Führer, not "blood and soil" — is the Word of God the church must obey, is the clearest real-world enactment of his theology: revelation, not culture, is the Church's only norm.

## Illustration
Imagine a courtroom where the judge's verdict does not depend on what the defendant already believes about justice, but arrives as a sovereign, external decision that redefines the situation entirely. For Barth, God's revelation in Christ works the same way: it does not confirm what humans already sensed about God, it breaks in and reorders everything, including what "religion" itself means.

## Key Terms
- **Actualism** — "the theology of the act": God's identity/being is located strictly in God's concrete, ongoing act of self-revealing relation to humanity in Christ, not in a fixed set of static attributes.
- **Krisis** (Greek) — "judgment/crisis": The ultimate judgment of the sovereign, transcendent God over every human cultural, historical, and religious achievement or pretension.
- **Nachdenken** (German) — "thinking-after": Theology's proper task: rational reflection that follows and responds to what God has already done in revelation, rather than reasoning toward God from human premises.
- **Das Nichtige** (German) — "nothingness / the null and void": Evil understood not as a positive force but as a rejected, defeated "impossible possibility" — real in its damage, yet without ultimate standing before God's grace.

## Connections
Barth is the paradigm case of Ford's "type two" theology ("faith seeking understanding": Christian self-description as primary, yet always rethought in engagement with the modern world) — a type shared, in the book's own account, by Bonhoeffer, de Lubac, von Balthasar, Torrance, and most postliberal theologians. He directly shaped the systematic work of Jüngel, Jenson, and Gunton (Unit 9 below), and Torrance's "scientific theology"; his relation to von Balthasar (over the analogia entis) and his early influence on the young Bonhoeffer are two of the volume's central inter-chapter threads.
