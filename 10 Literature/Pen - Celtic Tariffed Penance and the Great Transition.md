# Unit 5 — Celtic Tariffed Penance and the Great Transition

This is the largest single structural change in the history of any sacrament, and it came not from a council but from Irish monks.

## The Irish System

Irish Christianity developed outside the Roman civic structure, in a monastic and tribal setting where the **anmchara** ("soul friend") was a normal spiritual institution. From this grew a radically different penitential practice:

| | **Canonical penance** | **Tariffed (Celtic) penance** |
|---|---|---|
| **Repeatable?** | **Once in a lifetime** | **Repeatable without limit** |
| **Public or private?** | Public state (*ordo paenitentium*) | **Entirely private** |
| **Minister** | The **bishop** | Any **priest**, often a monk |
| **Penance determined by** | The bishop's judgement | A **fixed tariff** in a written handbook |
| **Sequence** | Confession, penance, *then* absolution | Confession, absolution, then satisfaction |
| **Lifelong disabilities?** | Yes | No |

## The Penitentials

Handbooks listing sins with their assigned penances, sometimes with startling specificity. The major ones: **Finnian** (6th c.), **Columbanus** (c. 600), **Cummean** (7th c.), and **Theodore of Canterbury** (late 7th c.), whose *Penitential* was the most influential in England.

Their governing principle is medicinal and is stated explicitly by Columbanus: ***contraria contrariis sanantur*** — "**contraries are cured by contraries**." The talkative are given silence, the greedy fasting, the violent restraint. The confessor is a **physician of souls** prescribing a remedy, not merely a judge imposing a fine.

**Commutation and its abuses.** Because tariffs could run to years of fasting, systems of **commutation** (*arreum*) developed, allowing substitution — so many psalms recited, so many alms given, so many days on bread and water — in place of longer periods. Genuine pastoral flexibility shaded easily into a market in penances, and the eventual monetisation of that logic is one root of the indulgence controversy (Unit 7).

## The Transition on the Continent

**Columbanus and the Irish missionaries** brought the practice to Gaul and beyond around 600. The reaction was initially hostile: the **Third Council of Toledo (589)** actually **condemned** the practice of repeated confession and absolution as an "abomination" and a departure from canonical tradition.

Yet within two centuries the argument was over — not because it was won theologically, but because the new system *worked* and the old one had ceased to function. The **Council of Chalon-sur-Saône (813)** recognised both forms as legitimate, and the private, repeatable form steadily became universal.

## Two Consequential Shifts

**1. Absolution moves before satisfaction.** In canonical penance, reconciliation *followed* completed penance. In the tariffed system, the priest absolved and the penitent then performed the assigned satisfaction. This reordering is what makes possible the scholastic account in which **satisfaction remedies the temporal punishment remaining after the guilt is forgiven** (Unit 6) — and therefore, downstream, the whole theology of indulgences.

**2. The formula changes from deprecatory to indicative.** Early formulas were **prayers**: "May God absolve you," *Absolutionem et remissionem tribuat tibi omnipotens Deus*. From roughly the twelfth century the West adopts the **indicative**: ***"Ego te absolvo a peccatis tuis"*** — "**I** absolve you." Aquinas defends the indicative form as expressing that the priest acts as an instrumental cause in Christ's person, not merely as an intercessor. The Eastern churches retain deprecatory forms to this day, and this remains a genuine difference of theological accent.

**Illustration.** Think of the shift as the difference between a court that sentences you and a clinic that treats you. Canonical penance was juridical and one-shot: you were tried, sentenced, and readmitted, once. The Irish system was medical and ongoing: you came back whenever you were ill, and the physician had a manual of prescriptions. The Church never formally chose between these models — it ended up carrying both, which is why the sacrament still speaks simultaneously of a tribunal and of a hospital.
