# Unit 7 — Ecumenical Councils, Conciliarism, and the Convocation of Vatican II

## The Theology of a Council
An ecumenical council gathers the world's bishops, in union with the pope, exercising the Church's highest teaching and legislative authority — grounded theologically in the college of bishops as successors of the apostolic college gathered around Peter (cf. Acts 15, the "Council of Jerusalem," as prototype). Both Küng and Rahner stressed, from different angles, that a council is not a mere administrative assembly but a privileged event of the whole Church's self-reflection under the Spirit. The Catholic Church recognizes twenty-one ecumenical councils, from Nicaea I (325) to Vatican II (1962-65).

## Conciliarism and Its Condemnation
Conciliarism, prominent in the fifteenth century, held that supreme authority in the Church resides in an ecumenical council rather than in the pope alone. The Council of Constance's decree Haec Sancta (1415) asserted a general council's authority even over the pope in matters of faith, schism, and reform; Frequens (1417) mandated regular future councils. Conciliarism developed partly in response to the Western Schism (1378-1417), when rival papal claimants divided Latin Christendom. It was effectively repudiated at Vatican I, which located supreme, ordinary, immediate jurisdiction in the pope, independent of the college's consent.

## Vatican II's Convocation
Announced unexpectedly by John XXIII in 1959 and guided through its four sessions (1962-65) first by him and, after his death in 1963, by Paul VI, Vatican II was animated by aggiornamento — "bringing up to date": not doctrinal change but a fresh, pastoral re-presentation of the faith to the modern world. It was genuinely a "world Church" council in ways earlier councils were not: bishops attended from every continent, not overwhelmingly from Europe as at Vatican I; non-Catholic observers were formally invited; and lay auditors, including women for the first time, participated in the sessions.

## Illustration
Vatican I met like a household drawing up a single, urgent legal document under threat from outside (the loss of the Papal States); Vatican II met more like an extended family reunion taking stock of who it now is across the whole world — less a legal act, more a communal self-examination.
