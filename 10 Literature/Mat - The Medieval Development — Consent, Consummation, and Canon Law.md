# Unit 4 — The Medieval Development — Consent, Consummation, and Canon Law

## The Central Question: What Makes a Marriage?

The twelfth century saw a genuine and consequential dispute between two schools:

| School | Position |
|---|---|
| **Gratian** and the Bolognese canonists | Marriage is *initiated* by consent but **completed by consummation**; before consummation it is not fully a marriage |
| **Peter Lombard** and the Paris theologians | ***Consensus facit nuptias*** — **consent alone** makes the marriage, following Roman law. Otherwise, they argued, the marriage of Mary and Joseph would not have been a true marriage |

**Alexander III's synthesis** (later 12th c.) resolved it and remains Catholic law:

- **Consent alone** creates a true and valid marriage (***matrimonium ratum***).
- **Consummation** makes it **absolutely indissoluble** (***matrimonium ratum et consummatum***).
- Hence a *ratum non consummatum* marriage — valid but unconsummated — **can** be dissolved by papal dispensation, a possibility that still exists (CIC c. 1142).

**Why this matters.** The distinction explains an otherwise puzzling feature of Catholic teaching: an unconsummated sacramental marriage can be dissolved, while a consummated one cannot. It also means that in Catholic theology the **spouses' consent** is the efficient cause of the marriage — from which follows the doctrine that **the spouses themselves are the ministers of the sacrament** (Unit 5).

## The Problem of Clandestine Marriage

Because consent alone sufficed, a valid marriage could be contracted **privately, with no witnesses and no priest**. The consequences were severe: secret marriages that one party later denied; bigamy; disputes over inheritance and legitimacy that the courts could not resolve; and the seduction of women by promises of marriage that were later contested.

The Church consistently declared clandestine marriages **illicit but valid** — which solved nothing practically. **Lateran IV (1215)** required the public announcement of banns, but did not make the omission invalidating. The problem was only solved by Trent (Unit 6), and solving it required the Church to do something it had never done: **make its own ceremony a condition of validity**.

## Impediments

Medieval canon law developed an elaborate system, most notoriously the prohibition of marriage within **seven degrees of consanguinity and affinity** — which, in small populations, made almost any local marriage technically impedable and turned dispensations into an instrument of power and revenue. **Lateran IV reduced the prohibition to four degrees**, an unusual instance of a council relaxing rather than tightening discipline, precisely because the wider rule had become unworkable and corrupting.
