# 18. reconsidering ecclesiology feminist perspective.pdf

**TITLE**: "RECONSIDERING ECCLESIOLOGY: FEMINIST PERSPECTIVES." By Natalie K. Watson (Ripon College Cuddesdon, Oxford). Published in *Theology & Sexuality*, Vol. 14, 2001, pages 59–77.

**MAIN SUBJECT**: The construction of a critical, constructive feminist ecclesiology that transcends the binary between inventing new models of the Church (the women-church movement) and maintaining a critical engagement with traditional, patriarchal ecclesiological heritages.

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   The historical and theological limitations of adapting Latin American liberation theology for feminist ecclesiology.
*   The foundational feminist claim that "women are church and have always been church" (Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza).
*   Critiquing the disembodied, asexual character of traditional, elite male ecclesiologies (Karl Barth, Paul Tillich, Vatican II).
*   The anthropological distinction between "literate" (male/theoretical) and "pre-literate" (female/lived/devotional) ecclesial practices (Susan Starr Sered).
*   Reclaiming the power centers of patriarchal ecclesiology: Word, Sacrament, and the presence of Christ.
*   Mary Daly's post-Christian theory of "sisterhood as cosmic antichurch" vs. a constructive "re-membering" of the Church.
*   Reinterpreting sacramentality as the sexuate, bodily performance of Christ's story (Louis-Marie Chauvet, Gerard Loughlin).
*   Justice as a mark of the Church and Mary Grey's recovery of prophecy and mysticism.

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Women are Church**: A foundational claim asserting that as fully baptized members of the body of Christ, women possess the intrinsic authority and agency to participate in the writing of ecclesiology and name what the Church is [42, 43].
2.  **Literate vs. Pre-literate Ecclesial Practice**: "Literate" refers to the highly intellectualized, theological, and canonical debates dominated by male clerics; "pre-literate" refers to the everyday, embodied practices, pieties, and spaces of faith that women have carved out historically, which are equally essential to the Church's reality [44-46].
3.  **Sexual Difference (Sexuate Existence)**: The poststructuralist recognition that human existence is inherently bodily and gendered, which must be integrated into systematic ecclesiology because a disembodied, asexual view of the "Body of Christ" is contradictory and alienating [47-49].
4.  **Embodied Performance / Storytelling Community**: Adapting Gerard Loughlin and Louis-Marie Chauvet, the understanding of the Church as a community that physically performs and inscribes the narrative of Christ within sexuate, historical human bodies rather than repeating a static, respectful mimesis [49-51].
5.  **Re-membering the Church**: A constructive alternative to Mary Daly’s radical exodus; it means women reclaiming their membership in the body of Christ, discovering their history of both oppression and empowerment, and physically embodying the sacramental presence of the Church in their bodies [52].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Limitations of the Liberationist Paradigm**: Rejecting the uncritical use of liberation theology because its focus on economic equality often subsumes sexual difference and ignores the unique, gendered dimensions of women's experiences [48].
2.  **The Exclusion of Women from Representing Christ**: Rebutting patriarchal arguments that exclude women from active sacramental leadership and representation by asserting that all sexuate human beings (male and female) are called to represent Christ to each other [53, 54].
3.  **The Disembodied Obsession of Mary Daly**: Critiquing Daly's post-Christian "sisterhood" for becoming an abstract, disembodied space of deconstruction that reduces women’s history to mere victimhood and ignores the transformative, constructive power of the female body in the Church [52, 55].
4.  **Gendered Symbolism and Nuptial Heterosexism**: Challenging traditional, patriarchal gender binaries (like a masculine priest representing an active Christ, and a submissive, feminine bride representing the passive Church) which reinforce unequal power structures [48, 54].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "...feminist ecclesiology seeks to shift the perspective from speaking about 'Women in the Church' or even 'Women and Church' to the much stronger claim that 'Women are church'." (Page 60-61 / Passage 182-183) - Context: Defining the core theological goal of feminist ecclesiology.
*   "Traditional ecclesiologies, such as those of authors like Karl Barth and Paul Tillich or the documents of the Second Vatican Council are to be re-read from a perspective that assumes women as readers..." (Page 61 / Passage 185) - Context: Proposing a critical, constructive re-reading of dominant patriarchal texts.
*   "The word of God has always been the site of fundamental ambivalence and we can notice a parallel between women's experiences as readers of Scripture and women's experiences in being church." (Page 66 / Passage 198) - Context: Describing the dual history of oppression and empowerment surrounding the Bible.
*   "Telling Christ's story, performing it as being church, does not take place in a vacuum... We come to telling Christ's story as the people we are..." (Page 73 / Passage 216) - Context: Emphasizing the bodily, contextual reality of theological performance.
*   "...the 'performance of the church should be understood as creative invention rather than as respectful mimesis'." (Page 73 / Passage 217) - Context: Quoting Gerard Loughlin to defend structural and liturgical creativity in the Church.
*   "Word and sacrament are categories which need to be reclaimed and re-interpreted by women." (Page 75 / Passage 221) - Context: Explaining how women must redefine the core structural practices of the Church.

**SUMMARY**:
Natalie K. Watson outlines a critical and constructive feminist ecclesiology that rejects the false dichotomy between radical separation and passive submission. Critiquing the asexual, disembodied manualist and post-conciliar traditions, she insists that because the Church is the "Body of Christ," it must be understood within a framework of sexual difference. Watson retrieves the "pre-literate" historical practices of women to show that women have always been active, transformative subjects of the Church. Rather than exiting the Church like Mary Daly, Watson calls for a robust "re-membering" of the Church, reinterpreting the Word, Sacrament, and Christ's presence as embodied, sexuate performances. She concludes that by integrating prophecy, mysticism, and a commitment to justice, the Church can be transformed into an open storytelling community where all bodily subjects are empowered with authority.
