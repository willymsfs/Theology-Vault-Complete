# Synodality — Tribal Governance, Worldview and the 2021-2024 Consultation

Source: *Synodality* (Indian Edition, 2025), via NotebookLM.

## Tribal governance as natural synods
[[Vincent Ekka]] (pp. 49-56) and [[Chinliankhup Stanislaus]] (pp. 399-408) document: the Khasi *Durbar
Kur* and *Durbar Shnong*; the Tangkhul Naga *Awunga* restrained by the *Hangva* council; Kuki
chieftainship bound by customary law; Munda/Ho/Santhal/Oraon village assemblies reaching decisions by
consensus, never majority vote — arriving independently at the Acts 15 method. See
[[Indigenous Indian Models of Synodality]] for the full schema.

## Tribal worldview
Commensality (communal eating after ritual, a levelling mechanism), egalitarian kinship, the sacred
grove (*Sarna*, *Jaherthan*), and the tribal monotheistic vision (*Dharmes*, *Singbonga*, *Thakurjiv*)
constitute what Ekka calls a "natural synodal soil."

## Practice in tribal parishes
Empirical figures: the *Mahila Sangh* (women's council) active in 74.4% of tribal parishes — more
widespread than the parish council itself (69.8%); *Yuva Sangh* (youth) in 67.4%. Catechists function
as the *Aliqui* of the tribal Church, leading Sunday worship across scattered villages.

## The 2021-2024 consultation on the ground
Bishop Paul Toppo (Raigarh) ran diocesan workshops and neighbourhood meetings; Bishop Telesphore Bilung
(Jamshedpur) made personal parish visits, and observed synodality "breaking the clericalism and
ritualism among the priests"; Archbishop Anil Couto (Delhi) formed a special committee to ensure the
tribal voice was heard, and noted that tribal migrants from Chotanagpur (some 70% of Delhi's Catholic
population) "brought synodality to the Church of Delhi." Consultation surveys nonetheless found the
poor (33.8%), the differently-abled (15%) and women (2.3%) still structurally under-represented — even
within participatory tribal parishes.
