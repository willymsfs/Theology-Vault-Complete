# Ch. 22 — Theology and the Social Sciences
*Chapter author: Richard H. Roberts*

## Core Idea
Roberts traces sociology's rise as theology's ambitious "aspirant successor" as queen of the sciences, critiques theology's defensive, self-isolating survival strategies, and proposes a "somatic" (embodied) theology reconnecting the Gospel to the real, global-local conditions of late modernity.

## Detailed Explanation
Marx, Weber, and Durkheim's modern social science was built on the assumption that religion was a premodern residue to be critically explained away as humanity matured — a marginalization physically enacted when Wilhelm von Humboldt and Schleiermacher structurally separated the theology faculty from the humanities at the founding University of Berlin. Roberts situates his own proposal within the wider "crisis" of the late-twentieth-century human sciences (Husserl's Krisis, Foucault's archaeology of the "death of God and man") and a "detraditionalized" postmodern condition, arguing theology too often responds by retreating into institutionally self-protective isolation rather than genuine engagement.

## Example
Roberts's sustained critique of John Milbank's Radical Orthodoxy — which he calls "postmodern quasi-fundamentalism" for entirely repudiating secular social theory as "heresy" rather than engaging real ethnographic and sociological evidence about actual human suffering — is the chapter's sharpest concrete case study in what Roberts considers theology's temptation to defensive self-isolation.

## Illustration
Roberts's "somatic theology" is like insisting a doctor examine the actual patient's body rather than only consulting an old textbook diagram of an idealized body. Where much modern theology retreated into abstract, self-protecting argument (the textbook), Roberts wants theology to get back into direct, embodied contact with the real, suffering, socially-embedded human beings modern sociology actually studies.

## Key Terms
- **Geisteswissenschaften** (German) — "human/spiritual sciences": Wilhelm Dilthey's early-20th-century configuration of the human sciences, which Roberts notes structurally repeated theology's exclusion from the secular academic humanities.
- **Somatic Theology**: Roberts's proposal for a theology of committed physical embodiment, connecting with humanity's shared, globally distributed processes of socialization rather than abstract linguistic system-building.
- **Mauvaise Foi** (French) — "bad faith / false consciousness": Existentialist term Roberts applies to a theology that keeps a safe, self-protective distance from the real conflicts of the modern world purely to preserve its own institutional survival.
- **Ecclesial Supersession**: Roberts's critical label for Milbank's Radical Orthodoxy: a strategy of "out-narrating" secular reason that Roberts judges a sectarian evasion of real ethnography and lived suffering.

## Connections
Reviews Troeltsch and Weber's classic "church-sect" typology; treats Bonhoeffer's Sanctorum Communio and H. Richard Niebuhr's Social Sources of Denominationalism (Part I, Part II.C) as exemplary non-reductive dialogues between theology and sociology. Sharply critiques John Milbank's Radical Orthodoxy (Part II.E) and retrieves Moltmann's "dialectic of the real" (Part II.A) against theology's self-isolation.
