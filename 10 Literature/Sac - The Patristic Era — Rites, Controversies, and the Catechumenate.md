# Unit 2 — The Patristic Era — Rites, Controversies, and the Catechumenate

## The Earliest Rubrics

**Didache 7** (late 1st/early 2nd c.) — the oldest surviving baptismal instruction, and startlingly practical: baptise in **living water** (running, cold); if you have none, other water; if not cold, warm; if you have neither, **pour water three times on the head** in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Let the baptiser and the baptised fast beforehand.

Two lessons: (1) the Trinitarian formula is already fixed by c. 100 AD; (2) the Church was *already* adapting the sign to circumstance without anxiety — the earliest evidence that **matter admits of degrees of fittingness, not a magic recipe**.

**Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 61** (c. 155) gives the first theological interpretation and a term that will dominate Greek baptismal vocabulary: baptism is **phōtismos, "illumination."** The newly baptised are *photizomenoi*, "the enlightened." Note the shift of metaphor: for Paul, baptism is a *tomb*; for Justin, it is *dawn*.

**Tertullian, *De Baptismo*** (c. 200) — the **first treatise ever devoted to a single sacrament** in Christian literature. Famous for its water theology: the Spirit who brooded over the waters at creation still sanctifies water, so that "we, little fishes, after the example of our *Ichthys* Jesus Christ, are born in water." But Tertullian is also the **first named opponent of infant baptism**: *"Let them become Christians when they have become able to know Christ"* (*De Bapt.* 18). Hold both facts together — the tradition's first great theologian of baptism was a paedobaptism sceptic.

## The Catechumenate and the Shape of Initiation

In the patristic Church, becoming a Christian was not an event but a **three-year apprenticeship**, culminating in a single unified rite at the Easter Vigil. The *Apostolic Tradition* (attributed to Hippolytus, c. 215 — authorship and dating genuinely contested; treat with scholarly caution) gives the fullest picture:

1. **Scrutiny of motives and occupation** — certain trades disqualify (gladiator, idol-maker, brothel-keeper, magician).
2. **Three years as a *catechumenus***, hearing the word, dismissed before the Eucharist.
3. **Election** at the start of Lent, producing the *competentes* / *electi*.
4. **Exorcisms, scrutinies, *traditio* and *redditio symboli*** — the Creed is handed over and handed back from memory.
5. **The Vigil**: renunciation of Satan facing West, profession facing East, anointing with oil of exorcism, **stripping naked**, **triple immersion with a triple interrogation** ("Do you believe in God the Father Almighty?" — *"I believe"* — immersion; and so three times).
6. **Post-baptismal anointing by the presbyter**, clothing in a white robe.
7. **The bishop's hand-laying and second anointing on the forehead** — *this is the future Confirmation*.
8. **First Eucharist**, including a cup of milk and honey (the promised land).

**Illustration.** Compare this to a modern parish's six-week baptism preparation course. The patristic Church treated conversion the way a guild treated a craft: three years, a master, an examination, and a public conferral of status. The 1972 RCIA is an explicit attempt to recover exactly this.

**The *disciplina arcani*** ("discipline of the secret") — the meaning of the rites was explained only *after* they had been experienced, in the Easter Week **mystagogical catecheses**. Cyril of Jerusalem, Ambrose (*De Mysteriis*, *De Sacramentis*), John Chrysostom, and Theodore of Mopsuestia give four superb specimens. The pedagogy is deliberately inverted: **experience first, explanation second**.

## The Two Great African Controversies

These two disputes generated the technical vocabulary the whole later tradition uses. Master these and half of scholastic sacramental theology becomes intelligible.

**(a) The Rebaptism Controversy — Cyprian vs Pope Stephen I (c. 255-6).** Is baptism administered by a heretic or schismatic valid? Cyprian: **no** — *salus extra ecclesiam non est*, you cannot give what you do not have. Stephen: **yes** — do not rebaptise, simply lay on hands. Rome's position prevailed and became the tradition. Cyprian, incidentally, settled the *other* question decisively in **Ep. 64** and the **Council of Carthage (253)**: infants should be baptised and need not wait until the eighth day (the circumcision analogy).

**(b) Augustine vs the Donatists (c. 400).** The Donatists revived Cyprian's logic: sacraments conferred by traitor-bishops are void. Augustine's answer is the foundation of Western sacramental theology, and it turns on a distinction:

- **Validity** — does the sacrament *exist*? This depends on Christ, not the minister. *"Peter may baptise, but this is he [Christ] that baptises; Paul may baptise, yet this is he that baptises; Judas may baptise, still this is he that baptises."*
- **Fruitfulness** — does it *profit* the recipient? This depends on charity and communion with the Church.

Augustine also gives us the **character (*character dominicus*)** — an indelible "brand" or military tattoo, which is why baptism is unrepeatable even when unfruitful. Later scholasticism will call this the *res et sacramentum*.

**(c) Augustine vs the Pelagians.** Pelagius denied inherited guilt. Augustine's counter-argument was liturgical and devastating: *the Church baptises infants for the remission of sins — therefore infants have sin to remit.* **Lex orandi, lex credendi** — the rule of prayer establishes the rule of belief. The cost of this argument was heavy: the unbaptised infant's fate. Augustine consigned them to the mildest punishment of hell; medieval theology softened this into *limbus infantium*; the International Theological Commission finally reopened it in 2007.

## The Constantinian Distortion

Once Christianity became socially advantageous, the catechumenate collapsed — and with it the unity of the initiation rites. Two symptoms:

- **Deferred baptism.** Because post-baptismal sin was thought nearly unforgivable, many delayed baptism until the deathbed. **Constantine himself was baptised only when dying (337)** — by an Arian bishop, no less. Ambrose was still a catechumen when acclaimed Bishop of Milan.
- **Mass infant baptism** became the norm as the Empire Christianised, so the three-year catechumenate lost its function and withered into vestigial ceremonies performed over a baby who could not answer for herself.

**This is the single most consequential fact in the history of both sacraments** — the medieval, Reformation, and modern developments all follow from it.

## Key Terms

| Term | Language | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *phōtismos* | Greek | illumination — the standard Greek name for baptism |
| *mystagōgia* | Greek | post-baptismal explanation of the mysteries |
| *disciplina arcani* | Latin | the discipline of secrecy about the rites |
| *competentes* / *electi* | Latin | candidates in the final Lenten stage |
| *traditio / redditio symboli* | Latin | handing over / handing back of the Creed |
| *character dominicus* | Latin | the Lord's indelible brand-mark on the soul |
| *lex orandi, lex credendi* | Latin | the rule of prayer is the rule of belief |
