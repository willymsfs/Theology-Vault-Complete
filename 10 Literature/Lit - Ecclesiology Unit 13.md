# Unit 13 — Ecumenism: Principles, History, and the Indian Church

## Meaning and Rationale
Ecumenism (from oikoumene, "the inhabited world") is the Church's engagement, in obedience to Christ's own prayer "that they may all be one" (Jn 17:21), with the scandal of Christian disunity — not a negotiable diplomatic courtesy but a theological imperative flowing directly from the nature of the Church as communion (Units 10-11) and as sacrament and sign of the unity of the whole human race (LG 1).

## Historical Divisions and Papal Engagement
The East-West division (1054) rooted in disputes over the filioque, papal primacy, and liturgical and cultural difference; the Catholic-Protestant division (16th century) rooted in disputes over justification, the authority of Scripture, and the sacraments and ministry. Papal engagement runs from Leo XIII's cautious late-nineteenth-century openings, through Pius XI's more guarded stance, to John XXIII, whose convening of Vatican II and founding of the Secretariat for Promoting Christian Unity (1960) marks the decisive Catholic turn toward the modern ecumenical movement.

## Unitatis Redintegratio's Three Principles
(1) Subsistit in (LG 8): the Church of Christ "subsists in" the Catholic Church, while genuine ecclesial elements — Scripture, sacraments, the life of grace — are really present outside her visible boundaries, avoiding both an exclusive "only we are Church" claim and a relativist "all churches are simply equivalent" claim. (2) Imperfect communion: other baptized Christians are already joined to the Catholic Church by real, if incomplete, bonds (shared baptism, shared Scripture, in many cases valid orders and sacraments, as with the Orthodox) — communion admits of degrees (recall the three concentric circles, Unit 9). (3) Hierarchy of truths: not every revealed truth carries equal weight or proximity to the very foundation of the faith (the Trinity, the Incarnation) — a principle allowing dialogue to distinguish core, shared convictions from secondary matters, without relativizing doctrine itself.

John Paul II's Ut Unum Sint (1995, Unit 11) reaffirmed and extended this commitment. Cardinal Kurt Koch and Cardinal Walter Kasper have continued, into the twenty-first century, to press both the achievements (the 1999 Joint Declaration on Justification with Lutherans) and the continuing obstacles (differing views of ministry, authority, and moral teaching) of the ecumenical project.

## Ecumenism in India
India is home to over two hundred distinct Christian denominations, an extraordinary fragmentation inherited chiefly from the colonial-era missionary period. Three reasons are commonly given for ecumenical solidarity in this minority context: (1) the shared experience of being a small religious minority (roughly 2-2.5% of the population) within an overwhelmingly non-Christian society makes internal division a serious pastoral and social liability; (2) the shared witness demanded by service to the poor, the Dalit and Adivasi communities, and education and healthcare is weakened by needless competition among Christian bodies; (3) India's own experience of reunion — the 1947 formation of the Church of South India, uniting Anglican, Methodist, Congregationalist, and Presbyterian traditions — offers the wider Church a genuine model of achieved, structural unity. The prevailing conviction in the Indian context is that ecumenism should aim at unity in spirit rather than uniformity of structure.

## Illustration
The "hierarchy of truths" can be pictured as concentric circles around a single sun — the Trinity and the Incarnation at the very centre. Truths closer to that centre (e.g. Christ's divinity) admit far less room for legitimate difference than truths further out (e.g. precise theories of how grace operates in justification), even though every revealed truth remains, in its own place, part of the one constellation of the faith.
