# Ch. 04 — Henri de Lubac
*Chapter author: John Milbank*

## Core Idea
De Lubac retrieved patristic and medieval sources (ressourcement) to argue there is no "pure nature" separate from the supernatural: every human person carries an innate, unmerited desire for the vision of God, which the modern nature/grace dualism had obscured.

## Detailed Explanation
A French Jesuit and central figure of the nouvelle théologie ("new theology"), de Lubac held the chair of the History of Religions at Lyon from 1930. His masterwork Surnaturel (1946) directly challenged the dominant Neo-Scholastic account, which treated "pure nature" as a self-sufficient philosophical category to which grace was then externally added — a picture de Lubac judged both historically inaccurate (it was not how the Church Fathers or Aquinas himself thought) and theologically dangerous, since it let "nature" (and so secular reason, culture, and politics) claim independence from grace altogether. The book drew official Roman suspicion and a period of enforced silence, but de Lubac was later fully vindicated: he served as a peritus (expert advisor) at Vatican II, whose documents echo his integration of nature and grace, and John Paul II made him a cardinal.

## Example
De Lubac's ressourcement method — go back to the actual patristic and medieval texts rather than relying on later scholastic textbook summaries — is concretely visible in his other major project, the multi-volume history of medieval biblical exegesis (Exégèse médiévale), recovering how the Fathers and medieval exegetes read Scripture on multiple simultaneous levels of meaning, a practice systematic modern exegesis had largely lost.

## Illustration
Neo-Scholasticism's "pure nature" is like imagining a plant that could, in principle, be perfectly healthy and complete while permanently sealed off from sunlight — nature as a self-contained system needing grace only as an optional extra. De Lubac's point is that there is no such plant: every human nature is, from the start, built with an orientation toward the "sunlight" of God, whether or not it ever receives it.

## Key Terms
- **Ressourcement** (French) — "return to the sources": The programmatic retrieval of patristic and medieval theology as a resource for renewing modern Catholic thought.
- **Natura Pura** (Latin) — "pure nature": The Neo-Scholastic hypothesis of a self-contained human nature without any intrinsic ordering toward the supernatural — the position de Lubac rejected.
- **Surnaturel** (French) — "the supernatural": The unmerited gift of grace which de Lubac nonetheless argued is the built-in, intrinsic goal (telos) of every human spirit.
- **Corpus Mysticum** (Latin) — "mystical body": De Lubac's historical retrieval showing the phrase originally named the Eucharist, only later coming to name the Church — evidence of their original, organic sacramental unity.

## Connections
De Lubac mentored and worked alongside Hans Urs von Balthasar at Lyon, who adopted his ressourcement method; alongside Rahner and Schillebeeckx he is a pioneer of the Catholic theological renewal that shaped Vatican II (Part II.D). John Milbank, the chapter's own author, builds his Radical Orthodoxy movement (Part II.E) directly on de Lubac's critique of the nature/grace divide.
