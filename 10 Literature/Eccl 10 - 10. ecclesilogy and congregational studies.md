# 10. ecclesilogy and congregational studies.pdf

**TITLE**: "On the Dynamic Relation Between Ecclesiology and Congregational Studies." By Roger Haight, S.J. (Scholar in Residence, Union Theological Seminary, New York City) and James Nieman (Professor of Practical Theology, Hartford Seminary, Connecticut) [88-90]. Published in *Theological Studies*, Vol. 70, 2009, pages 577–599 [88, 91].

**MAIN SUBJECT**: The dialogical and mutually critical relationship between academic systematic ecclesiology ("ecclesiology from below") and the practical field of congregational studies, proposing that they are complementary disciplines that hold the theological and social dimensions of the Church in healthy tension [88, 92, 93].

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   The tension between preserving the essential, normative character of the Church and the need for local inculturation [91].
*   The methodological contrast between "ecclesiology from above" and "ecclesiology from below" [94-96].
*   Methodological reductionism (doctrinal/theological vs. historical/sociological) [97-99].
*   The "whole-part" optic in understanding regional and denominational pluralism [100].
*   The congregation as the primary existential unit of the Church [101, 102].
*   The descriptive (indicative) and prescriptive (subjunctive) dimensions of practical theology [103].
*   Reinterpreting the traditional marks of the Church (unity, holiness, catholicity, apostolicity) through a congregational lens [104-107].
*   Focusing on concrete, socially embedded "practices" rather than arbitrary institutional boundaries [108, 109].
*   The educational task of theology in helping local assemblies analyze their primary theological actions [110, 111].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Ecclesiology from Below**: An ecclesiological method that begins with the historical appearance of the church and its actual, existential communities, using historical and sociological categories to trace its development while recognizing its confessional, Spirit-led dimension [94, 96, 112].
2.  **Whole-Part Optic**: A critical perspective in ecclesiology from below asserting that one's own particular denomination or parish is not the entire Church, but a authentic, localized embodiment of the whole Church which exists in communion with other parts [100].
3.  **Theological vs. Historical Reductionism**: Theological reductionism is the exclusive use of biblical/doctrinal language to describe the Church, ignoring its human, historical reality. Historical reductionism is the exclusive use of social-science categories, ignoring its transcendent, Spirit-led dimension [98, 99, 113].
4.  **Ecclesial Practice**: A concrete, socially embedded, and meaningful pattern of human activity that has five features: it involves tangible actions, is socially embedded, is meaningful for participants, offers strategies for right use, and seeks an intended purpose [109, 114].
5.  **Indicative and Subjunctive Practical Theology**: The double nature of studying congregations where descriptive work documenting "what is" (indicative) naturally carries the seeds for and initiates local reflection about "what might be" (subjunctive), balancing description with prescriptive challenge [103].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Divide Between Theory and Reality**: Bridging the disconnect between academic, highly idealized doctrines of the Church and the actual, messy social and political dynamics driving particular congregations [91, 92].
2.  **The Credibility Gap of the Marks of the Church**: Addressing how idealist claims about the Church's holiness and unity lack credibility in contemporary society (especially in the wake of the clergy sexual abuse crisis) unless they are realistically measured and reinterpreted through the actual life of communities [104, 115].
3.  **The Vague Social Boundaries of Congregations**: Overcoming the difficulty of defining where a congregation physically starts and stops by shifting the study focus to its concrete theological "practices" that extend into the public square [108, 109].
4.  **The Objectification of Congregations**: Preventing outside academic experts from treating local assemblies as passive vessels of study, thereby reclaiming active theological reflection and agency at the grassroots level [110].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "The theological discipline of ecclesiology has to be chastened by consideration of the congregations in order to be credible..." (Page 577 / Passage 1) - Context: Setting down the central "intellectual pincer movement" thesis of the authors.
*   "ecclesiology from below begins historically with a historical account of the genesis of the church beginning with the ministry of Jesus." (Page 579 / Passage 11) - Context: Defining the starting point of an empirical, historically conscious ecclesiology.
*   "The study of the church must attend simultaneously to the historical and theological character of the church." (Page 581 / Passage 17) - Context: Presenting the foundational thesis that governs the integration of sociological and doctrinal languages.
*   "The church community as mystery cannot be found behind or above concrete, visible reality. The church community is to be found in this reality..." (Page 581 / Passage 18, quoting Edward Schillebeeckx) - Context: Denouncing any dualistic separation of the divine and human dimensions of the Church.
*   "Through congregational studies one can critically measure the credibility and ultimately the authenticity of the theological language about the church." (Page 585 / Passage 29) - Context: Explaining how concrete field studies serve as an antidote to theological reductionism.
*   "We no longer ought to be satisfied with approaches that reduce ecclesial existence to its functions and processes on the one hand or its ideas and ideals on the other." (Page 599 / Passage 73) - Context: Summarizing the need for a holistic, tensive vision of the Church in the article's conclusion.

**SUMMARY**:
Roger Haight and James Nieman argue that the discipline of ecclesiology is best served by a dynamic, interactive relationship with congregational studies, utilizing a method of "ecclesiology from below" [88, 92, 94]. They assert that while general ecclesiology provides the formal, theological definitions and historical-ecumenical context of the Church's mission, congregational studies examines the material, concrete reality of local assemblies, serving as an essential reality-check that prevents theological reductionism and tests the credibility of idealist doctrinal claims [116-118]. By focusing on lived theological practices and employing both descriptive (indicative) and prescriptive (subjunctive) reflection, this interdisciplinary partnership helps local congregations reclaim active theological agency and contextualizes them within the wider, catholic tradition, resulting in a more holistic and realistic understanding of the Church [93, 103, 109, 110].
