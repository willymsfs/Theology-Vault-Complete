# 23. trinitarian being of the church.pdf

**TITLE**: "The Trinitarian Being of the Church." By John Behr (St. Vladimir's Orthodox Theological Seminary) [65]. Published in *St Vladimir's Theological Quarterly*, Vol. 48, No. 1, 2003, pages 67–88 [65].

**MAIN SUBJECT**:
A critical systematic evaluation of the relationship between Trinitarian theology and ecclesiology, offering an Eastern Orthodox critique of modern "communion ecclesiology" and proposing a scripturally, sacramentally, and patristically integrated model of the Church [65, 66].

**KEY TOPICS**:
*   The historical development of modern ecclesiology out of the ecumenical encounters of the 20th century [65].
*   A theological critique of John Zizioulas's "communion ecclesiology" [66-68].
*   The three primary scriptural images of the Church: People of God, Body of Christ, and Temple of the Holy Spirit [69].
*   The trinitarian economy of salvation (Cappadocian and Byzantine theology) [69-71].
*   The eschatological tension ("already but not yet") and the "one true Church" claim of Orthodoxy (Georges Florovsky) [72-74].
*   The paschal, lifelong character of baptism vs. the eucharisticized focus [75, 76].
*   The critique of Cyprianic exclusivism and the historical canon law on receiving converts (Basil the Great) [77-79].
*   The Church as "Mother" and the formation of Christian identity (Hippolytus) [80].

**CONCEPTS EXPLAINED**:
1.  **Communion Ecclesiology**: The modern theological framework where the eternal *koinonia* of the three Persons of the Holy Trinity acts as the direct paradigm for the horizontal, relational structure of the earthly Church [66].
2.  **Immanent/Economic Separation**: The systematic error of treating the "immanent Trinity" (God's inner life) as a realm apart from the "economic Trinity" (God's action in salvation), which Behr argues separates the Church from God by setting them up as parallel "communions" [67].
3.  **The Israel of God**: The biblical self-understanding of the Church as the new creation called out by the Cross, where the basis of election is Christ himself rather than race or fleshly circumcision [81, 82].
4.  **Eucharisticized Pagans**: John Erickson's diagnostic term for church members who participate in the Eucharist as a dry ritual of realized eschatology but lack any practical consciousness of the daily, paschal "dying to sin" required of the baptized [83].
5.  **The Church as Mother**: The patristic image (articulated by Hippolytus and Revelation 12) depicting the Church as a virgin-mother in labor, weaving the flesh of the Word in the womb of Scripture to continually give birth to sons of God [80, 84].

**ISSUES ADDRESSED**:
1.  **The Horizontalization of the Church**: Resolving the tendency in modern communion ecclesiology to reduce the Church to a "Platonic image" or horizontal reflection of God's being, rather than showing its direct ontological integration into the life of Christ through the Spirit [67, 85].
2.  **The Neglect of the Cross in Eucharistic Revivals**: Countering the modern eucharistic revival's tendency to focus so heavily on the glory of the Kingdom that it treats baptism as a mere preliminary gate, obscuring the lifelong, paschal necessity of the Cross [76, 83].
3.  **The Rigid Exclusivism of Cyprianic Boundaries**: Critiquing Saint Cyprian's third-century strict identification of the Church's boundaries with the formal jurisdiction of the bishop, which condemned non-Catholics to eternal fire and demanded re-baptism [77, 78, 86].
4.  **Triumphalism vs. Historical Failure**: Reconciling the Orthodox Church's claim to be the "one true Church" with its actual, historically visible losses and failures, using Florovsky's distinction between the "true" and the "perfect" Church [72].

**IMPORTANT QUOTES**:
*   "The relationship between Trinitarian theology and ecclesiology has been much discussed in recent decades... the question of ecclesiology... is our modern problem, one... provoked by the ecumenical encounter of the twentieth century." (Page 67 / Passage 129) - Context: Explaining why the early Church spent its energy on Christology rather than explicit ecclesiologies.
*   "We only know God from his activities, as he reveals himself, and what he reveals of himself is what he is." (Page 75 / Passage 149) - Context: Grounding Trinitarian theology and the Church in the concrete, biblical economy of salvation rather than abstract metaphysics.
*   "The Church is not just a communion of persons in relation, but the body of Christ giving thanks to the Father in the Spirit." (Page 78 / Passage 152) - Context: Rejecting non-liturgical, purely relational models of the Church in favor of a specific Trinitarian flow.
*   "We forget that the eucharist is but a foretaste of the kingdom, not its final realization... We want the glory and forget the cross." (Page 83 / Passage 165) - Context: Quoting John Erickson on the danger of a premature "realized eschatology" that ignores baptismal discipline.
*   "...the ancients decided to accept that baptism which in no way deviates from the faith... to accept that of schismatics, as still being of the Church." (Page 85 / Passage 173) - Context: Citing Saint Basil's Epistle 188 to prove that the historic Church accepted a measure of ecclesial reality outside its formal bounds.
*   "The Church, as the body of Christ and the temple of the Spirit, incarnates the presence of God in this world, and does so also as the mother of the baptized, in travail with them until their death..." (Page 88 / Passage 179) - Context: Concluding with an integrated, sacramental and maternal vision of the Church's Trinitarian being.

**SUMMARY**:
John Behr criticizes modern "communion ecclesiology" for separating the Church from God by treating it as an abstract, horizontal reflection of the "immanent Trinity" [67]. Drawing upon the Cappadocians, Saint Athanasius, and later Byzantine theologians, he asserts that the Church is directly integrated into the life of the Trinity through its three primary scriptural images: called by the Father, constituted as the Body of Christ, and indwelt by the Holy Spirit [69-71]. Behr warns against the modern "eucharistic revival" when it overshadows the lifelong, paschal character of baptism and collapses into a triumphalist "realized eschatology" that ignores historical sin [76, 83, 87]. He concludes that the Church must be understood as a pilgrim, laboring "mother" that prepares its members for ultimate resurrection in the Father [80, 88].
