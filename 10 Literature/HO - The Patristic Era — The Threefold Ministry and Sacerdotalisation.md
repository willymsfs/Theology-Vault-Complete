# Unit 3 — The Patristic Era — The Threefold Ministry and Sacerdotalisation

## Clement of Rome (c. 96)

***1 Clement* 40-44** is the earliest post-apostolic text on ministry, written to settle a revolt in Corinth in which presbyters had been deposed. Three contributions:

1. **An argument from order.** Clement draws an explicit analogy from the Old Testament cult — high priest, priests, Levites, and *laypeople* — and uses the word ***laikos*** (layman) for the first time in Christian literature. **The clergy/laity distinction enters the vocabulary here**, by analogy with the Temple.
2. **An argument from succession.** The apostles appointed their first converts as bishops and deacons, and "gave instruction that when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed to their ministry" — the seed of **apostolic succession**.
3. **The conclusion**: deposing blameless presbyters is sin.

## Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107) — The Monepiscopate

Ignatius gives the first unambiguous picture of a **single bishop** with a college of presbyters and deacons under him:

> "Let no one do anything pertaining to the Church without the bishop... Wherever the bishop appears, there let the people be, just as wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church."

His analogy is significant: the bishop stands in the place of **God the Father** (not, at this stage, of Christ), the presbyters as the **apostolic council**, the deacons entrusted with the service of Jesus Christ. Note that Ignatius is *urging* this structure, which suggests it was not yet universal — his letters are evidence of a pattern being established, not merely described.

## The Apostolic Tradition (c. 215) — The Ordination Prayers

The earliest surviving ordination rites, and a standard examinable text:

| Order | Who imposes hands | Prayer asks for |
|---|---|---|
| **Bishop** | **Other bishops** (with the assent of all) | The "princely Spirit," high-priestly ministry, power to forgive sins |
| **Presbyter** | The bishop, **with the presbyters also laying on hands** | The Spirit of grace and counsel, to share in the presbyterate and govern |
| **Deacon** | **The bishop alone** — "for he is ordained not to the priesthood but to the service of the bishop" | The Spirit of grace and zeal for service |

**The detail about the deacon is doctrinally live to this day.** That the deacon is ordained to the bishop's service and *not to the priesthood* is precisely what Benedict XVI's *Omnium in Mentem* (2009) would re-inscribe in canon law (Unit 10).

## Sacerdotalisation: When Ministers Became Priests

The decisive linguistic shift happens in the third century, above all with **Cyprian of Carthage**, who applies ***sacerdos*** (priest) and ***summus sacerdos*** (high priest) to the **bishop**, and speaks of the bishop offering sacrifice "in the place of Christ" (*vice Christi*). *Sacerdos* is extended to presbyters only later, as presbyters increasingly presided at the Eucharist in outlying communities.

**Why it happened.** Once the Eucharist was consistently understood as a sacrifice (see the Eucharist course, Unit 3 — the *thysia* of Didache 14, Malachi 1:11), the one who presides at a sacrifice is naturally called a priest. **The sacerdotal theology of orders is a corollary of the sacrificial theology of the Eucharist**, which is why the Reformation attacked both together and why they must be studied together.

## Conciliar Consolidation

- **Nicaea (325), c. 4** — a bishop is to be appointed by **all the bishops of the province**, or at minimum **three**, with the metropolitan's confirmation. This is the origin of the rule of three consecrators.
- **Chalcedon (451), c. 6** — **no "absolute" ordinations**: no one may be ordained without attachment to a specific church or community. Ordination is *for* a people, not a personal dignity. This ancient principle survives in the modern requirement of **incardination**.
