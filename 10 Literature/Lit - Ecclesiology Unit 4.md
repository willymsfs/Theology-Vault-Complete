# Unit 4 — Johannine and Pauline Ecclesiology

## John: Sheepfold, Vine, and Paraclete
John favours organic, relational images over institutional ones. The sheepfold (Jn 10): the flock knows the shepherd's voice (10:27) — a community defined by intimate, personal relationship with Christ. The vine and branches (Jn 15): total, life-giving dependence ("apart from me you can do nothing," 15:5). The Paraclete (Jn 14–16) is the Spirit who will "teach you all things" and "guide you into all truth" after Jesus's departure, making ongoing communion with the absent-yet-present Christ possible.

## The Eight Themes of the Johannine Ideal Community
(1) The saving significance of Jesus's life, death, and resurrection. (2) Foot-washing (Jn 13) as the paradigm of community relationship — leadership as humble service. (3) Faith in Jesus as the sole foundation of belonging (Jn 20:31). (4) A movement sovereignly guided by the Spirit/Paraclete. (5) Organic relationship with Christ (the vine and branches). (6) Disciples as people of hope. (7) A community charged with mission to the world. (8) Unity — the object of Jesus's high-priestly prayer (Jn 17) and the supreme sign of discipleship the world is meant to see (17:21; 13:35).

## Paul: Founding and Sustaining Communities
Paul plants churches in strategic urban centres — Corinth, Ephesus, Philippi, Thessalonica — along trade routes, then sustains them by letter and delegate rather than permanent residence. House churches, meeting in the homes of patrons such as Prisca and Aquila (Rom 16:3-5), are the basic cell of the earliest Pauline communities. Ministry is shared, not solitary: Paul names dozens of co-workers (Timothy, Titus, Phoebe, Junia). 1 Corinthians 12 pictures a diversity of gifts (charismata) united in one body, each member indispensable ("the eye cannot say to the hand, I do not need you," 12:21).

## Two Pauline Insights: Baptism and Eucharist
Baptism is the doorway of incorporation: salvation is not an individual transaction but incorporation into Christ's corporate, saving death-and-resurrection (Rom 6:3-4; 1 Cor 12:13, "in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body"). The Eucharist sustains and manifests that same unity in an ongoing way: sharing the one bread and one cup makes the diverse assembly ontologically "one body" (1 Cor 10:17). Baptism initiates; the Eucharist nourishes and continually re-embodies the same communion — the vertical bond with God and the horizontal bond with one another are inseparable.

## Illustration
John's vine (15:1-8) is a living-organism illustration: cut a single branch off, and its individual fruitfulness dies at once, however healthy it looked. Paul's "one loaf" (1 Cor 10:17) is a single-body illustration: many grains ground into one loaf. Both refuse a picture of the Church as a mere collection of individuals who happen to share beliefs; the Church is an organism, not an aggregate.
