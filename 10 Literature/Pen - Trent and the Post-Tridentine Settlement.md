# Unit 9 — Trent and the Post-Tridentine Settlement

## Session XIV (25 November 1551)

Trent treated Penance together with Extreme Unction. Its doctrine and canons answer the Reformers point by point.

| Point | Trent's teaching |
|---|---|
| **Institution** | A true and proper sacrament instituted by Christ, principally in **John 20:22-23** |
| **Distinct from baptism** | Necessary for those who fall after baptism; the ***secunda tabula post naufragium***, "the second plank after shipwreck" |
| **Necessity of confession** | Penitents must confess **all mortal sins of which they are conscious after diligent examination**, including hidden ones and those against the last two commandments, **according to species and number** and with the circumstances that change the species |
| **Attrition** | Imperfect contrition, conceived from the consideration of sin's ugliness or fear of hell, **is a true gift of God**; though insufficient alone to justify, it **disposes to grace in the sacrament** |
| **Absolution is judicial** | Not a bare ministry of announcing forgiveness, but a **judicial act** (*actus iudicialis*) in which the priest pronounces sentence as a judge — hence the necessity of confession, since a judge cannot judge a cause he does not know |
| **Satisfaction** | Real and salutary; does not detract from Christ's merit but derives all its power from it — "we can do nothing of ourselves as of ourselves" |
| **The minister** | Priests alone, requiring **jurisdiction** as well as orders; reservation of cases upheld |

**Teaching point on canon 6.** Trent's insistence on the *judicial* character of absolution is the doctrinal hinge, because it generates the requirement of confession as a matter of internal logic: if the priest is a judge, he must know the case. Any essay on why Catholics confess to a priest should run through this argument rather than through mere positive-law appeal.

## The Institutional Consequences

- **The Roman Ritual (1614)** standardised the rite for the Latin Church.
- **The confessional box** — a genuine innovation, introduced by **Charles Borromeo** in Milan in the 1560s and rapidly adopted. Its purpose was twofold: to guarantee **anonymity** and to prevent solicitation and scandal by physically separating confessor and penitent. It is worth stressing to students that this piece of furniture, so identified with the sacrament in the popular mind, is **younger than the Reformation**.
- **Seminary formation in moral theology** was established to produce competent confessors — which generated the manual tradition and, with it, the controversies of the next unit.
