# Unit 5 — The Scholastic Synthesis

## Fixing It Among the Seven

**Peter Lombard's *Sentences* IV, d. 23** lists *unctio extrema* among the seven sacraments, and the septenary is then confirmed at **Lyons II (1274)** and **Florence (1439)**.

**Florence's Decree for the Armenians (1439)** gives the standard late-medieval definition, and its restrictiveness should be noted precisely: the sacrament is **"not to be given except to a sick person of whose death there is fear"** (*nisi infirmo, de cuius morte timetur*). Matter: olive oil blessed by a bishop. Anointings: the five senses, plus loins and feet, with the deprecatory form *"Per istam sanctam unctionem..."* Effect: healing of the mind and, so far as expedient, of the body.

## Aquinas (ST Suppl. qq. 29-33)

| Category | Content |
|---|---|
| **Matter** | Olive oil **blessed by a bishop** |
| **Form** | The deprecatory prayer, *"Per istam unctionem..."* — note that unlike penance, the form here is a **prayer, not an indicative declaration** |
| **Minister** | Priests |
| **Principal effect** | The remission of the ***reliquiae peccati*** — the "**remnants of sin**": the weakness, spiritual debility, and residual disorder left by sin even after its guilt is forgiven |
| **Bodily healing** | An effect ***per accidens*** — granted when it is conducive to the person's salvation, not otherwise |
| **Character** | None; the sacrament is **repeatable** in a new illness |

**The *reliquiae peccati* is the concept to master here.** Aquinas needed to identify an effect *proper* to this sacrament and not already covered by penance (which forgives guilt) or the Eucharist (which nourishes). His answer: penance heals the wound, but the patient remains weakened; anointing addresses that remaining debility, preparing the soul for its final passage. This is a genuinely elegant solution, and it explains why the sacrament came to be called the ***sacramentum exeuntium***, the "**sacrament of those departing**."

**The problem with it.** The account is coherent but it *presupposes* and *reinforces* the deathbed setting. If the proper effect is the removal of the remnants of sin in preparation for glory, the sacrament's natural moment is indeed the end. The scholastic synthesis is thus a brilliant systematisation of a practice that had itself arisen by historical accident — a pattern we have now seen in confirmation, penance, and anointing alike.

## Other Scholastic Positions

**Bonaventure** and **Duns Scotus** broadly concur on the deathbed orientation, though with differences over whether the sacrament requires actual proximity to death or merely dangerous illness. Scotus, characteristically, is the more restrictive: he holds it should be deferred until death is genuinely imminent, so that the grace conferred is not subsequently lost through further sin.
