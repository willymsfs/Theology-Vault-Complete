# Unit 7 — Indulgences and the Late Medieval Crisis

Indulgences cannot be understood, or fairly criticised, without the distinctions the tradition drew. Getting these right is essential for the Reformation unit.

## The Underlying Distinction

The tradition distinguishes:

- **The guilt of sin (*culpa*)** — remitted by absolution in the sacrament.
- **The temporal punishment due to sin (*poena temporalis*)** — the residual disorder and debt, which may remain after the guilt is forgiven, and is expiated either in this life (by satisfaction, penance, works of charity) or in **purgatory**.

The biblical warrant offered is precisely the Nathan-David episode (Unit 1): the sin is forgiven, yet a consequence remains.

**An indulgence is the remission of that *temporal punishment*, not the forgiveness of sin.** An indulgence presupposes that the sin has already been forgiven in confession. This distinction was routinely obscured in popular preaching, which is a large part of why the system detonated.

## The Treasury of Merits

The theological basis was formalised by **Clement VI** in the bull ***Unigenitus Dei Filius*** (1343): the infinite merits of Christ, together with those of Mary and the saints, constitute a **treasury (*thesaurus*)** which the Church, exercising the power of the keys, may apply to individual members.

## How It Went Wrong

- Indulgences originated in **commutation** — the Celtic practice of substituting a lighter work for a heavier penance (Unit 5). The logic was already in place; only the scale changed.
- The **Crusade indulgences** (from Urban II, 1095) established the plenary indulgence attached to a costly work.
- Extension **to the dead** by way of suffrage (15th c.) transformed the pastoral dynamics entirely, since one could now purchase relief for deceased relatives.
- **Financial association** — almsgiving as the qualifying work, with the alms directed to a specific project — produced, in practice, a market.
- The immediate trigger: the **St Peter's Basilica indulgence** preached in Germany by the Dominican **Johann Tetzel**, with its notorious jingle (attributed by Luther): *"As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs."*

## Luther's Ninety-Five Theses (31 October 1517)

Read the Theses carefully and you find a document that is *not*, at this stage, a rejection of the sacrament or even of indulgences as such, but a protest against their misuse:

- **Thesis 1** is the theological heart: *"When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said 'Repent' (poenitentiam agite), he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance."* Luther is exploiting a **translation point** — the Vulgate's *poenitentiam agite* ("do penance") renders the Greek *metanoeite* ("repent, be converted"), and Luther, following Erasmus's Greek text, argues the Latin had narrowed an interior lifelong conversion into a discrete ritual act.
- **Thesis 36**: "Any truly repentant Christian has a right to full remission of penalty and guilt, even without indulgence letters."
- **Thesis 82** is the sharp pastoral question: if the pope can release souls from purgatory, "why does he not empty purgatory for the sake of holy love... rather than for the sake of miserable money?"

**Teaching point.** Thesis 1 is the hinge of the whole Reformation on this sacrament. Everything downstream — the rejection of enumerated confession, of satisfaction, of the priestly tribunal — flows from the claim that repentance is the *whole shape of the Christian life* rather than a repeatable transaction. A good essay begins here rather than with Tetzel.

## The Catholic Correction

The Church's own reform was real, if late: **Trent Session XXI** abolished the office of *quaestores* (indulgence-preachers) outright, and **Session XXV** affirmed indulgences while condemning "all evil gains" attached to them. **Paul VI's *Indulgentiarum Doctrina* (1967)** substantially reformed the system, abolishing the misleading numerical day-and-year attachments and re-grounding indulgences in the communion of saints.
