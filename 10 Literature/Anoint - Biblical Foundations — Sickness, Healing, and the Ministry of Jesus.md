# Unit 1 — Biblical Foundations — Sickness, Healing, and the Ministry of Jesus

## The Old Testament: Sickness, Sin, and Protest

The Old Testament holds two convictions in unresolved tension, and both survive into Christian theology.

**The retribution principle.** Sickness is widely presented as connected to sin — Deuteronomy 28's blessings and curses, Psalm 38 ("there is no health in my bones because of my sin"), Miriam's leprosy (Num 12). This is the intuitive theology every pastor still meets: *what did I do to deserve this?*

**The protest against it.** **Job** is the sustained, canonical refutation. Job's friends argue the retribution principle with increasing severity; God's verdict is that they "have not spoken of me what is right" (Job 42:7). The book does not explain suffering — it forbids the easy explanation.

Three further texts matter:

- **Isaiah 53:4-5** — "surely he has borne our griefs (*ḥŏlāyēnû*, literally *our sicknesses*) and carried our sorrows... by his stripes we are healed." Matthew 8:17 applies this verse directly to Jesus's healing ministry, which makes it the bridge text between the Suffering Servant and the theology of Christ bearing our infirmity.
- **Sirach 38:1-15** — a remarkably balanced text: "Honour the physician... for the Lord created him," while also urging prayer and repentance. **Medicine and prayer are not rivals.** This is the scriptural warrant for the Church's consistent refusal to set the sacrament against medical treatment.
- **Psalm 41:3** — "The LORD sustains him on his sickbed; in his illness you restore him to full health" — the prayer of the sick person that the later rite takes up.

## Jesus's Healing Ministry

Healing is not incidental to the Gospels but structurally central: roughly **a third of Mark** concerns healings and exorcisms. Three features shape the later sacrament:

1. **Healing is a sign of the Kingdom's arrival**, not a display of power. When John's disciples ask if he is the one to come, Jesus answers by listing healings (Mt 11:4-5, echoing Isa 35).
2. **Jesus touches.** He touches the leper (Mk 1:41) — a man whom the Law made untouchable. The *physicality* of the encounter is theologically deliberate and is why the sacrament is an **anointing**, a bodily act, rather than a prayer alone.
3. **Forgiveness and healing are linked but not equated.** In Mark 2 the paralytic is forgiven *and* healed. But in **John 9:2-3** the disciples ask "who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" and Jesus answers **"neither"** — decisively breaking the automatic link. Both texts must be held: sin and sickness belong to the same disordered world, but this illness is not that person's punishment.

## Mark 6:13 — The Sending of the Twelve

> "And they cast out many demons, and **anointed with oil many that were sick and healed them**."

This is the only Gospel text describing the disciples anointing the sick with oil, and it is the passage Trent would cite as the sacrament being *"insinuated"* (*insinuatum*) by Christ — hinted at and prepared — before being *promulgated* by James. Note the careful conciliar verb: Trent does not claim Mark 6:13 is the institution, only its intimation.

**Illustration.** Notice that the Twelve are given no explanation of *why* oil. Oil in the ancient Mediterranean was simply what you used on a body — for wounds, for athletes, for the dead, for kings. It was food, medicine, fuel, and cosmetic at once. The sacrament takes the most ordinary substance in the household and makes it the vehicle of the Spirit's strengthening — which is precisely the sacramental logic in miniature.
