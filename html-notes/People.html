Trinity (BTh course)

Summa Theologiae Ia qq. 27-32 develops the classical processions-relations-persons framework, defining each divine person as a relatio subsistens (subsisting relation). Disagreed with Bonaventure over whether the Father's innascibility is a pure negation (Aquinas) or a positive "fontal fullness" (Bonaventure). Retrieved by Gilles Emery in the 20th century against social trinitarianism.

See: Trinity MOC

Sacraments (BTh course)

ST III qq. 66-71 treat Baptism (matter, form, minister, effects, and the three baptisms — fluminis, sanguinis, flaminis) and q. 72 treats Confirmation, distinguishing them by the analogy of birth (generatio spiritualis) and coming of age (augmentum, robur). He also formulates the principle that saves sacramental theology from magic: "God is not bound to the sacraments" (III q.64 a.7). Against Bonaventure and the Victorines he treats the Father's innascibility as pure negation — see the Trinity course.

See: Sacraments MOC

Eucharist (BTh course)

ST III qq. 73-83 give the classical account of the Eucharist: transubstantiation as conversion of the whole substance with the species remaining; the crucial qualification that Christ is present per modum substantiae and so is neither contained, divided, nor moved; concomitance, grounding the reception of the whole Christ under either species; and the threefold distinction whose res tantum is the unity of the Mystical Body. He is also the tradition's greatest Eucharistic poet — Pange Lingua, Lauda Sion, Verbum Supernum, Adoro Te Devote — composed for the Corpus Christi office of 1264.

See: Eucharist MOC

Penance (BTh course)

ST III q.84-90 and the Supplement give the classical analysis: because Penance has no physical element, the three acts of the penitent (contrition, confession, satisfaction) serve as the quasi-materia and the absolution as the form; the res et sacramentum is interior penance and reconciliation with the Church; and no character is imprinted, which is why the sacrament is repeatable. He defends the indicative Ego te absolvo as expressing that the priest acts as instrumental cause in the person of Christ.

See: Penance MOC

Anointing of the Sick (BTh course)

ST Suppl. qq. 29-33 treat Extreme Unction. Matter: olive oil blessed by a bishop; form: the deprecatory prayer, not an indicative declaration as in penance. He identifies the proper effect as the remission of the reliquiae peccati — the spiritual weakness remaining after sin's guilt is forgiven — which distinguishes anointing from both penance and the Eucharist, and makes it the sacramentum exeuntium. Bodily healing is an effect per accidens, granted when conducive to salvation. No character is imprinted, so the sacrament is repeatable.

See: Anointing MOC

Holy Orders (BTh course)

ST Suppl. qq. 34-40 treat Orders as conferring an indelible character and a spiritual power ordered to the Eucharist, and distinguish the power of orders from the power of jurisdiction. Following the practice of his time he identified the matter as the traditio instrumentorum, later corrected by Pius XII. With most scholastics he held the episcopate to be not a distinct sacramental order but a dignity added to the priesthood — the position LG 21 would reverse.

See: Holy Orders MOC

Matrimony (BTh course)

ST Suppl. qq. 41-68 identify the matter and form of matrimony as the words or signs expressing consent, and the ministers as the spouses themselves — unique among the seven sacraments and the root of the entire law of nullity. He retains Augustine's three goods and the hierarchy of ends, with procreation and education of offspring primary and mutual help and the remedy for concupiscence secondary, the scheme Vatican II would decline to repeat.

Trinity (BTh course)

Author of De Trinitate, source of the West's psychological analogy (mens/notitia/amor, later memoria/intelligentia/voluntas) illustrating — not proving — the Trinity from the structure of the human mind. Luigi Gioia reframes the whole treatise as an epistemology of revelation; Maarten Wisse deploys Augustine's insistence on the Trinity's radical otherness against modern relational/social trinitarian revivals.

See: Trinity MOC

Sacraments (BTh course)

Two controversies made him the foundation of Western sacramental theology. Against the Donatists he distinguished validity (which depends on Christ — "Judas baptises, still this is he that baptises") from fruitfulness (which depends on charity and communion), and articulated the indelible character (character dominicus) that makes baptism unrepeatable. Against the Pelagians he argued from the Church's practice of baptising infants for the remission of sins that infants must have sin to remit — the classic case of lex orandi, lex credendi. The cost was his severe position on unbaptised infants, softened medievally into limbus infantium and set aside by the ITC in 2007.

See: Sacraments MOC

Eucharist (BTh course)

The most contested patristic figure in Eucharistic theology, claimed by both Catholics and Reformers. He holds a robust sacramentum/res distinction whose res is the unity of the Body of Christ, teaches totus Christus ("you will be changed into me"), and in Sermon 272 tells the newly baptised: "If you are the body of Christ and his members, it is your own mystery that is placed on the Lord's table... Be what you see, and receive what you are." His sign-language is not modern symbolism — forcing him onto either side of the Berengarian or Reformation divide is anachronistic.

See: Eucharist MOC

Penance (BTh course)

Contributed the framework of three kinds of penance (before baptism, for daily sins, and the grave canonical penance) and assessed deathbed penance with characteristic honesty — we cannot be sure such a person is condemned, but neither can we praise the security of the repentance. His anti-Donatist argument for the Church as corpus permixtum is the same principle the Church had already established against Novatianism.

See: Penance MOC

Holy Orders (BTh course)

His answer to the Donatists supplied the theology of orders with its permanent structure: validity depends on Christ rather than on the minister's worthiness, and the indelible character — compared to a soldier's brand, which a deserter still bears — makes ordination unrepeatable. Three consequences follow: re-ordination is impossible, an unworthy minister ordains validly, and orders can be valid but illicit.

See: Holy Orders MOC

Matrimony (BTh course)

De bono coniugali (401) established the three goods — proles, fides, and sacramentum — that governed Western marriage theology for fifteen centuries, written in part to correct the severity of Jerome's attack on Jovinian. But De nuptiis et concupiscentia, arguing against the Pelagians, holds that intercourse is now inevitably accompanied by concupiscence and that original sin is transmitted through generation — leaving a tradition that affirms marriage's goodness while treating its sexual expression with persistent unease.

Avicenna (Ibn Sina)

Islamic philosopher whose account of essence and accident supplied Aquinas the tool to make Eucharistic accidents without a subject non-contradictory.

Signature move

Defined an accident not as "that which actually exists in a subject" but as "that to whose nature it is due or fitting to exist in a subject." This distinction — nature versus current condition — is what Aquinas borrows wholesale for the Eucharist. Also foundational for the real distinction between essence and existence that underwrites Thomist metaphysics generally.

Bernard Lonergan

Canadian Jesuit; author of Insight (1957) and Method in Theology (1972). The most sustained twentieth-century attempt to say what theology's procedure actually is.

Signature phrase

"A normative pattern of recurrent and related operations yielding cumulative and progressive results."

Method

Turn to the subject: theology is grounded in the four levels of intentional consciousness under the transcendental precepts — be attentive, be intelligent, be reasonable, be responsible. Divides the discipline into Eight Functional Specialties in two phases, pivoting on Conversion. Objectivity is the fruit of authentic subjectivity; the myth to be destroyed is that knowing is "taking a good look".

Criticism

The separation of Doctrines from Systematics is widely judged artificial and unworkable; the scheme is complex, and its guarantee — "authentic subjectivity" — cannot be strictly measured. Systematics risks becoming technical and elitist.

Claims

Method is a pattern of operations, not a set of rules · Objectivity is the fruit of authentic subjectivity · Conversion, not logic, is what makes theology faithful

Calvin

Reformer; with Luther championed Sola Scriptura and the sovereignty of the Gospel.

Connected

Scripture · Luther

Sacraments (BTh course)

Institutes IV.15-16 treat baptism as a sign and seal of engrafting into Christ, genuinely instrumental in the Spirit's hands but never automatic, with infant baptism defended on covenant continuity with circumcision. Institutes IV.19 rejects Confirmation as a sacrament while explicitly praising the ancient practice of catechesis followed by the laying on of hands, and wishing it restored.

See: Sacraments MOC

Eucharist (BTh course)

Institutes IV.17 develops a distinctive middle position: Christ is truly given and truly fed upon by the power of the Holy Spirit, but rather than Christ descending into the elements, the communicant is lifted up to Christ in heaven — which is why Calvin takes the liturgical Sursum corda with full seriousness. He rejects both transubstantiation and Zwingli's bare memorialism.

See: Eucharist MOC

Penance (BTh course)

Institutes III.4 attacks the requirement of enumerating sins as "a torture of consciences," since no one can number their sins any more than the hairs on their head, and rejects satisfaction as impugning Christ's sufficiency. He denies Penance is a sacrament while retaining and strengthening ecclesiastical discipline through the consistory, and permits voluntary private confession to a minister for the troubled conscience.

See: Penance MOC

Anointing of the Sick (BTh course)

Institutes IV.19.18-21 makes the cessationist argument: the healing described in James and Mark was a temporary charismatic gift given to authenticate apostolic preaching, which ceased with the apostolic age, so that continuing the outward sign after the gift has been withdrawn is a "hypocritical stage-play." This is the argument that most directly severs the sacrament from its scriptural warrant.

See: Anointing MOC

Holy Orders (BTh course)

Institutes IV.3 sets out four permanent offices — pastors, teachers, elders, and deacons — derived from Ephesians 4:11. Ordination by the laying on of hands is retained and valued as apostolic and useful, but not counted a sacrament; election by the community and examination of doctrine and life are essential, and the resulting structure is presbyterial rather than episcopal.

See: Holy Orders MOC

Matrimony (BTh course)

Institutes IV.19.34-37 denies that matrimony is a sacrament, attacking its inclusion as resting on the Vulgate's rendering of mystērion at Eph 5:32 as sacramentum — a genuinely sharp philological point. Geneva's ordinances placed marriage under the consistory and permitted divorce for adultery and desertion.

Giacomo Antonelli

Cardinal Secretary of State to Pius IX; the architect of papal diplomacy for nearly thirty years, and a layman in orders who never became a priest.

Papal diplomacy here is calculating and interest-driven, not merely humanitarian: the Holy See behaving as a state among states.

Penance (BTh course)

Argues both sides of the penitential question at different periods, making him a superb illustration of the early Church's tension. De Paenitentia (Catholic period, c. 203) permits the "second repentance," describes exomologesis vividly, and urges sinners not to delay out of shame. De Pudicitia (Montanist period, c. 217) reverses course, denying the Church any power to forgive adultery and fornication and attacking a bishop — probably Callistus of Rome — for extending forgiveness to sins of the flesh.
