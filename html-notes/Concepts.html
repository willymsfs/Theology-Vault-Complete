Accidens Sine Subjecto (Accidents Without a Subject)

The central metaphysical puzzle of the Eucharist. In Aristotelian metaphysics an accident is ens in alio — "being in another" — and cannot exist except inhering in a substance (the grin cannot exist without the cat). After consecration the accidents of bread and wine (colour, taste, shape, weight) persist, but the substance of bread is gone and they do not inhere in Christ's body either (which is not actually white, crunchy or flat). Aquinas resolves the apparent contradiction by redefining accident, following Avicenna, as that whose nature is to inhere — not that which must actually inhere — so that God can directly sustain the accidents in existence without a subject.

Apophatic Theology

Negative theology: stressing God's transcendence over human language and preferring to say what God is not. Its counterpart is the cataphatic (affirmative) way.

Not agnosticism. For Lossky apophaticism is the primordial theological moment — a stripping of the intellect that ends not in silence but in ecstasy and in the birth of personal being in response to God's self-gift. Palamas's essence/energies distinction is what keeps this from collapsing: God's essence is unknowable, his energies genuinely participated in. Aquinas runs a Western version (easier to say what God is not); Marion radicalizes it against onto-theology.

Atonement

CCEO and the St Thomas Christian Yogams

Two indigenous and Eastern Catholic precedents showing synodality was a functioning structure before Vatican II supplied the word — evidence against the charge that synodality is a Western import.

The CCEO (Codex Canonum Ecclesiarum Orientalium). Unlike the Latin Synod of Bishops, which is purely advisory, the Synods of Patriarchal and Major Archiepiscopal Churches — including the Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Churches in India — hold actual legislative, executive and judicial power: electing their own Patriarchs and Major Archbishops, erecting eparchies, and enacting particular law without prior Roman approval.

The Yogams. Before the Latinisation imposed by the Synod of Diamper (1599), the St Thomas Christians were governed by a three-tier lay-clerical assembly system: the Palliyogam (parish assembly, deciding local and judicial matters), the Desiyayogam and Mahayogam (regional and general assemblies), and the Jathikkukarthavian (a native lay Archdeacon holding actual executive power, with the foreign Metropolitan restricted to spiritual duties).

Circumscriptive and Non-Circumscriptive Presence

Two ways a body can occupy space. Circumscriptive presence is the ordinary way: a body's dimensions are commensurate with and bounded by its location — it takes up room part by part. Non-circumscriptive presence is how Christ's body is present in the Eucharist: the whole substance is present in the whole host and wholly present in every part of it. Breaking the host does not break Christ's body; he remains fully present in each fragment. This is the technical answer to the problem of Multilocation — Christ's body is not stretched or divided across altars, because it is not present in the ordinary, dimension-filling way at all.

Eucharistic Semantics: Realist Versus Nominalist

A live fault line in contemporary defences of transubstantiation. Nominalist semantics (the inheritance of Ockham and Buridan) treats a noun like "bread" as a label for a bundle of observable properties — which makes transubstantiation sound like an unintelligible trick, since the properties never change. Realist semantics treats "bread" as naming a genuine underlying organising principle (a substantial form), not merely a description of appearances — on this view, Joshua Hochschild argues, transubstantiation is a coherent claim about a change in what a thing fundamentally is, which nominalist vocabulary has simply lost the resources to say.

The nineteenth-century Catholic answer to industrial poverty: more than charity, less than revolution.

The problem it exists to solve

The poverty produced by industrial society could not be remedied by traditional charity. Communism offered a rival answer that looked like a secularized Christianity — heaven on earth in a workers' paradise. So: how to produce change without encouraging revolution?

Temporal Power

The pope's sovereignty over territory — the Papal States — as distinct from his spiritual authority.

The Consultative Vote (Canon Law)

The principal canonical obstacle to synodality in the Latin Church. The 1983 Code establishes genuine participatory structures — Parish Pastoral Councils (c. 536), Parish Finance Councils (c. 537), Diocesan Pastoral Councils (c. 511) — but restricts every one of them to a consultative, not deliberative, vote. Behind this stands canon 129 §1, which reserves the power of governance (potestas regiminis) to those in sacred orders, permitting the laity only to "co-operate" in its exercise. Practical effect: a council may reach unanimous consensus, and the bishop or parish priest may legally decide the opposite. The canonist Sabine Demel's diagnosis: this reduces lay co-responsibility to a concession from clerical authority rather than a right rooted in baptism.

Acts 15:1-35 is read by the tradition as the archetype of every synod. Theology and Science

The dialogue reopened when the reductionist programme collapsed — relativity, quantum indeterminacy and chaos theory ended the picture of nature as a closed deterministic machine, and so made a theology of nature thinkable again after Kant and Schleiermacher had turned theology inward.

The live problem is divine action: how God influences the world without violating natural law. Clayton's spectrum runs from naturalism and deism, through a single divine act (Wiles), double agency (Farrer, Whitehead), top-down causation (Peacocke), to robust miraculous intervention. Panentheism supplies the favoured analogy: God acts on the world as mind acts on its own body.

Method: Torrance — epistemology follows ontology; the object dictates how it may be known.

Theology of Hope

Moltmann's programme (1964): eschatology is not the last chapter of dogmatics but the medium of all Christian doctrine. The Church lives from a promised future that contradicts the present, so hope is not consolation but a source of unrest.

Its engine is the dialectic of cross and resurrection — continuity in radical discontinuity. Its philosophical partner is Bloch's Principle of Hope; its theological rival is Pannenberg's prolepsis, which grounds the same future in universal history and public reason rather than in promise.

Effect: made political theology, liberation theology and ecological theology thinkable in a European key.

Theology of Religions

The theological account of other faiths: their status, their salvific value, and their relation to Christ.

Theology of the Cross

Ultramontanism

Literally "beyond the mountains" — looking over the Alps to Rome for authority. The nineteenth-century movement to centralize the Church on the papacy, against national ecclesiastical liberties (Gallicanism).

Its intellectual charter is Joseph de Maistre's Du Pape (1819): the papacy is essential as a restriction on the power of the state, and papal infallibility follows. Its institutional victory is Vatican I (1870).

The complication worth remembering

Felicite de Lamennais was an ultramontane too. He wanted a strong papacy precisely so the Church could be free of the state and united with the people. Rome took the authority and refused the democracy — which is why the same doctrine produced both Du Pape and L'Avenir.
