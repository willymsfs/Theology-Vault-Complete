David Ford's Twelve Theses for 21st-Century Theology

Closing maxims from the Epilogue, distilled from David F. Ford editing three editions of The Modern Theologians.

  1. "God is the One who blesses and loves in wisdom." The ultimate ground and object of all theological inquiry is a personal, loving, superabundantly wise God.

  2. "Theology is done for the sake of God and the Kingdom of God." Theology's purpose is not academic self-preservation but service of God and the active realization of God's reign in history.

  3. "The cry of prayer is the beginning, accompaniment, and end of theology: Come, Holy Spirit! Hallelujah! and Maranatha!" Prayer and worship are the indispensable starting point, continuous accompaniment, and final destination of any authentic theology.

  4. "Study of scripture is at the heart of theology." Rigorous, postcritical study of the Bible is Christian theology's primary, normative, animating anchor.

  5. "Describing reality in the light of God is a basic theological discipline." Theology must realistically describe the physical, biological, and social world through the lens of divine creation and providence.

  6. "Theology hopes in and seeks God's purposes while immersed in the contingencies, complexities, and ambiguities of creation and history." Theology stays eschatologically oriented toward God's promised future while remaining fully embedded in present historical struggle.

  7. "Theological wisdom seeks to do justice to many contexts, levels, voices, moods, genres, systems, and responsibilities." Theology must actively incorporate diverse regional contexts, academic methods, and marginalized voices, not one dominant standpoint.

  8. "Theology is practiced collegially, in conversation and, best of all, in friendship; and, through the communion of saints, it is simultaneously premodern, modern, and postmodern." Theology is inherently relational and collaborative, spanning premodern, modern, and postmodern eras at once through the communion of saints.

  9. "Theology is a broker of the arts, humanities, sciences, and common sense for the sake of a wisdom that affirms, critiques, and transforms each of them." Theology plays a crucial interdisciplinary role, correlating and transforming the sciences, arts, and humanities for the sake of a unified wisdom.

  10. "Our religious and secular world needs theology with religious studies in its schools and universities." Rigorous theology belongs alongside religious studies in secular educational institutions, informing the wider public sphere.

  11. "Conversation around scriptures is at the heart of interfaith relations." Shared, collaborative reading of scriptural texts (Scriptural Reasoning) is the most fruitful path for interreligious dialogue.

  12. "Theology is for all who desire to think about God and about reality in relation to God." Theological reflection is not the exclusive domain of academic specialists but a calling open to all who seek to understand reality in relation to God.

Phase 1 — encountering the data (mediating)

# Specialty Precept Governing question
1 Research Be attentive Have we got an accurate text?
2 Interpretation Be intelligent What is the literary form? What did the author intend?
3 History Be reasonable What was moving forward — what really happened?
4 Dialectics Be responsible Where do I stand before these oppositions?

Pivot

Conversion — religious, moral, intellectual, psychic.

Phase 2 — handing on the tradition (mediated)

# Specialty Task
5 Foundations Objectify one's conversion; choose framework and categories
6 Doctrines Judgements of fact and value affirming the tradition's truths
7 Systematics Coherent understanding of the mysteries, by analogy
8 Communications Transpose into common-sense language for a culture

The eighth feeds the first: what communications produces becomes the historical data of the next generation's research — the Hermeneutic Circle at the scale of a tradition.

Standing criticism: the split between Doctrines and Systematics is often judged artificial and unworkable.

Five Core Principles of Synodality

  1. Sensus fidei fidelium (LG 12) — see Sensus Fidei as Locus Theologicus. Consultation is a theological necessity, not a democratic concession.
  2. The People of God and baptismal dignity — every Christian shares the tria munera (Priest, Prophet, King) through baptism, establishing a fundamental equality of dignity preceding any differentiation of ministry — the primary theological antidote to clericalism.
  3. Communion, Participation, Mission — the triad governing the 2021-2024 Synod. Communion is the vertical relationship with God flowing horizontally into communion among believers; Participation is its structural manifestation; Mission is the outward, evangelising drive that keeps synodality from becoming self-referential.
  4. Omnes, Aliqui, Unus — see Omnes Aliqui Unus. All consulted, some discern, one decides.
  5. Synodality, Collegiality, Primacy as concentric, not competing — synodality is the whole Church's attribute; collegiality (cum et sub Petro) is a restricted manifestation of it in the College of Bishops; primacy is re-dimensioned as a listening office within synodality, not compromised by it.

Four Criteria for Choosing a Theological Method

  1. Starting point — anthropological (begin from human living, then ask what God's message means for that world) or revelatory (begin from Scripture and Tradition, then draw out human implications). The choice determines the trajectory of everything that follows.
  2. Nature of the task — discovery or teaching. Historical recovery and establishing facts require inductive and historical-critical methods; pedagogical presentation of doctrine requires systematic and deductive method.
  3. The life situation — issues often choose the theologian: a bioethical case, a parish, a social crisis. Fit the method to the actual powers and questions at hand.
  4. Epistemic humility vs. systematic exigency — does this task call for restraint before a mystery that cannot be grasped, or for the drive to synthesise coherently for a modern audience?

On combining: methods may and must be combined when sequenced by task — Lonergan's pipeline runs empirical-inductive work into constructive-pastoral work; Yoder mixes duties, consequences and virtues as a debate requires. Combination is illegitimate when used to dodge a difficulty or smuggle in a conclusion.

Indigenous Indian Models of Synodality

Tribal and regional governance structures reaching decisions by consensus rather than majority vote -- independently arriving at the method of the Council of Jerusalem (Acts 15), for the same underlying reason: a majority vote leaves a defeated minority inside the community.

  1. Khasi Durbar (Meghalaya) — multi-tiered: the Durbar Kur (clan council, led by the Rangbahkur) and the Durbar Shnong (village council, led by the Rangbah-shnong).
  2. Tangkhul Naga — the village headman (Awunga) is restrained by a council of councillors (the Hangva); customary law leaves "no room for the Awunga to be despotic."
  3. Kuki chieftainship — nominally monarchic, but the chief cannot act unilaterally; bound by customary law enforced through a participatory village council.
  4. Munda, Ho, Santhal, Oraon village assemblies — decisions reached by patient dialogue until consensus, never by majority vote.

Tribal parish data: the Mahila Sangh (women's council) functions in 74.4% of tribal parishes — more widespread than the parish council itself (69.8%).

Caution. Not an unqualified model: the Khasi Durbar and Tangkhul Hangva are structurally restricted to male clan heads. Indigenous synodality needs its own internal conversion on the point that matters most for gender.

Four unresolved tensions, each requiring genuine engagement rather than resolution by assertion.

Obstacles to Synodality in the Indian Church

Seven distinct obstacle categories, not one undifferentiated "resistance to change."

  1. Caste — upper-caste clergy dominance, casteism carried into seminaries and religious formation, Dalit clergy sometimes adopting the dominant mindset once ordained rather than remaining in solidarity.
  2. Clericalism — priest-centred parishes, resistance to real lay authority (not merely lay labour), the gap between consultative sessions and actual decision-making.
  3. Women religious and gender — exclusion from decision-making structures despite constituting the active majority of parish life; patriarchal control extending into religious congregations themselves.
  4. Tribal-specific pressures — land alienation and displacement, the collision between tribal customary law and canon law, Latin-rite liturgical uniformity suppressing genuine inculturation, anti-conversion legislation and Hindutva surveillance restricting open assembly.
  5. Structural/canonical obstacles — see The Consultative Vote (Canon Law); councils that exist on paper but never meet, or are populated by appointees chosen to agree.
  6. Formation — seminary formation not equipping priests for synodal, collaborative leadership; see Susairaj.
  7. Self-critique of synodality itself — the charge that the process is rhetoric without structural change, captured by an educated elite, and framed in Eurocentric theological vocabulary that does not reach the illiterate or the Adivasi poor it claims to serve.

The Scholastic Fracture: Aquinas to Luther on the Fate of the Bread

One question, five answers, tracing how each fix in the chain opened the next crack.

  1. Aquinas — substance converted entirely into Christ's body; accidents survive, held in existence directly by God (bypassing the usual role of substance), because an accident's nature to inhere does not require it to be actually inhering. Borrowed from Avicenna.
  2. Duns Scotus — substance converted, as for Aquinas; but accidents must now be granted a kind of independent existence of their own, since dependent existence cannot spontaneously become independent. Begins treating accidents as quasi-substances.
  3. Ockham — substance converted, as for Aquinas; accidents survive by brute divine power alone, with no explanatory scaffold (rejects Aquinas's surviving dimensive quantity as an unnecessary multiplication of miracles).
  4. Buridan — substance converted, as for Aquinas; but since the surviving accidents are visibly, tangibly real, "being" must be a univocal term covering both substances and free-standing accidents — which dismantles the Aristotelian category system the whole account depends on.
  5. Luther — nothing is converted; the substance of bread remains, and Christ's body and blood are present together with it ("in, with, and under") — Sacramental Union. Cheaper: avoids Accidens Sine Subjecto entirely. Costs more elsewhere: no account of why the words of institution require metaphysical change at all.

Claims

  • Aquinas's Avicennean redefinition of accident as that whose nature is to inhere, not that which must inhere, is what makes accidents without a subject non-contradictory
  • Scotus's objection exposes that dependent existence cannot spontaneously become independent existence without redefining what an accident is
  • Ockham rejects Aquinas's surviving dimensive quantity as an unnecessary multiplication of miracles beyond what parsimony allows
  • Buridan's argument that separated accidents are genuine beings forces being to become a univocal term across substance and accident
  • Luther's sacramental union is metaphysically cheaper than transubstantiation because it removes the need to explain accidents without a subject

Three Mediations of Liberation Theology

Clodovis Boff's formal account of how the method actually operates.

  1. Socio-analytic mediation — name the situation with the social sciences: who is poor, by what structures, in whose interest. Not illustration but analysis; this is where Marxist tools entered and where the standard criticism lands.
  2. Hermeneutic mediation — read scripture and tradition from that situation. Segundo's hermeneutic circle: the situation raises a suspicion about the received reading, which sends you back to the text, which changes the situation, which raises a new suspicion.
  3. Practical mediation — act, at the level of structures as well as persons, then re-enter the circle. Theology is a second act, following commitment.

Pastoral form: see–judge–act, in Base Ecclesial Communities. Governing conviction: the preferential option for the poor is first a statement about God's gratuitous love, not a strategy.
